Fact sheet

Myasthenia Gravis

An autoimmune condition where antibodies block the signal from nerves to muscles. Muscles tire with use and recover with rest, so droopy eyelids, double vision or weakness get worse as the day goes on.

Quick answer: Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune condition that blocks signals from nerves to muscles, causing weakness that worsens with use and improves with rest. Droopy eyelids and double vision are often first. It’s diagnosed with antibody blood tests and nerve studies. Pyridostigmine, steroids, other immune treatments and sometimes thymus surgery control it for most people.

How common. About 15 to 25 people in 100,000, or roughly 1 in 5,000. It has two peaks: women in their 20s and 30s, and men over 60. It isn’t inherited and isn’t contagious.

Causes. Antibodies attack the receptor that receives the nerve’s chemical signal (acetylcholine) at the junction with the muscle. About 85 in 100 people with widespread weakness have acetylcholine receptor (AChR) antibodies; a smaller group has MuSK or LRP4 antibodies, and some have none detectable. The thymus gland is often involved, and about 1 in 10 people has a thymus tumour (thymoma).

Symptoms. Weakness that fluctuates, worsens with use or later in the day, and improves with rest. Droopy eyelids (ptosis) and double vision are the first sign in more than half. Others: slurred or nasal speech, trouble chewing or swallowing, a weak neck, and tired arms and legs. Sensation and pain aren’t affected. In about half of people who start with eye-only symptoms, weakness spreads within 2 years.

Triggers. Infection, surgery, stress, heat, pregnancy and some medicines can make it worse, including some antibiotics (aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones and macrolides), magnesium given by drip, beta-blockers and botulinum toxin. Always tell doctors and pharmacists you have MG.

How it’s diagnosed. From the pattern of weakness on examination plus antibody blood tests (AChR, then MuSK if negative). Nerve studies (repetitive nerve stimulation or single-fibre EMG) help when antibodies are negative. A simple ice pack test over a drooping eyelid can help at the bedside. A CT or MRI of the chest looks for a thymoma. Thyroid tests are checked too, as thyroid disease is commoner with MG.

Treatment

Most people get good control. Treatment is guided by a neurologist, following the international consensus guidance (2020 update).

Living with it

  • Plan demanding tasks for when you’re strongest, often the morning, and rest between.
  • Carry a list of your medicines and an MG alert card or bracelet.
  • Check new medicines with your pharmacist or neurologist first.
  • Keep vaccinations up to date (ask which are safe with your treatment) and treat infections early.
  • Plan pregnancy with your neurologist; babies may have brief weakness after birth.

See your GP if

  • Your eyelid droops or you see double, especially later in the day
  • You’re weak in a way that gets worse with use and better with rest
  • Your speech becomes slurred or nasal after talking for a while, or chewing tires you
  • You have MG and symptoms are slowly getting worse

Get urgent help for breathlessness, trouble breathing when lying flat, a weak cough, or choking or being unable to swallow saliva. This can be a myasthenic crisis, which can stop breathing: call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number.

Outlook. With modern treatment, most people with MG live a normal life span and many have few symptoms. Up to about 1 in 5 has a myasthenic crisis at some point, usually early on or with an infection, which is treatable in hospital. Common questions

What are the first signs of myasthenia gravis?

Usually a drooping eyelid or double vision that’s worse when tired or later in the day. Some people first notice slurred speech, trouble chewing or swallowing, or weak arms.

Is myasthenia gravis serious?

It can be, as weakness of breathing and swallowing muscles is dangerous. But with treatment most people do well and live a normal life span.

What medications should be avoided with myasthenia gravis?

Some antibiotics (aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin, and macrolides such as azithromycin), magnesium by drip, beta-blockers and botulinum toxin can worsen it. Always check new medicines with your doctor or pharmacist.

Is myasthenia gravis hereditary?

No, it’s an autoimmune condition, not inherited. A rare group of conditions called congenital myasthenic syndromes is genetic, but that’s different.

Can myasthenia gravis go into remission?

Yes. Many people reach minimal or no symptoms with treatment, and some can reduce or stop medicines over time, especially after thymectomy.

What blood test is used for myasthenia gravis?

Acetylcholine receptor (AChR) antibodies first, then MuSK and sometimes LRP4 antibodies if negative. About 1 in 10 people has none detectable.

Is myasthenia gravis a neurological disease?

Yes. It affects the junction between nerves and muscles and is managed by neurologists.
