Interstitial lung disease: IPF, progressive fibrosis and the rest of the family

For health professionals Respiratory Primary care

More than 200 diseases that scar or inflame the lung between the air sacs, from the slow, stubborn idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis to a stonemason's silicosis and a pigeon keeper's cough. The GP's job is to hear the crackles early; the big shift of the last decade is that fibrosis can now be slowed, and the newest drugs are adding to that.

At a glance

  • A family, not one disease: idiopathic interstitial pneumonias (IPF the commonest), connective tissue disease ILD, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, sarcoidosis, occupational and drug-induced ILD.
  • Clue in the chest: exertional breathlessness, a dry cough and fine basal "Velcro" crackles. A normal chest X-ray doesn't rule it out; HRCT does the work.
  • Diagnosis is a team sport: HRCT pattern plus history, serology and exposures, agreed at a multidisciplinary meeting. A typical UIP pattern in the right setting needs no biopsy.
  • Antifibrotics halve the fall in lung function: nintedanib about 110 mL a year less FVC loss in IPF (INPULSIS) and about 107 mL in other progressive fibrosis (INBUILD). Both nintedanib and pirfenidone are PBS-listed.
  • New this year: nerandomilast (FDA-approved for IPF and progressive pulmonary fibrosis, under TGA evaluation) and inhaled treprostinil (TETON) both slowed FVC decline in phase 3.

Biggest take-home: Velcro crackles in someone over 60 with exertional breathlessness earn an HRCT, not a puffer.

For patients: Pulmonary fibrosis, Sarcoidosis and COPD. Related deep dives: Mould, Antimicrobial resistance and Type 2 diabetes.

Definition

  • ILD: a group of diffuse parenchymal lung diseases in which inflammation, fibrosis or both affect the interstitium, the tissue around the alveoli and small airways.
  • Idiopathic interstitial pneumonias (ATS/ERS 2013): major types are idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), idiopathic non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), respiratory bronchiolitis ILD, desquamative interstitial pneumonia, cryptogenic organising pneumonia and acute interstitial pneumonia; rare types are lymphoid interstitial pneumonia and pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis (Travis 2013).
  • IPF: a chronic, progressive fibrosing ILD of unknown cause, mostly in older adults, with a usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern on HRCT or biopsy (Raghu 2022).
  • Progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF): any fibrosing ILD other than IPF with at least 2 of 3 within a year, with no other explanation: worse symptoms; physiological decline (absolute FVC fall of 5% predicted or more, or DLCO fall of 10% predicted or more); radiological progression (Raghu 2022).
  • Interstitial lung abnormalities (ILA): incidental CT changes in people not suspected of ILD; found in about 4–9% of smokers and 2–7% of non-smokers, and linked with higher mortality (Hatabu 2020).

History

  • 1932, Cumbria: J.M. Campbell described farmer's lung in men forking mouldy hay, the first well-described hypersensitivity pneumonitis.
  • 1935–1944, Baltimore: Louis Hamman and Arnold Rich reported rapidly fatal diffuse fibrosis; "Hamman–Rich syndrome" is now called acute interstitial pneumonia.
  • 1964: John Scadding coined "fibrosing alveolitis"; the UK term "cryptogenic fibrosing alveolitis" lasted into the 2000s.
  • 1969: Averill Liebow and Charles Carrington split the interstitial pneumonias into UIP, DIP, BIP, LIP and GIP, the root of today's classification.
  • 2000–2002: ATS/ERS consensus statements defined IPF by the UIP pattern and set out the idiopathic interstitial pneumonias.
  • 2012: PANTHER-IPF showed the standard of the day (prednisone, azathioprine and N-acetylcysteine) increased deaths, ending immunosuppression for IPF (Raghu 2012).
  • 15 October 2014: the FDA approved pirfenidone and nintedanib on the same day; pirfenidone had first been approved in Japan in 2008.
  • 2018–2024, Australia: a surge of accelerated silicosis in engineered stone workers led to the world's first national ban on engineered stone from 1 July 2024.

Pathophysiology

IPF is now seen as a disease of failed repair in an ageing lung, not of inflammation.

  • Repeated epithelial injury: microinjuries to alveolar type II cells (smoking, reflux, dusts, viruses) in a susceptible lung.
  • Susceptible lung: the MUC5B promoter variant raised IPF risk about 7-fold in carriers of one copy and about 21-fold with two (Seibold 2011); short telomeres and telomerase gene variants (TERT, TERC) cluster in familial disease.
  • Aberrant repair: injured epithelium signals (TGF-β and others) to fibroblasts, which become myofibroblasts and lay down collagen in "fibroblastic foci".
  • Stiff matrix, more scar: stiffer tissue itself switches on fibroblasts, a feed-forward loop that keeps going after the trigger stops.
  • Inflammatory ILDs differ: hypersensitivity pneumonitis, CTD-ILD and organising pneumonia are driven by immune responses, which is why steroids and immunosuppression help them but harm IPF.
  • Honeycombing: end-stage cystic spaces lined by bronchiolar epithelium, mostly subpleural and basal in UIP.

Epidemiology

  • IPF incidence: 0.09–1.30 per 10,000 a year across 12 countries, highest in Asia-Pacific (0.35–1.30); prevalence 0.33–4.51 per 10,000 (Maher 2021). No Australian figures made that review.
  • Who gets IPF: mostly over 60, more men than women, most with a smoking history; familial in a minority.
  • CTD-ILD: common in systemic sclerosis, where it is a leading cause of death, and seen in rheumatoid arthritis, myositis (especially anti-synthetase syndrome) and Sjögren's.
  • Progressive fibrosis outside IPF: a substantial minority of non-IPF fibrosing ILD (HP, CTD-ILD, unclassifiable) progresses despite treatment, the group studied in INBUILD.
  • Australian silicosis: of 544 stone benchtop workers screened in Victoria, 117 of the 414 who had CT (28%) had silicosis (Hoy 2023).

Causes and risk factors

  • IPF: age, male sex, smoking, MUC5B and telomere variants, family history, occupational dusts (metal, wood) and possibly reflux.
  • Hypersensitivity pneumonitis: inhaled antigens: birds and feathers, mould (including humidifiers and water damage), hot tubs (Mycobacterium avium), farm dusts. The antigen is often never found (Raghu 2020).
  • Connective tissue disease: systemic sclerosis, RA, myositis, Sjögren's, mixed connective tissue disease; ILD can come first.
  • Occupational: silica (engineered stone, mining, tunnelling), asbestos, coal dust, beryllium.
  • Drugs: amiodarone, nitrofurantoin, methotrexate, bleomycin, immune checkpoint inhibitors and others (pneumotox.com lists hundreds).
  • Radiation: pneumonitis after chest radiotherapy, sometimes progressing to fibrosis.

Preventable factors

  • Silica: Australia banned engineered stone from 1 July 2024; wet cutting, extraction and respirators still matter for natural stone and other silica work.
  • Smoking: a risk factor for IPF, and the cause of respiratory bronchiolitis ILD, DIP and Langerhans cell histiocytosis.
  • Antigen avoidance in HP: removing the bird, the mouldy humidifier or the hot tub is the single most important step; continued exposure predicts worse outcomes (Raghu 2020).
  • Drug vigilance: new breathlessness on amiodarone, nitrofurantoin or methotrexate warrants stopping the drug and investigating.
  • Vaccination: influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19 and RSV vaccines, since infections can trigger acute exacerbations.

Diagnosis essentials

  • Listen: bilateral fine end-inspiratory basal crackles; finger clubbing in a minority. Don't accept "chest infection" or "asthma" for crackles that don't clear.
  • First tests: spirometry (restrictive, though can be normal early), DLCO (often the first to fall), oxygen saturations at rest and on walking.
  • HRCT, not chest X-ray: thin-section, non-contrast, prone and expiratory views. Patterns are reported as UIP, probable UIP, indeterminate or alternative diagnosis (Raghu 2022).
  • Look for a cause: exposure history (birds, mould, occupation, drugs); CTD serology (ANA, ENA, RF, anti-CCP, myositis panel, CK); examine skin, joints and hands for Raynaud's and mechanic's hands.
  • Multidisciplinary discussion: respiratory physician, radiologist and, where tissue is considered, pathologist; required for PBS antifibrotic access.
  • Tissue only if needed: UIP on HRCT in the right clinical setting is enough for IPF; otherwise bronchoalveolar lavage, transbronchial cryobiopsy or surgical lung biopsy (Raghu 2022).
  • Stage it: the GAP index (gender, age, FVC, DLCO) gave 1-year mortality of about 6%, 16% and 39% for stages I–III in IPF (Ley 2012).

Current treatment

Treat the type: antifibrotics slow scarring; immunosuppression helps inflammatory ILD and harms IPF.

Antifibrotics in IPF

  • Nintedanib (INPULSIS 1 and 2, n=1,066): annual FVC decline 114.7 vs 239.9 mL and 113.6 vs 207.3 mL; diarrhoea in about 6 in 10 (Richeldi 2014).
  • Pirfenidone (ASCEND, n=555): cut the proportion with an FVC fall of 10% or more or death by 47.9% at 52 weeks; nausea, rash and photosensitivity (King 2014).
  • Australian status: both PBS-listed (Authority) for IPF; diagnosis by a multidisciplinary team and treatment by or with a respiratory or specialist physician.

Progressive pulmonary fibrosis and CTD-ILD

  • Nintedanib (INBUILD, n=663): FVC decline 80.8 vs 187.8 mL a year across progressive fibrosing ILDs, regardless of HRCT pattern (Flaherty 2019). PBS-listed for progressive fibrosing ILD since 1 May 2022.
  • Systemic sclerosis ILD: nintedanib slowed decline (52.4 vs 93.3 mL a year; SENSCIS, Distler 2019); mycophenolate matched cyclophosphamide for FVC with better tolerability (SLS II, Tashkin 2016); tocilizumab is FDA-approved for SSc-ILD (2021).
  • HP and inflammatory ILD: antigen avoidance first, then prednisolone and steroid-sparing agents (mycophenolate, azathioprine) by specialist plan (Raghu 2020).

Everyone with ILD

  • Pulmonary rehabilitation: improves walking distance (about 40 m on 6MWT) and quality of life (Dowman 2021).
  • Oxygen: ambulatory oxygen improved quality of life in fibrotic ILD with exertional desaturation (AmbOx, Visca 2018).
  • Cough: low-dose slow-release morphine cut daytime cough by 39.4% vs placebo in IPF over 2 weeks (PACIFY, Wu 2024).
  • Don't: prednisone plus azathioprine plus NAC in IPF (more deaths, PANTHER); co-trimoxazole or doxycycline for IPF outcomes (no benefit: EME-TIPAC, CleanUP-IPF).
  • Transplant and palliative care: refer early to a transplant centre if eligible; plan breathlessness and end-of-life care alongside active treatment.

Emerging treatments and trials

  • Nerandomilast (PDE4B inhibitor): FIBRONEER-IPF (n=1,177): FVC decline 114.7 mL on 18 mg vs 183.5 mL placebo; diarrhoea 41% vs 16% (Richeldi 2025). FIBRONEER-ILD: 98.6 and 84.6 vs 165.8 mL (Maher 2025). FDA-approved for IPF (October 2025) and PPF (December 2025) as Jascayd; TGA application accepted December 2025.
  • Inhaled treprostinil: TETON-1 (n=598): FVC 130 mL better than placebo at 52 weeks in IPF (NEJM 2026); pooled TETON-1 and 2 halved acute exacerbations (HR 0.52), with no survival difference yet. Already used for ILD-associated pulmonary hypertension (INCREASE, Waxman 2021).
  • Admilparant (LPA1 antagonist): cut the rate of FVC decline by 62% vs placebo in a phase 2 IPF trial led by Sydney's Tamera Corte (Corte, phase 2); phase 3 under way.
  • Setbacks: several promising agents (pamrevlumab, ziritaxestat, bexotegrast) failed or stopped in late-phase trials, a reminder that FVC signals in phase 2 often don't hold.

Theoretical frontiers

Hypotheses, not recommendations.

Causes: theories

  • Hypothesis: IPF is accelerated lung ageing. For: telomere shortening, senescent epithelial cells and onset after 60. Against: most old lungs never scar. Test: senolytic trials with biomarker-defined senescence.
  • Hypothesis: the airway mucus barrier starts it. For: MUC5B is the strongest genetic risk (OR about 7–21; Seibold 2011). Against: most carriers never get IPF, and the variant links to better survival once ill. Test: mucus-targeted interventions in high-risk carriers.
  • Hypothesis: microaspiration drives injury. For: reflux is common in IPF. Against: antacids and fundoplication haven't clearly helped. Test: objective aspiration markers with long-term outcomes.
  • Hypothesis: unrecognised exposures explain some "idiopathic" disease. For: occupational dusts raise risk, and HP antigens are often never found. Against: exposure histories are unreliable. Test: structured exposure tools and environmental sampling in registries.

Prevention: theoretical options

  • Hypothesis: screening relatives finds treatable early disease. For: ILA is common in relatives in familial pulmonary fibrosis. Against: no trial shows early antifibrotics change outcomes in ILA. Test: randomised early-treatment trials in progressive ILA.
  • Hypothesis: lung cancer CT screening doubles as ILD screening. For: ILA in 4–9% of smokers (Hatabu 2020). Against: overdiagnosis and anxiety. Test: pathway studies linking screening ILA to MDT review and outcomes.
  • Hypothesis: the stone ban will end a silicosis wave. For: removal of the highest-silica product. Against: latency means cases will keep appearing for years. Test: national surveillance of new cases by exposure year.

Treatment: theoretical options

  • Hypothesis: combination antifibrotics beat single agents. For: different mechanisms (nintedanib, pirfenidone, nerandomilast). Against: more side effects, and combination data are limited. Test: factorial trials with tolerability end points.
  • Hypothesis: treat by molecular endotype, not pattern. For: INBUILD showed shared progression across ILD types. Against: no validated biomarker panel. Test: biomarker-stratified adaptive trials.
  • Hypothesis: pulmonary vascular therapy slows fibrosis. For: TETON's FVC benefit. Against: mechanism unclear and one programme. Test: replication and mechanistic studies.

Gaps and open questions

  • Australian numbers: no national incidence data for IPF; the Australian IPF Registry captures only part of the picture.
  • Early detection: average delays from symptoms to diagnosis are long, and primary care pathways to HRCT vary.
  • Survival: antifibrotics slow FVC decline, but no single trial was powered to show they prolong life.
  • Access: pulmonary rehab, MDT meetings and transplant are concentrated in cities.
  • Silicosis: no treatment reverses it; whole-lung lavage and antifibrotics are unproven.
  • Equity: little data on ILD in First Nations, rural and culturally diverse communities.

Prognosis and quality of life

  • IPF: median survival historically about 3–5 years from diagnosis, varying widely; GAP staging helps frame the conversation (Ley 2012).
  • Acute exacerbations: sudden worsening without another cause, roughly 1 in 10 people with IPF a year, with high in-hospital mortality (Collard 2016).
  • Other ILDs: NSIP, HP without fibrosis and organising pneumonia often respond to treatment and carry a much better outlook.
  • Symptoms that matter: breathlessness, cough and fatigue drive quality of life more than FVC numbers.

Weird trivia

  • Westies get it too: West Highland white terriers develop a canine idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, complete with crackles.
  • Trombone player's lung: a 2010 case report traced hypersensitivity pneumonitis to mould growing inside a trombone (Metersky 2010).
  • Same-day approval: the FDA approved both IPF antifibrotics on 15 October 2014.
  • Velcro crackles are named for the sound of Velcro being pulled apart, and you can mimic them by rubbing hair between your fingers near your ear.
  • Sunscreen as a side-effect plan: pirfenidone causes photosensitivity, so sun protection is part of the prescription.
  • Honeycomb: radiologists borrowed the beehive for the clustered subpleural cysts of end-stage fibrosis.
