Autism

For health professionals General practice

Autism used to be a diagnosis for young boys. Now the fastest-growing group being diagnosed is adults, many of them women who spent decades masking. Autism is a lifelong difference in how a person communicates and experiences the world, with real strengths, real needs and some very preventable harms. This is what the evidence says across the lifespan.

At a glance

  • Prevalence: 1 in 31 US 8-year-olds in 2022 (CDC ADDM, 2025); 61.8 million people worldwide, 1 in 127 (GBD 2021); 290,900 Australians, 1.1% (ABS 2022).
  • Adults: recorded UK diagnoses rose 787% from 1998 to 2018, fastest in adults and females (Russell 2022). In Australia, prevalence is 3.1% under 25 but 0.3% at 25 and over, a gap that likely reflects under-recognition in older adults.
  • Sex: about 3:1 male to female in high-quality studies, not 4:1. Girls who meet criteria are less likely to be diagnosed (Loomes 2017).
  • Genetics: heritability about 80% (Bai 2019, 2 million people in 5 countries).
  • Vaccines: no link. MMR HR 0.93 in 657,461 Danish children (Hviid 2019). The 1998 Wakefield paper was retracted and called fraud.
  • Health: ADHD in 28% and anxiety in 20% (Lai 2019); premature death OR 2.56 (Hirvikoski 2016).
  • Support: no medicine treats core autism. Supports target participation, wellbeing and co-occurring conditions.

Biggest take-home: in adults, ask about masking and exhaustion before you settle on "just anxiety".

Language: many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic person"), and we use it here. Some prefer person-first ("person with autism"). Ask, and use what the person prefers. For patients: Autism, Autism in adults and Autism in children.

Definition

DSM-5-TR

  • A. Social communication and interaction: persistent differences in all 3: social-emotional reciprocity; non-verbal communication; developing, keeping and understanding relationships.
  • B. Restricted, repetitive patterns: at least 2 of 4: repetitive movements, object use or speech; insistence on sameness or routines; intense, focused interests; sensory hyper- or hyporeactivity.
  • C. Onset: early developmental period, though features may not show until social demands exceed capacity, or may be masked by learned strategies.
  • D–E: clinically significant impairment; not better explained by intellectual disability or global developmental delay.
  • Support levels: 1 (requires support), 2 (substantial), 3 (very substantial), rated separately for each domain. Specifiers: with or without intellectual or language impairment, and associated conditions.

ICD-11

  • One category (6A02) replaces ICD-10's childhood autism, atypical autism and Asperger syndrome.
  • Less prescriptive: no fixed symptom count, examples instead, and no operationalised severity. Notes that compensation can hide features, especially with high intelligence.

History

  • 1910s, Eugen Bleuler: coined "autistic" for withdrawal in schizophrenia.
  • 1925, Grunya Sukhareva (Soviet child psychiatrist): described 6 boys with what we'd now call autism, two decades before Kanner. Sula Wolff brought the paper to English readers in 1996.
  • 1943, Leo Kanner: "Autistic disturbances of affective contact": 11 children, 8 boys and 3 girls.
  • 1944, Hans Asperger: published his thesis on "autistic psychopathy". Herwig Czech's 2018 archival study found he publicly legitimised race hygiene and cooperated with Vienna's child "euthanasia" programme.
  • 1981, Lorna Wing: "Asperger's syndrome: a clinical account" placed it with autism in a wider group: the root of today's spectrum.
  • 1998: Wakefield's Lancet paper (12 children) launched the MMR scare. The same year "neurodiversity" reached print (sociologist Judy Singer, journalist Harvey Blume), growing out of online autistic communities.
  • 2010: the Lancet retracted the paper (February); Wakefield lost his UK licence (May). The BMJ called it "an elaborate fraud" in 2011.
  • 2013–2023: DSM-5 merges the subtypes; Australia publishes a national assessment guideline (2018) and its second edition (2023).

Pathophysiology

A highly heritable neurodevelopmental difference. Mechanisms are partly mapped; there is no single cause.

  • Heritability: median 80.8% across Denmark, Finland, Sweden, Israel and Western Australia; maternal effects 0.4–1.6% (Bai 2019).
  • Common variants: a GWAS of 18,381 autistic people and 27,969 controls found 5 genome-wide loci, implicating neuronal function and corticogenesis (Grove 2019). Each variant has a tiny effect.
  • Environment, proven: prenatal valproate: 4.42% autism vs 1.53% background (adjusted HR 2.9; Christensen 2013).
  • Environment, ruled out: MMR (HR 0.93; Hviid 2019). Prenatal paracetamol: HR 1.05 overall but 0.98 in sibling comparisons (Ahlqvist 2024, 2.48 million children): familial confounding, not causation.
  • Outcome depends on context: the Supporting Autistic Children Guideline frames barriers to participation as starting in the environment, not the child.

Epidemiology

Global

  • GBD 2021: 61.8 million autistic people, age-standardised 788 per 100,000; males 1,065, females 508 per 100,000. Among the top 10 causes of non-fatal health burden under 20.
  • US (ADDM, 2022 data): 1 in 31 8-year-olds, up from 1 in 36 in 2020; boys more than 3 times as likely; 39.6% also had intellectual disability. Children born in 2018 were 1.7 times as likely to be identified by age 4 as those born in 2014.
  • Why the rise: in UK primary care data the biggest increases were in adults, females and people without intellectual disability, and the authors concluded "augmented recognition" most likely explains them (Russell 2022).

Australia

  • ABS 2022: 290,900 autistic people (1.1%), up 41.8% from 2018. Males 1.6%, females 0.7%. 73.0% reported a profound or severe disability.
  • Work: 42.2% of working-age autistic people employed; unemployment 18.2%, more than double that of people with disability overall (7.5%).

Causes and risk factors

  • Family history and genetics: the dominant factor (heritability about 80%).
  • Sex: diagnosed about 3 times more often in males; part of the gap is diagnostic bias (Loomes 2017).
  • Prenatal valproate: about 3-fold risk (Christensen 2013).
  • Not risk factors: vaccines or prenatal paracetamol once family factors are accounted for.

Preventable factors

Autism itself isn't something to prevent. This section is about avoidable harms: drug exposures, missed diagnoses and the secondary health effects of unmet needs.

  • Valproate in pregnancy: avoid where possible, with specialist epilepsy or psychiatric advice and contraception planning.
  • Periconceptional folic acid: recommended for neural tube defects anyway. In Norway, users had autistic disorder 0.10% vs 0.21% (adjusted OR 0.61); the authors note it can't establish causality (Surén 2013).
  • Missed and late diagnosis: especially in girls, women and adults who mask.
  • Secondary harms: untreated anxiety and depression, unemployment and poor access to health care drive much of the excess morbidity and mortality.

Diagnosis essentials

Australian National Guideline (Autism CRC; 2nd edition, 2023)

  • 66 consensus recommendations, NHMRC-approved, for children and adults.
  • Two pathways: a Lead Practitioner Diagnostic Evaluation (renamed from "single clinician" in 2023; one clinician leads, drawing on others' input) when the picture is clear, or a Consensus Team Diagnostic Evaluation when it isn't.
  • Holistic: diagnosis sits alongside an assessment of strengths, needs and functioning. Funding eligibility is a separate question from diagnosis.
  • Language: the update moved to neurodiversity-affirming terminology.

In general practice

  • Children: act on parent concern and regression; refer for developmental assessment; hearing test.
  • Adults: clues include lifelong social exhaustion, sensory overwhelm, rigid routines, burnout after life changes, recurrent "treatment-resistant" anxiety or depression, and an autistic child or relative.
  • Masking: camouflaging combines masking and compensation; motivations are fitting in and connection; consequences include exhaustion and threats to self-perception (Hull 2017, 92 adults). A normal-seeming consultation doesn't exclude autism.
  • Screen for co-occurring conditions: ADHD 28%, anxiety 20%, sleep-wake disorders 13%, depression 11%, OCD 9% (Lai 2019, pooled clinical and population studies).
  • Differentials: social anxiety, ADHD, trauma, personality disorders, psychosis, intellectual disability and language disorders. Several can coexist.

Current treatment

"Treatment" here means support for participation, wellbeing and co-occurring conditions, not changing who someone is.

Children

  • Naturalistic developmental behavioural interventions (NDBI): social communication g=0.36 and autism characteristics g=0.44 on non-caregiver measures. With high-detection-bias studies excluded, only one effect stayed significant (g=0.30). Adverse events were poorly monitored (Project AIM, 252 studies, 13,304 children, 2023).
  • Behavioural interventions (including ABA-based): g=0.58 for challenging behaviour in RCTs, but mostly caregiver-reported; effects did not survive restriction to blinded measures.
  • Parent-mediated (PACT): reduced autism symptom severity across about 6 years of follow-up (effect size 0.55); no language difference (Pickles 2016).

Medicines

  • No medicine treats core autism. Intranasal oxytocin: no difference in social withdrawal (difference −0.2; 290 children; Sikich 2021).
  • Severe irritability: risperidone response 69% vs 12% on placebo; irritability fell 57% vs 14% (RUPP 2002, n=101). Weight gain and appetite are the main costs; review regularly.
  • Co-occurring conditions: treat ADHD, anxiety, depression, sleep problems and epilepsy on their own evidence, starting low with medicines.

Adults

  • Post-diagnosis support, peer connection, workplace and study adjustments, and sensory-friendly care (longer or first appointments, written plans, low-light rooms).
  • Mental health: treat co-occurring conditions; adapt communication in therapy.
  • NDIS, in one line: access is based on functional need; Thriving Kids (from October 2026) supports children 8 and under with low to moderate needs without a diagnosis, and NDIS access changes take effect on 1 January 2028.

Emerging treatments and trials

  • Pre-emptive support in infancy: iBASIS-VIPP in 104 Australian infants (9–14 months) with early signs: 6.7% vs 20.5% met autism criteria at 3 (OR 0.18; NNT 7.2), with better caregiver responsiveness and language (Whitehouse 2021).
  • Leucovorin: after September 2025 claims, the FDA's March 2026 label update covers only cerebral folate deficiency from FOLR1 gene defects (about 1 in a million), not autism. The largest autism RCT was retracted.
  • Paracetamol: the 2025 US claims sit against sibling-controlled data showing no association (Ahlqvist 2024).
  • Policy: Australia's first National Autism Strategy 2025–31 (January 2025) includes a National Roadmap to Improve the Health and Mental Health of Autistic People.

Theoretical frontiers

Hypotheses, not recommendations.

Causes: theories

  • Hypothesis: thousands of common variants add up. For: heritability 80%, 5 GWAS loci (Grove 2019). Against: each locus explains very little; samples mostly European. Test: larger, ancestrally diverse GWAS combined with sequencing.
  • Hypothesis: the sex ratio is partly ascertainment. For: 3.25:1 when populations are screened vs 4.56:1 for existing diagnoses (Loomes 2017). Against: a male excess persists. Test: population screening with instruments validated for camouflaging.
  • Hypothesis: prevalence growth is recognition, not incidence. For: biggest UK rises in adults and females (Russell 2022). Against: a small true rise can't be excluded. Test: repeated population surveys with identical methods.
  • Hypothesis: prenatal folate pathways matter. For: adjusted OR 0.61 (Surén 2013). Against: observational, residual confounding. Test: sibling comparisons and Mendelian randomisation.
  • Hypothesis: prenatal paracetamol is causal. For: small unadjusted associations (HR 1.05). Against: sibling HR 0.98, no dose response (Ahlqvist 2024). Test: triangulation across sibling, negative-control and genetic designs.

Prevention: theoretical options

  • Hypothesis: pre-emptive support improves development. For: iBASIS-VIPP (OR 0.18). Against: 104 infants, one-tailed tests; "fewer diagnoses" is not an outcome autistic people prioritise. Test: a larger multisite RCT with quality-of-life and wellbeing outcomes.
  • Hypothesis: avoiding valproate cuts preventable cases. For: adjusted HR 2.9. Against: seizure control matters too. Test: registry monitoring after prescribing restrictions.
  • Hypothesis: earlier diagnosis in girls prevents mental ill-health. For: camouflaging brings exhaustion (Hull 2017). Against: qualitative evidence. Test: a cohort comparing early vs late diagnosis on mental health.
  • Hypothesis: employment support prevents secondary harm. For: 18.2% unemployment (ABS 2022). Against: no Australian RCT found. Test: an RCT of supported employment for autistic adults.

Treatment: theoretical options

  • Hypothesis: NDBIs change long-term outcomes. For: blinded social communication effects (Project AIM). Against: one effect survives strict bias limits. Test: active-comparator RCTs with outcomes chosen with autistic people.
  • Hypothesis: leucovorin helps a folate-antibody subgroup. For: small studies cited by the FDA. Against: the largest RCT was retracted. Test: a new multisite placebo-controlled RCT.
  • Hypothesis: oxytocin improves social function. For: early small trials. Against: SOARS-B null (Sikich 2021). Test: none justified without new mechanism data.
  • Hypothesis: reducing camouflaging demands improves wellbeing. For: Hull 2017. Against: no intervention trial. Test: a workplace-adjustment RCT with burnout outcomes.
  • Hypothesis: better-tolerated options for severe irritability exist. For: risperidone works but causes weight gain. Against: few head-to-head trials. Test: comparative effectiveness trials with metabolic outcomes.

Gaps and open questions

  • Adult services: recorded diagnoses are rising fastest in adults (Russell 2022). How should adult assessment and post-diagnosis support be organised?
  • Ageing: we found few data on autistic people in later life.
  • Mortality: why is premature death more than doubled, and what prevents it?
  • Equity: we found few data on First Nations autistic Australians or culturally diverse communities.
  • Intervention harms: adverse events are rarely monitored in trials (Project AIM).

Prognosis and quality of life

  • Mortality: 2.60% of autistic people died during follow-up vs 0.91% of matched controls (OR 2.56), across almost all causes (Hirvikoski 2016, Sweden, 27,122 people).
  • Work: 18.2% unemployment in Australia (ABS 2022).
  • Lifespan: autism is lifelong; skills, support needs and wellbeing change with environment, support and co-occurring conditions.
  • Early support: PACT symptom benefits persisted about 6 years after the trial.

Weird trivia

  • Sukhareva first: a Soviet woman psychiatrist described autism in 1925, 18 years before Kanner.
  • Bleuler's word: "autistic" first described schizophrenia.
  • Kanner's 11 included 3 girls.
  • Temple Grandin's hug machine was inspired by cattle calming in a squeeze chute on her aunt's ranch.
  • Autistic Pride Day (18 June, since 2005) was set on the birthday of the founding group's youngest member.
  • "Neurodiversity" is often credited to one person, but a 2024 Autism letter argues nobody knows who coined it.
