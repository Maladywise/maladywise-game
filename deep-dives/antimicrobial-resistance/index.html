Antimicrobial resistance in primary care

For health professionals Infectious diseases Primary care

Bacteria have been beating antibiotics for at least 30,000 years; we just gave them a training camp. More than a third of Australians have an antibiotic dispensed each year, so the front line of resistance is often a 15-minute consult about a cough. The good news: the interventions that work are cheap, mostly behavioural, and measurable in your own prescribing data.

At a glance

  • Big and growing: 1.27 million deaths attributable to bacterial AMR in 2019, 4.95 million associated (GRAM, Murray 2022); forecast 1.91 million attributable a year by 2050 (Naghavi 2024).
  • Common now: about 1 in 6 lab-confirmed bacterial infections worldwide were resistant in 2023, and resistance rose in over 40% of drug–bug pairs from 2018 (WHO GLASS 2025).
  • Australia: 37.1% of Australians had at least one PBS antimicrobial dispensed in 2024; GPs still prescribe for about 80% of acute bronchitis (Sixth AURA report 2026; ACSQHC).
  • Your patient, your resistance: after a primary care antibiotic for UTI, odds of a resistant urinary isolate are 2.5 within 2 months (Costelloe 2010).
  • What works: delayed scripts (antibiotic use 31% vs 93%), shared decision-making (RR 0.61), CRP point-of-care testing (RR 0.77) and shorter courses, without worse recovery.

Biggest take-home: the most powerful anti-resistance drug in general practice is a well-explained "not today", backed by a safety net and a delayed script.

For patients: Antibiotics, UTI, Sore throat, Ear infection, Sinusitis and Common cold.

Definition

  • Antimicrobial resistance (AMR): microbes (bacteria, fungi, viruses, parasites) no longer respond to medicines that used to kill or inhibit them. This page focuses on bacteria.
  • Attributable vs associated deaths: attributable = deaths that would not have happened if the infection were susceptible; associated = deaths in which a resistant infection played any part (GRAM).
  • Key organisms in the community: ESBL-producing E. coli and Klebsiella, MRSA, resistant N. gonorrhoeae and, rarer but alarming, carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE).
  • WHO critical priority pathogens (2024): carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, third-generation cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacterales, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales and rifampicin-resistant TB (15 entries in all).
  • Antimicrobial stewardship (AMS): coordinated actions to use antimicrobials only when needed, with the right drug, dose and duration (ACSQHC Clinical Care Standard 2020, which explicitly covers general practice and community pharmacy).

History

  • 1940, Oxford: Edward Abraham and Ernst Chain described a bacterial enzyme that destroys penicillin (Nature), before penicillin was used in patients.
  • 1941, Oxford: Adelaide-born Howard Florey's team treated Constable Albert Alexander; penicillin was recovered from his urine to reuse, but supplies ran out and he died.
  • 1945: Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin solved penicillin's structure by X-ray crystallography, confirming the beta-lactam ring. In his Nobel lecture, Alexander Fleming warned that under-dosing could make microbes resistant.
  • 1947, London: Mary Barber documented hospital staphylococcal infection due to penicillin-resistant strains (BMJ).
  • Late 1950s, Japan: Japanese workers showed multidrug resistance could pass between Shigella and E. coli on transferable "R factors", reviewed by Tsutomu Watanabe in 1963.
  • 1961: M.P. Jevons reported methicillin-resistant staphylococci in the BMJ, about 2 years after methicillin arrived.
  • 2009–2015: NDM-1 carbapenemase described in a Swedish patient treated in New Delhi (Yong 2009); Liu Yi-Yun, Shen Jianzhong and colleagues found plasmid colistin resistance (mcr-1) in Chinese pigs (2015).
  • 2015–2024: WHO Global Action Plan (2015); UN General Assembly declaration (2024) to cut AMR-associated deaths by 10% by 2030 and make 70% of human antibiotic use WHO Access drugs.

Pathophysiology

Resistance is evolution in fast-forward: antibiotics don't create resistance genes, they select for them and help them spread.

  • Ancient genes: beta-lactam, tetracycline and glycopeptide resistance genes, including intact vanA, were found in 30,000-year-old permafrost DNA (D'Costa 2011).
  • Four main survival tricks: drug-destroying enzymes (beta-lactamases, ESBLs, carbapenemases), altered targets (PBP2a in MRSA), efflux pumps and reduced entry through porins (Darby 2023).
  • Mutation vs acquisition: point mutations (e.g. fluoroquinolone targets) arise in an individual; acquired genes travel on plasmids and transposons between species, as with NDM-1 and mcr-1.
  • Selection below the MIC: antibiotic concentrations up to several hundred-fold below the MIC can still enrich resistant bacteria (Gullberg 2011), which is why gut, skin and environmental exposures matter.
  • Collateral damage: antibiotics reshape the gut microbiome; community C. difficile risk OR 20.4 after clindamycin, 5.7 fluoroquinolones, 4.5 cephalosporins, 3.3 penicillins (Deshpande 2013).
  • Resistance is sticky: an 85% cut in trimethoprim use over 24 months in Kronoberg, Sweden, had only a marginal effect on E. coli resistance (Sundqvist 2010).

Epidemiology

  • Global deaths: 1.14 million attributable and 4.71 million associated in 2021; since 1990, AMR deaths fell over 50% in under-5s and rose over 80% in people 70 and over (Naghavi 2024).
  • Six pathogens (E. coli, S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, S. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa) caused 929,000 attributable deaths in 2019 (Murray 2022).
  • Geography: about 1 in 3 infections resistant in South-East Asia and the Eastern Mediterranean; over 40% of E. coli and over 55% of K. pneumoniae resist third-generation cephalosporins worldwide (WHO 2025). Australasia had the lowest attributable death rate, 6.5 per 100,000 (Murray 2022).
  • Australian use: 23.2 million PBS antibiotic prescriptions in 2024, up 4.8%; critical resistance reports up 25.2% (Sixth AURA report). Community use 16.8 DDD/1,000/day in 2023: mid-range in Europe, nearly double the Netherlands (ACSQHC 2025).
  • Australian GP prescribing (2024): acute bronchitis 80.0%, acute otitis media 80.3%, sinusitis 73.8%, URTI 38.6% of patients (ACSQHC, MedicineInsight). Earlier estimate: 4–9 times guideline-recommended rates for respiratory infections (McCullough 2017).
  • Australian resistance: E. coli third-generation cephalosporin resistance 8.5% and bloodstream ciprofloxacin resistance 15.4% in 2024; trimethoprim resistance 27% in Melbourne GP cystitis isolates (Curtis 2024).
  • CPE in Australia: 1,708 CARAlert reports in 2025; NDM (40.9%) has overtaken the historically dominant IMP type (35.2%). Ceftriaxone-nonsusceptible gonorrhoea: 49 reports in 2025 (CARAlert 2025).
  • Aged care: on survey day 11.9% of residents were on an antimicrobial, 34.7% of scripts were over 6 months old, and only 3.6% of residents had signs of suspected infection (Aged Care NAPS 2023).

Causes and risk factors

  • Recent antibiotics: urinary isolate resistance OR 2.5 at 2 months and 1.33 at 12 months; respiratory OR 2.4; larger with longer or repeated courses (Costelloe 2010).
  • Travel: 34.3% of Dutch travellers acquired ESBL-producing Enterobacterales, 75.1% after southern Asia; 11.3% still carried them at 12 months and 7.7% of household members acquired them (COMBAT, Arcilla 2017).
  • Healthcare and aged care: asymptomatic bacteriuria in 25–50% of women and 15–40% of men in long-term care drives needless treatment (Nicolle 2015).
  • Animals and food: antimicrobial use in animals is about three times that in humans (Van Boeckel 2019); mcr-1 was in 21% of pigs tested and 15% of raw meat in the original Chinese survey (Liu 2016).
  • Sanitation and poverty: sewage resistance genes from 60 countries tracked sanitation and health more than antibiotic use (Hendriksen 2019).
  • Penicillin allergy labels: push prescribers to broader or second-line drugs; about 10% of people carry a label, yet fewer than 5% have clinically significant hypersensitivity (Shenoy 2019).

Preventable factors

  • Self-limiting infections: most bronchitis, sinusitis and URTIs; the gap between actual and guideline prescribing is the largest modifiable driver in Australian general practice.
  • Duration: courses longer than needed, and repeats nobody needs. The PBS dropped default repeats for common antibiotics from 1 April 2020.
  • Treating colonisation: screen and treat asymptomatic bacteriuria only in pregnancy and before invasive urological procedures (IDSA 2019).
  • Vaccination: after PCV7, invasive disease from penicillin-nonsusceptible pneumococci fell 57% (81% in under-2s) (Kyaw 2006). WHO estimates vaccines could cut antibiotic use by 22%, 2.5 billion doses a year (2024).
  • Hand hygiene: community interventions cut gastrointestinal illness 31% and respiratory illness 21% (Aiello 2008), fewer infections to tempt a script.
  • Wrong allergy labels: delabelling is cheap and mostly safe in low-risk patients (see Diagnosis essentials).

Diagnosis essentials

  • Ask two questions: antibiotics in the last 12 months, and travel or hospital care overseas. Both raise the odds of a resistant isolate (Costelloe 2010; Arcilla 2017).
  • Culture before treating when it changes management: cystitis in men, pregnancy, pyelonephritis, recurrence, failed first-line treatment, aged care residents, recent travel. Don't send urine from asymptomatic adults.
  • Read the report: ESBL means third-generation cephalosporins fail; check nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin results for cystitis. CPE or critical results trigger infection control; CPE is notifiable in some states and nominated for national notification, expected from 1 January 2027 subject to legislation.
  • CRP point-of-care: cut prescribing from 516 to 397 per 1,000 (RR 0.77; 12 trials, n=10,218) with no difference in recovery at 7 days (Smedemark 2022).
  • Penicillin allergy: PEN-FAST score under 3 has a 96.3% negative predictive value (Trubiano 2020). PALACE: direct oral challenge was non-inferior to skin testing in low-risk adults, 0.5% vs 0.5% reactions (n=382; Copaescu 2023).
  • Gonorrhoea: ceftriaxone-nonsusceptible reports ran 37 (2022), 23, 39 and 49 (2025) (CARAlert). NAAT gives no susceptibilities, so culture matters.

Current treatment

"Treatment" for AMR in primary care is mostly prescribing well. Effect sizes are for prescribing outcomes unless stated.

Interventions that cut prescribing

  • Delayed prescription: antibiotic use 31% vs 93% with immediate scripts (OR 0.04), complications no different, satisfaction 86% vs 91% (NS); immediate antibiotics modestly helped AOM and sore throat symptoms (Spurling 2017).
  • Shared decision-making: prescribing 47% to 29% (RR 0.61) with no rise in re-consultation (RR 0.87) (Coxeter 2015).
  • Peer-comparison letters: England's Chief Medical Officer letter cut items 3.3% (about 73,406 fewer) in 6 months (Hallsworth 2016). Australia's 2017 "Nudge vs Superbugs" letters to 6,649 high prescribers cut scripts 9.3–12.3% (about 126,352 fewer) vs 3.2% for education alone (BETA 2018).
  • PBS no-repeats policy (2020): appropriate supply rose (originals OR 1.75), though COVID-19 confounds total volume (Contreras 2023).

Knowing the benefit you're buying

  • Acute otitis media: no effect on pain at 24 h; NNTB 20 for pain at 2–3 days vs NNTH 14 for adverse effects. NNTB 4 under 2 years with bilateral AOM, 3 with otorrhoea (Venekamp 2015).
  • Sore throat: NNTB under 6 at day 3 but 18 at 1 week; 82% of untreated people are symptom-free by 1 week (Spinks 2021).

Shorter courses

  • Adult CAP (inpatients, PTC): stopping at day 3 in stable patients was non-inferior to 8 days, cure 77% vs 68% (Dinh 2021).
  • Children's CAP (CAP-IT): 3 days of amoxicillin non-inferior to 7, re-treatment 12.5% in both; lower dose also non-inferior (Bielicki 2021).
  • Cystitis in women: 3 days vs 5–10 days, symptomatic failure RR 1.06 with fewer adverse effects (Milo 2005).
  • Afebrile UTI in men: 7 days non-inferior to 14, symptom resolution 93.1% vs 90.2% (Drekonja 2021).

Resistant and recurrent infections

  • Australian guidance: the 2025 Therapeutic Guidelines Antibiotic update made nitrofurantoin first line for cystitis instead of trimethoprim.
  • ESBL cystitis: nitrofurantoin or oral fosfomycin trometamol 3 g (TGA-registered, not PBS-listed) when susceptible; ID advice for upper tract ESBL infection.
  • Recurrent UTI without antibiotics: methenamine hippurate was non-inferior to daily antibiotic prophylaxis: 1.38 vs 0.89 episodes per person-year, within the margin (ALTAR, Harding 2022).
  • Gonorrhoea: ceftriaxone 500 mg IM plus azithromycin 1 g (2 g for pharyngeal infection) remains the Australian regimen.

Emerging treatments and trials

  • Pipeline: 90 antibacterials in clinical development in 2025, down from 97 in 2023; only 15 innovative and 5 active against a critical pathogen (WHO 2025).
  • Gepotidacin (new class, oral): superior to nitrofurantoin for uncomplicated UTI in EAGLE-3, success 58.5% vs 43.6% (Wagenlehner 2024). FDA-approved for uUTI (March 2025) and gonorrhoea (December 2025); under TGA evaluation.
  • Zoliflodacin (single 3 g oral dose for gonorrhoea): non-inferior to ceftriaxone plus azithromycin, cure 90.9% vs 96.2% (n=930; Lancet 2026); FDA-approved December 2025.
  • Hospital drugs: sulbactam–durlobactam non-inferior to colistin for Acinetobacter, 28-day mortality 19% vs 32% (ATTACK, Kaye 2023); cefepime–taniborbactam superior to meropenem for complicated UTI, 70.6% vs 58.0% (CERTAIN-1, 2024), not yet FDA-approved.
  • Zosurabalpin: the first new class against A. baumannii in over 50 years, blocking lipopolysaccharide transport (Zampaloni 2024); entering phase 3.
  • MV140 sublingual bacterial vaccine: UTI-free 56–58% vs 25% with placebo (Lorenzo-Gómez 2022); not registered in Australia.
  • Phage therapy: Australia's STAMP study (Westmead, under SAS) is collecting safety data; no phage RCT has yet shown efficacy. A CRISPR-Cas3-armed phage cocktail (LBP-EC01) cut E. coli fast in a 39-person UTI trial phase (Kim 2024); the randomised part is enrolling.
  • Paying for antibiotics differently: England's subscription model (£100m a year, contracts from 2026); the US PASTEUR Act is still not law; Australia commissioned a scoping study but has no pull incentive yet.

Theoretical frontiers

Hypotheses, not recommendations.

Causes: theories

  • Hypothesis: low-level environmental antibiotics drive resistance as much as clinical doses. For: selection at several hundred-fold below the MIC (Gullberg 2011). Against: mostly lab data. Test: field studies linking wastewater antibiotic concentrations to local carriage.
  • Hypothesis: sanitation, not prescribing, sets the global baseline. For: sewage resistomes track sanitation and health more than use (Hendriksen 2019). Against: ecological, cross-sectional data. Test: before–after resistome monitoring around sanitation projects.
  • Hypothesis: resistance genes carry little fitness cost, so they won't fade. For: Kronoberg's 85% cut in trimethoprim had only a marginal effect (Sundqvist 2010). Against: costs vary by gene and host. Test: longer national de-escalation programmes with genomic tracking.
  • Hypothesis: food animals seed human resistance. For: mcr-1 in pigs and meat before patients (Liu 2016); animal use about 3 times human use. Against: direction of flow is hard to prove. Test: One Health genomic surveillance of shared plasmids.
  • Hypothesis: travel is the main route for new genes into Australia. For: 75% ESBL acquisition after southern Asia, 12% per-traveller household transmission (Arcilla 2017); NDM overtaking IMP in CARAlert 2025. Against: local spread also grows. Test: genomic linkage of CPE cases to travel history.

Prevention: theoretical options

  • Hypothesis: broader vaccination is a stewardship tool. For: 57% fall in resistant pneumococcal disease after PCV7 (Kyaw 2006); WHO 22% antibiotic-use estimate. Against: the ExPEC9V E. coli vaccine phase 3 stopped for lack of efficacy (J&J 2025). Test: Klebsiella and TB vaccine trials with resistance end points.
  • Hypothesis: gut decolonisation prevents resistant infection. For: uncontrolled FMT series reported 37.5–87.5% eradication (Saha 2019). Against: a sham-controlled RCT (n=114) found 31.0% vs 30.4% (Narang 2026). Test: targeted defined-consortium trials.
  • Hypothesis: universal penicillin delabelling cuts resistance. For: labels push broad-spectrum use; direct oral challenge is safe in low risk (PALACE). Against: no resistance outcome data. Test: cluster RCT of GP-led delabelling with resistance and C. difficile outcomes.
  • Hypothesis: travellers' ESBL carriage can be shortened. For: 11.3% still colonised at 12 months. Against: no safe, proven decolonisation. Test: probiotic or phage RCTs in returned travellers.

Treatment: theoretical options

  • Hypothesis: collateral sensitivity cycling traps bacteria. For: cycling gentamicin and cefuroxime selected against resistance to either in E. coli (Imamovic and Sommer 2013). Against: in vitro only. Test: pharmacodynamic models then pilot cycling in recurrent UTI.
  • Hypothesis: AI finds new chemical classes faster. For: halicin (Stokes 2020), narrow-spectrum abaucin against A. baumannii (Liu 2023), generative designs active in mouse gonorrhoea and MRSA models (Krishnan 2025). Against: none in humans yet. Test: phase 1 of an AI-discovered lead.
  • Hypothesis: phages or CRISPR-armed phages replace antibiotics in UTI. For: LBP-EC01 early data (Kim 2024). Against: PhagoBurn's cocktail cut bacterial load more slowly than silver sulfadiazine (Jault 2019). Test: ELIMINATE part 2.
  • Hypothesis: antibodies can prevent S. aureus pneumonia. For: suvratoxumab 18% vs 26% (Francois 2021). Against: missed significance (p=0.17); tosatoxumab missed its phase 3 end point. Test: SAATELLITE-2.
  • Hypothesis: soil "dark matter" holds resistance-proof drugs. For: teixobactin, no resistant mutants obtained (Ling 2015). Against: never reached patients. Test: clinical development of lipid II binders.

Gaps and open questions

  • Community resistance data: AURA gives no national community urine susceptibility figure, so GPs treat cystitis on hospital-weighted data.
  • Notification: CPE is not yet nationally notifiable.
  • Outcomes: stewardship trials measure prescriptions, rarely resistance or deaths.
  • Short courses in primary care: much of the duration evidence is from hospital inpatients.
  • Economics: the pipeline is thin because new antibiotics are meant to be used rarely; Australia has no pull incentive.
  • Equity: national summaries say little about resistance in remote Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, and the highest burden is in low- and middle-income countries with the least data.

Prognosis and quality of life

  • Forecast: 1.91 million attributable and 8.22 million associated deaths a year by 2050, about 39 million attributable deaths in 2025–2050 (Naghavi 2024; Wellcome).
  • The "10 million by 2050" figure (O'Neill 2014) was a projection on limited data and has been criticised (de Kraker 2016).
  • Older people carry the burden: AMR deaths in people 70 and over rose over 80% from 1990 to 2021.
  • In practice: resistant UTI and gonorrhoea mean fewer oral options, IV treatment, more admissions and repeat visits.

Weird trivia

  • Recycled cure: in 1941, Oxford researchers extracted penicillin from Albert Alexander's urine to give it again.
  • Mouldy Mary: the high-yield penicillin strain (NRRL 1951, now Penicillium rubens) came from a mouldy cantaloupe in Peoria, about 1943; the find is attributed to Mary Hunt, but USDA calls it an enduring mystery.
  • Cash for Florey: Howard Florey was on Australia's $50 note from 1973 until polymer notes replaced it in 1995.
  • Ancient resistance: resistance genes in 30,000-year-old permafrost (D'Costa 2011), and cave bacteria isolated for over 4 million years in Lechuguilla Cave resisted up to 14 antibiotics (Bhullar 2012).
  • Ant farmers: leafcutter ants carry antibiotic-making bacteria on their bodies to protect their fungus gardens from a parasitic fungus (Currie 1999).
  • Enzyme before drug: penicillinase was described in 1940, a year before penicillin's first patient.
