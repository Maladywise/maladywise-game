Deep dive #15 · 27 November 2026

Disorders of gut–brain interaction: IBS, functional dyspepsia and the spectrum

For health professionals Gastroenterology Primary care

"Functional" used to be code for "we found nothing, off you go". The biology has since caught up: nerves that fire at lower balloon volumes, mast cells hugging sensory fibres, infections that leave a gut permanently twitchy. About 4 in 10 adults meet criteria for at least one of these disorders, and the treatments that work have numbers attached.

At a glance

  • Very common: 40.3% of adults in a 33-country internet survey met Rome IV criteria for at least one DGBI (20.7% in household interviews); Rome V gives a near-identical 40.9% (Sperber 2021; 2026).
  • Real biology: visceral hypersensitivity, altered motility, mucosal immune activation, microbiome and bile acid changes, and central pain processing, talking in both directions.
  • After gastro: about 1 in 10 people develop IBS within a year of infectious enteritis (RR 4.2), and 42% after protozoal infection (Klem 2017).
  • Positive diagnosis: symptom criteria plus a few targeted tests (FBC, CRP, coeliac serology, faecal calprotectin) beat a colonoscopy-for-everyone approach.
  • What works: low-FODMAP diet (RR 0.67), soluble fibre (NNT 7), peppermint oil (NNT 3), low-dose amitriptyline (ATLANTIS), CBT and gut-directed hypnotherapy (RR 0.61–0.67).

Biggest take-home: make the diagnosis out loud, explain the mechanism, and start treatment on day one. "Your tests are normal" is not a diagnosis.

For patients: IBS, Functional dyspepsia and SIBO. Related deep dives: Coeliac disease, POTS, Hypermobility and ME/CFS.

Definition

  • DGBI: chronic gastrointestinal symptoms from any combination of motility disturbance, visceral hypersensitivity, altered mucosal and immune function, altered gut microbiota and altered central nervous system processing (Rome V, Drossman 2026).
  • The reframe: Rome IV (2016) renamed "functional gastrointestinal disorders" as disorders of gut–brain interaction; Rome V (May 2026) went further in cutting imprecise or stigmatising terms (Drossman and Hasler 2016; Drossman 2026).
  • IBS (Rome IV): recurrent abdominal pain at least 1 day a week in the last 3 months, with 2 or more of: related to defecation, change in stool frequency, change in stool form; onset at least 6 months before diagnosis. Subtyped by Bristol stool form into IBS-C, IBS-D, IBS-M and unclassified.
  • IBS (Rome V): allows abdominal pain or discomfort with altered bowel habit, bringing back "discomfort" that Rome IV dropped (Chang 2026).
  • Functional dyspepsia (Rome IV): bothersome postprandial fullness, early satiation, epigastric pain or burning, with no structural cause. Postprandial distress syndrome (meal-related, at least 3 days a week) and epigastric pain syndrome (at least 1 day a week) can overlap.
  • The rest of the spectrum: functional constipation and diarrhoea, bloating and distension disorder, centrally mediated abdominal pain, cyclic vomiting and cannabinoid hyperemesis, belching disorders and rumination syndrome. Rome V adds abdominal migraine and inability to belch syndrome to the adult list (Sperber 2026).

History

  • 1962, Oxford: Chaudhary and Truelove described the "irritable colon syndrome" in 130 patients; in 26% it began after dysentery, and those cases resolved more often (63% vs 29%).
  • 1973: Ritchie's balloon study: inflating to 60 mL in the sigmoid colon hurt 55% of people with IBS vs 6% of controls, the first hard evidence of visceral hypersensitivity.
  • 1978: Manning, Thompson, Heaton and Morris identified 4 symptoms that discriminated IBS from organic disease in 109 patients: the first positive criteria.
  • 1984, Manchester: Peter Whorwell's trial of gut-directed hypnotherapy in 30 people with refractory IBS: no relapses in the hypnotherapy group at 3 months.
  • 1988–1994, Rome: Aldo Torsoli set up working teams at the 1988 Rome congress; W. Grant Thompson's IBS team reported in 1989, and the Rome I book followed in 1994.
  • 1996: Kok-Ann Gwee, later of Singapore, and colleagues in Sheffield showed psychological traits predicted who kept IBS symptoms after gastroenteritis (22 of 75 developed them).
  • 2004–2014, Australia: Allen and colleagues described cannabinoid hyperemesis in South Australia; at Monash, Sue Shepherd and Peter Gibson proposed FODMAPs (2005) and Emma Halmos showed the diet halved symptoms in a blinded crossover (2014).
  • 2016–2026: Rome IV coined "disorders of gut–brain interaction"; the Rome V criteria followed in May 2026, with Douglas Drossman, Lin Chang and Jan Tack summarising the process.

Pathophysiology

Not one mechanism but a loop: gut signals change the brain, and the brain changes how the gut feels and moves.

  • Visceral hypersensitivity: pain at lower distension volumes (Ritchie 1973), with central amplification and weaker descending inhibition.
  • Post-infection change: pooled IBS prevalence 10.1% at 12 months after enteritis, RR 4.2; risk higher with female sex (OR 2.2), anxiety (2.0), antibiotics (1.7) and severe illness (Klem 2017). After the Walkerton water outbreak, 36.2% vs 10.1% of controls had IBS at 2 years (Marshall 2006).
  • Mast cells near nerves: in IBS, mast cells within 5 µm of colonic nerves were tripled and correlated with pain severity (Barbara 2004).
  • Food-specific gut IgE: in mice, infection broke oral tolerance and later food exposure caused IgE- and mast-cell-dependent pain via histamine H1 receptors; food antigens injected into IBS rectal mucosa caused local oedema and mast cell activation (Aguilera-Lizarraga 2021).
  • Duodenal eosinophils in FD: in a Swedish population endoscopy study, high duodenal bulb eosinophil counts carried an OR of 11.7 for dyspepsia, linked to early satiety (Talley 2007).
  • Bile acids: 28.1% of people labelled IBS-D have bile acid diarrhoea on SeHCAT (under 10% retention) (Slattery 2015).
  • Brain to gut and gut to brain: in a 12-year Australian cohort, higher baseline anxiety predicted new DGBI, and DGBI at baseline predicted new anxiety and depression (Koloski 2012).

Epidemiology

  • Global (Rome IV): 73,076 adults in 33 countries; IBS 4.1% (internet) and 1.5% (household), functional dyspepsia 7.2% and 4.8%, functional constipation 11.7% and 6.6%. Women OR 1.7 (Sperber 2021).
  • Global (Rome V): 28,771 adults in 15 countries; any DGBI 40.9%, functional dyspepsia 8.1%, IBS 8.5%, chronic constipation 8.9%, abdominal migraine 5.1% (Sperber 2026).
  • Criteria change the count: IBS was 10.1% under Rome III vs 4.1% under Rome IV in the same internet sample; older pooled estimates were 11.2% (Lovell and Ford 2012).
  • Sex: women vs men OR 1.67 for IBS (Lovell and Ford 2012).
  • Overlap is the rule: 31.7% of people with a DGBI had symptoms in 2 or more gut regions, and severity rose with each region (IBS-SSS 208 with one, 292 with four) (Sperber 2022).
  • Beyond the gut: a median 49% of people with fibromyalgia have IBS (Whitehead 2002); joint hypermobility syndrome was found in 39% of GI clinic patients with a DGBI vs 27.5% with organic disease (Fikree 2015); 37.5% of people with HSD/hEDS in an online survey reported diagnosed POTS, with more Rome IV symptoms (Tai 2020).

Causes and risk factors

  • Gastroenteritis: the clearest identifiable trigger; protozoal infections carry the highest risk (41.9% vs 13.8% after bacterial) (Klem 2017).
  • Female sex: higher prevalence across studies; female sex also predicts IBS after enteritis (OR 2.2).
  • Psychological factors: anxiety (OR 2.0), somatisation (4.1) and neuroticism (3.3) predicted post-infection IBS (Klem 2017); anxiety predicted new DGBI over 12 years in Australia (Koloski 2012). These are risk factors, not explanations of the symptoms.
  • Antibiotics during enteritis: OR 1.7 for post-infection IBS (Klem 2017).
  • Cannabis: prolonged heavy use causes cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (Allen 2004).
  • Mimics to keep in mind: coeliac disease is about 4.5 times more common in people with IBS-type symptoms (biopsy-proven 3.3%) (Irvine 2017); bile acid diarrhoea in 28% of IBS-D.

Preventable factors

  • Gastroenteritis: safe water and food prevent the main known trigger; Walkerton showed how one contaminated supply produced a wave of IBS.
  • Antibiotics: avoid unnecessary antibiotics in self-limiting gastro, given the post-infection IBS association (observational).
  • Cannabis: sustained cessation is diagnostic and curative in cannabinoid hyperemesis (Rome IV).
  • Opioids: 18% of people with IBS in a large US survey were taking opioids (Drossman 2009); opioids worsen constipation and can drive centrally mediated pain.
  • Iatrogenic harm: repeated normal tests without a named diagnosis feed anxiety and cost; a confident positive diagnosis is itself a treatment step (BSG 2021).

Diagnosis essentials

  • Make a positive diagnosis: Rome criteria plus history and examination, not a diagnosis of exclusion (BSG 2021; ACG 2021).
  • Ask about red flags: age over 50 with new symptoms, weight loss, rectal bleeding, iron deficiency anaemia, nocturnal symptoms, family history of colorectal cancer, IBD or coeliac disease. Any one changes the work-up.
  • Baseline tests for IBS: FBC, CRP and coeliac serology; faecal calprotectin when diarrhoea predominates. CRP 0.5 mg/dL or less, or calprotectin 40 µg/g or less, means a 1% or lower probability of IBD (Menees 2015).
  • Bile acid diarrhoea: SeHCAT under 10% predicted 80% response to colestyramine (Wedlake 2009); where SeHCAT isn't available, an empirical bile acid sequestrant trial is common.
  • Functional dyspepsia: test and treat H. pylori; endoscopy from age 60, or earlier with alarm features (ACG/CAG 2017).
  • Breath tests with care: SIBO is "positive" with a hydrogen rise of 20 ppm or more within 90 minutes (Rezaie 2017), but with lactulose an early rise can reflect fast transit rather than overgrowth.
  • Rome V accuracy: in 726 Leeds clinic patients, Rome V sensitivity was 66.1% and specificity 80.1%, vs 78.9% and 81.0% for Rome IV (Staller 2026).
  • Cyclic vomiting (Rome IV): stereotyped episodes lasting under 1 week, at least 3 in the past year and 2 in the past 6 months, at least 1 week apart, well between episodes.

Current treatment

Start with explanation, then pick by subtype and the most bothersome symptom. RR below is for symptoms persisting unless stated.

IBS: first line

  • Soluble fibre (psyllium): RR 0.83, NNT 7; bran did not help (Moayyedi 2014).
  • Low-FODMAP diet, with a dietitian: RR 0.67 vs habitual diet, ranked first for every end point in a network meta-analysis (Black 2022). Monash crossover: symptom score 22.8 vs 44.9 mm on a typical Australian diet (Halmos 2014). Reintroduce after 2–6 weeks.
  • Peppermint oil: global improvement RR 2.39, NNT 3 (Alammar 2019); the larger PERSUADE trial missed its primary end point (46.8% vs 34.4% pain response, p=0.17) (Weerts 2020).
  • Antispasmodics: RR 0.68; hyoscine RR 0.63 (Ford 2008).

IBS: gut–brain neuromodulators and psychology

  • Antidepressants overall: RR 0.66 (Ford 2019); tricyclics ranked first for abdominal pain (RR 0.53) (Black 2020).
  • ATLANTIS (primary care, n=463): amitriptyline 10–30 mg improved IBS-SSS by 27 points more than placebo over 6 months; 13% vs 9% stopped for adverse effects (Ford 2023).
  • CBT: RR 0.61 self-guided, 0.62 face to face; gut-directed hypnotherapy RR 0.67 (Black 2020, high risk of bias).
  • ACTIB: telephone CBT cut IBS-SSS by 61.6 points and web CBT by 35.2 vs usual care at 12 months (Everitt 2019).
  • Hypnotherapy vs diet: equal at Monash, 72% vs 71% responders; hypnotherapy also helped anxiety and depression (Peters 2016).

IBS: subtype drugs and Australian status

  • Linaclotide (IBS-C): FDA responder 33.7% vs 13.9%, NNT 5 (Chey 2012). TGA-registered (Constella), not PBS-listed.
  • Prucalopride (chronic constipation): TGA-registered after 2 laxative classes fail; not PBS-listed.
  • Rifaximin (IBS-D): adequate relief 40.7% vs 31.7%, NNT about 11 (TARGET 1 and 2; Pimentel 2011). PBS-listed only for hepatic encephalopathy.
  • Eluxadoline: FDA-approved, but the Australian application was withdrawn in 2017; not on the ARTG.
  • Loperamide and bile acid sequestrants: cheap options for IBS-D and confirmed or suspected bile acid diarrhoea.

Functional dyspepsia

  • H. pylori eradication: NNT 14 for cure (Ford 2022).
  • PPIs: RR 0.88, NNT 11 (Pinto-Sanchez 2017).
  • Tricyclics: adequate relief 53% amitriptyline vs 40% placebo; escitalopram 38% (no benefit); OR 3.1 in painful (ulcer-like) FD (Talley 2015).
  • Prokinetics: RR 0.81, NNT 7, very low-quality evidence (Pittayanon 2018).
  • Mirtazapine: improved early satiation, weight and quality of life in FD with weight loss (n=34) (Tack 2016).
  • Buspirone: improved fundic accommodation (229 vs 141 mL) and fullness in a small crossover (n=17) (Tack 2012).

Emerging treatments and trials

  • Faecal microbiota transplant: 76.9–89.1% vs 23.6% response with a single "super-donor" (El-Salhy 2020), but a 7-trial meta-analysis found no overall benefit (RR 0.75, 0.43–1.31; capsules worse than placebo) (Wu 2022).
  • Antihistamines: ebastine 20 mg gave symptom relief in 46% vs 13% in a phase 2 trial (Wouters 2016); phase 3 data are needed.
  • Ondansetron (IBS-D): the UK TRITON trial recruited 80 of a planned 400 and missed its primary end point (40.5% vs 27.9%); pooled with earlier trials, NNT 9 (Gunn 2023).
  • Linaclotide for children: FDA-approved for functional constipation aged 6–17 (2023) and for IBS-C from age 7 (November 2025).
  • Digital therapeutics: a 378-person RCT of an app-based CBT programme missed its primary end point (30.4% vs 27.1%) (Berry 2023), so app evidence lags face-to-face CBT.
  • Rome V in practice: new criteria will reshape trial entry; whether a broader, milder IBS group changes effect sizes is untested.

Theoretical frontiers

Hypotheses, not recommendations.

Causes: theories

  • Hypothesis: some IBS is a local food allergy. For: infection-induced, gut-confined food IgE causes mast cell pain in mice; food injection caused mucosal reactions in IBS (Aguilera-Lizarraga 2021). Against: the full chain is unproven in humans and serum IgE tests are useless here. Test: blinded mucosal challenge with targeted elimination in a randomised trial.
  • Hypothesis: post-infection IBS is immune memory. For: RR 4.2 in year one, fading to 2.3 later; 42% after protozoa (Klem 2017). Against: psychological traits predict persistence too (Gwee 1996). Test: prospective cohorts after outbreaks with mucosal immune profiling.
  • Hypothesis: FD is duodenal micro-inflammation. For: duodenal eosinophilia OR 11.7 (Talley 2007). Against: not all FD has eosinophilia and causality is unclear. Test: eosinophil-depleting therapy trials stratified by duodenal counts.
  • Hypothesis: much IBS-D is undiagnosed bile acid diarrhoea. For: 28–32% with low SeHCAT (Slattery 2015; Wedlake 2009). Against: SeHCAT access is patchy and responses vary. Test: routine C4 or SeHCAT in IBS-D with sequestrant randomisation.
  • Hypothesis: the gut can start it. For: DGBI at baseline predicted new anxiety in 12 years (Koloski 2012). Against: shared genetic and early-life factors could explain both. Test: Mendelian randomisation and longer cohorts.

Prevention: theoretical options

  • Hypothesis: early psychological support after gastro prevents IBS. For: anxiety (OR 2.0) and somatisation (OR 4.1) predict post-infection IBS. Against: no prevention trial exists. Test: RCT of brief CBT after culture-proven enteritis.
  • Hypothesis: withholding antibiotics in mild gastro lowers later IBS. For: antibiotics OR 1.7 (Klem 2017). Against: confounded by severity. Test: pragmatic trials with 12-month IBS outcomes.
  • Hypothesis: preventing enteritis prevents IBS at scale. For: Walkerton 36% vs 10%. Against: hard to measure without a natural experiment. Test: IBS incidence before and after water or food safety upgrades.
  • Hypothesis: avoiding new foods during gastro limits food sensitisation. For: mouse data link infection with tolerance loss to food eaten at the time. Against: purely preclinical. Test: diet-timing studies in outbreak cohorts.

Treatment: theoretical options

  • Hypothesis: H1 antihistamines treat mast-cell-driven pain. For: ebastine 46% vs 13% (Wouters 2016). Against: one small phase 2 trial. Test: phase 3 trial stratified by mucosal mast cell markers.
  • Hypothesis: donor selection makes FMT work. For: single super-donor results (El-Salhy 2020). Against: pooled RR 0.75 with high heterogeneity (Wu 2022). Test: donor-matched, defined-consortium RCTs.
  • Hypothesis: neuromodulators belong in first line. For: ATLANTIS showed benefit in primary care with modest adverse effects. Against: placebo response is high and effect modest. Test: head-to-head early TCA vs diet trials.
  • Hypothesis: phenotype-led therapy beats symptom-led. For: subgroups (bile acid diarrhoea, eosinophilia, hypersensitivity) respond differently. Against: no validated biomarker panel. Test: biomarker-stratified adaptive trials.
  • Hypothesis: scalable hypnotherapy equals diet. For: 72% vs 71% at Monash (Peters 2016). Against: app trials have disappointed (Berry 2023). Test: non-inferiority trial of digital hypnotherapy vs dietitian-led low FODMAP.

Gaps and open questions

  • Biomarkers: no validated test confirms a DGBI or predicts treatment response.
  • Australian data: few local prevalence studies under Rome IV or Rome V, and no national DGBI care standard.
  • Access: the drugs with the best IBS-C and IBS-D data are not PBS-listed for IBS, and psychological gut therapies and dietitians are scarce outside cities.
  • Criteria churn: Rome III, IV and V pick different patients, so trial results don't transfer cleanly.
  • Diet long term: microbiome and nutritional effects of years on restricted diets are unclear.
  • Equity: data in First Nations, rural and low-income populations are thin.

Prognosis and quality of life

  • Burden: to be symptom-free, people with IBS said they would give up 25% of their remaining life (about 15 years), and 14% would accept a 1 in 1,000 risk of death; they averaged 73 days of restricted activity a year (Drossman 2009).
  • Post-infection IBS often improves: RR falls from 4.2 within a year to 2.3 later (Klem 2017); post-dysentery cases resolved more often in 1962 too.
  • Overlap predicts severity: each extra gut region adds to symptom burden (Sperber 2022).
  • Healthcare use: DGBI were associated with lower quality of life and more doctor visits across 33 countries (Sperber 2021).

Weird trivia

  • Balloon test: 60 mL of air in the sigmoid colon was enough to hurt 55% of people with IBS in 1973, and only 6% of controls.
  • Hot showers: 9 of the first 10 cannabinoid hyperemesis patients described, from South Australia, bathed compulsively in hot water during attacks (Allen 2004).
  • FODMAP's first target was Crohn's: the 2005 hypothesis from Box Hill was about Crohn's disease susceptibility, not IBS (Gibson and Shepherd 2005).
  • Second brain: the gut has 400–600 million enteric neurons (Furness 2012); Michael Gershon's book The Second Brain appeared in 1998.
  • The Bristol scale was validated in 66 volunteers: stool form tracked whole-gut transit time (r = −0.54) (Lewis and Heaton 1997).
  • Why "Rome": the criteria are named after the 1988 Rome congress where the working teams began, not a Roman physician.
