Coeliac disease

For health professionals

About 1 in 100 people have it and most don't know. The diet is the only treatment, about 4 in 10 adults still have villous atrophy on it, and the drug pipeline is a graveyard with one or two survivors. The numbers, the trials, and the gaps.

At a glance

  • Common: seroprevalence 1.4% and biopsy-confirmed prevalence 0.7% worldwide (Singh 2018). Incidence is rising about 7.5% a year.
  • Mostly missed: about 1 in 70 Australians, with an estimated 80% undiagnosed (Coeliac Australia; an advocacy estimate, not a measured figure).
  • Test first, then go gluten free: tTG-IgA is about 91% sensitive and 87% specific in adults. It is useless on a gluten-free diet.
  • Healing is slow: 34% of adults had mucosal recovery at 2 years and 66% at 5 years on a GFD. 43% had persistent villous atrophy on follow-up biopsy in a Swedish cohort.
  • Mortality: all-cause HR 1.21 vs matched controls (Lebwohl, JAMA 2020), highest in the first year after diagnosis and in 18–39-year-olds.
  • No approved drug. Larazotide, Nexvax2, latiglutenase and two Takeda assets have fallen over. A TG2 inhibitor (TAK-227/ZED1227) is the main survivor.

Biggest take-home: test anyone with unexplained iron deficiency, type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease or a first-degree relative with coeliac disease, and do it before they cut out gluten.

Definition

  • Oslo 2013: a chronic small intestinal immune-mediated enteropathy precipitated by exposure to dietary gluten in genetically predisposed individuals.
  • ACG 2023: a permanent immune-mediated response to gluten in wheat, barley and rye; a multisystem disorder, not just a gut disease.
  • Dermatitis herpetiformis is its skin form (about 13% of patients).
  • Not the same as: wheat allergy (IgE-mediated) or non-coeliac gluten/wheat sensitivity (symptoms without serology or enteropathy).

History

  • 2nd century AD, Aretaeus of Cappadocia (Anatolia): described a chronic malabsorptive diarrhoea. "Coeliac" comes from his Greek koiliakos, "abdominal", via Francis Adams's 1856 translation for the Sydenham Society.
  • 1887–1888, Samuel Gee: lectured at Great Ormond Street, then published "On the coeliac affection" (1888): "if the patient can be cured at all, it must be by means of diet." He picked the wrong diet, banning rice, fruit and vegetables. Christian Herter's 1908 "intestinal infantilism" gave the old eponym Gee–Herter disease.
  • 1941–1953, Willem-Karel Dicke (Netherlands): published a wheat-free diet in 1941 after a decade of observation. The 1944–45 Hunger Winter, when flour was scarce, backed him up. His 1950 Utrecht thesis and 1953 work with van de Kamer and Weijers pinned the harm on gluten's gliadin fraction. "The famine gave him the idea" is a tidy myth.
  • 1954–1957, the lesion: John Paulley described the jejunal changes in four laparotomy specimens (BMJ 1954). Margot Shiner, a Berlin-born paediatric gastroenterologist working in London, published a peroral small-bowel biopsy tube in 1956. The Crosby capsule followed in 1957, and biopsy became the diagnostic standard.
  • 1989, the genes: Ludvig Sollid's Oslo group found 93 of 94 Norwegian children with coeliac disease (98.9%) carried the alleles for one HLA-DQ α/β heterodimer (DQ2), vs 25% of controls.
  • 1997, the autoantigen: Walburga Dieterich, Detlef Schuppan and colleagues in Berlin identified tissue transglutaminase as the endomysial antigen (Nat Med 1997). That gave us tTG-IgA serology.
  • 2002, the 33-mer: Shan, Khosla, Sollid and colleagues (Science) showed an α-gliadin 33-mer resists every gastric, pancreatic and brush-border protease and stimulated gut T cells from all 14 patients tested. This launched the enzyme-therapy field.

Pathophysiology

Genetics

  • About 90–95% of patients carry HLA-DQ2.5; most of the rest carry DQ8 or DQ2.2. Under 1% carry neither (ESsCD 2025).
  • The genes are necessary but not sufficient: 30–40% of Western populations carry DQ2/DQ8, and 40–50% of Australians.
  • Risk is up to 30% with homozygous DQ2.5 vs about 3% with other risk genotypes (ESsCD 2025; likely at-risk cohorts).
  • HLA explains about 40% of genetic variance; non-HLA loci about 15%.
  • Negative HLA typing makes coeliac disease very unlikely (under 1% per AJGP). Positive typing means little.

Immune cascade

  • Gliadin peptides resist digestion. The 33-mer (α-gliadin p55–87) is the classic immunodominant fragment.
  • Tissue transglutaminase 2 (TG2) deamidates glutamine to glutamate, increasing peptide binding to HLA-DQ2/DQ8.
  • Gluten-specific CD4⁺ T cells drive the response, and B cells make anti-TG2 and anti-DGP antibodies (the serology targets).
  • IL-15 licenses cytotoxic CD8⁺ intraepithelial lymphocytes (via NKG2D) to kill enterocytes, causing villous atrophy and crypt hyperplasia.
  • Histology is graded on the modified Marsh (Marsh–Oberhuber) scale.

Infant feeding: timing doesn't prevent it

  • PreventCD (n=944): small gluten doses at 16–24 weeks gave coeliac disease by age 3 in 5.9% vs 4.5% (HR 1.23, 0.79–1.91). No prevention.
  • CELIPREV (n=832): introducing gluten at 12 vs 6 months delayed disease (2 years: 5% vs 12%) but didn't prevent it (5 years: 16% vs 16%).
  • TEDDY (observational, n=6,605 at-risk children): each extra 1 g/day of gluten in early childhood gave HR 1.50. Amount may matter more than timing.

Epidemiology

Global

  • Prevalence (Singh 2018): seroprevalence 1.4%, biopsy-confirmed 0.7%. Europe and Oceania 0.8%, Asia 0.6%, North America and Africa 0.5%, South America 0.4%.
  • Females 0.6% vs males 0.4%; children 0.9% vs adults 0.5%. Diagnosed cases run about 1.5–2 female to 1 male, but screening suggests true occurrence is closer to equal.
  • Incidence (King 2020): women 17.4 and men 7.8 per 100,000 person-years; children 21.3 vs adults 12.9. Rising about 7.5% a year.
  • Hotspots: Saharawi refugee children 5.6%, the highest reported. Northern India 1.23% seropositive vs southern India 0.10%, with similar HLA carriage, so wheat intake explains the gap.
  • Lower rates: Japan about 1 in 20,000. No reliable sub-Saharan African data.

Australia

  • Serogenetic study (Anderson 2013): about 1.3% of adults, versus 0.5–0.7% biopsy-confirmed.
  • Coeliac Australia and newsGP: about 1 in 70, with about 80% undiagnosed.
  • No AIHW national prevalence figures found.

Causes and risk factors

  • Necessary: HLA-DQ2/DQ8 plus gluten.
  • Higher-risk groups (who to test):
    • First-degree relatives: 5–10% lifetime risk, up to 20% with several affected relatives.
    • Type 1 diabetes: 6.0% biopsy-confirmed.
    • Down syndrome 5.8%, Turner syndrome 4.8%, Williams syndrome about 9.5% (one small series).
    • Selective IgA deficiency: about 6% have coeliac disease (BSG 2026).
    • Autoimmune thyroid disease: 1.6%.
    • Iron deficiency anaemia: 3.2% (5.5% in high-quality studies).
    • IBS-type symptoms: OR 4.48 for biopsy-proven coeliac disease.
    • Osteoporosis: 1.6%. Whether to screen is contested.
  • Hypothesised environmental triggers: early-life gluten amount (TEDDY) and gut infections. None is proven causal.

Preventable factors

  • No intervention has prevented coeliac disease in a trial. Infant gluten timing (PreventCD, CELIPREV) failed.
  • No population attributable fractions exist.
  • What is preventable is the harm of late diagnosis: anaemia, osteoporosis and fracture (OR 1.43), adverse pregnancy outcomes, and years of symptoms.

Diagnosis essentials

  • Must be eating gluten. Gluten challenge if already gluten free:
    • Coeliac Australia: about 10 g a day (about 4 slices of bread; 5 g in children) for at least 6 weeks.
    • BSG 2026: 3–6 g a day for at least 6 weeks, or about 10 g a day if only 2 weeks.
    • ESsCD 2025: at least 3 g a day for 6 weeks. Check HLA first: negative typing can avoid the challenge.
  • Serology (Sheppard 2022):
    • tTG-IgA: adults sensitivity 90.7%, specificity 87.4%. Children 97.7% and 70.2%.
    • EMA: adults 88.0% and 99.6%.
    • Always check total IgA (2–3% of patients are IgA deficient) or add DGP-IgG.
    • Australia: tTG-IgA plus DGP-IgG or total IgA (MBS 71164). Expect 10–15% false negatives.
    • Isolated positive DGP predicts coeliac disease in only about 15%.
  • Biopsy: at least 4 from the distal duodenum plus 1–2 from the bulb. Bulb biopsies add 6.9% yield.
  • No-biopsy pathways:
    • Children (ESPGHAN 2020): tTG-IgA at least 10× upper limit of normal plus positive EMA on a second sample.
    • Adults (ESsCD 2025): tTG-IgA at least 10× ULN confirmed on a second sample, age under 45, no red flags.
    • Adults (BSG 2026): symptomatic adults in secondary care with at least 10× ULN, by shared decision; no age limit and no second sample needed.
    • Only 20–30% of adults with a raised tTG reach 10× ULN, so most still need biopsy.
    • Australia: no Australian adult no-biopsy guidance found. Assays vary and EMA is hard to get, so involve a gastroenterologist.
  • Point-of-care tests are not a diagnosis and aren't validated for primary care.

Current treatment

Lifelong gluten-free diet

  • Threshold (Catassi 2007 RCT, n=39 completers): 50 mg of gluten a day caused mucosal damage (villous height to crypt depth ratio −20%). 10 mg a day did not significantly (−1%) but was a grey area. One slice of bread holds about 2–3 g.
  • Labelling:
    • Australia and NZ: "gluten free" means no detectable gluten (ELISA detects about 1–3 ppm), and no oats.
    • USA, UK and EU: up to 20 ppm.
    • "Low gluten" (up to 200 ppm) is not suitable.
  • Oats: fewer than 10% of patients react to avenin, unpredictably. Oats can't be labelled gluten free in Australia; a supervised trial with biopsy is an individual decision.
  • Adherence: strict adherence 42–91% across 38 studies.
  • Hidden exposure: 88.7% of adults on a long-term GFD had at least one positive stool or urine gluten immunogenic peptide test over 4 weeks.
  • Burden: treatment burden scored 44.9/100, above diabetes (41.7) and below dialysis (56.4). Gluten-free baskets cost up to 17% more (Australia, 2016 data).

Follow-up

  • Coeliac Australia:
    • Dietitian at diagnosis and ongoing.
    • Serology every 3–6 months until normal, then every 1–2 years.
    • Optional repeat biopsy at about 2 years.
    • DXA for all adults at diagnosis, then every 5 years.
    • Pneumococcal vaccination (hyposplenism); consider meningococcal and Hib.
  • About 80% are seronegative by 6–12 months (ACG 2023). Normal serology doesn't guarantee mucosal healing.
  • ACG 2023 advises against home gluten-detection devices.

Non-responsive and refractory disease

  • Ongoing gluten exposure causes over a third of non-response. True refractory disease (RCD) is about 10% of non-responders. Exclude microscopic colitis, SIBO, pancreatic insufficiency and IBS first.
  • RCD I (normal intraepithelial lymphocytes): open-capsule budesonide gave clinical response in 92% and histological response in 89% (Mukewar 2017, n=57).
  • RCD II (aberrant clonal lymphocytes): 5-year survival 58% vs 96% in RCD I. 52% progressed to enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma (EATL), with 2-year survival of 15–20% (Amsterdam series).

Dermatitis herpetiformis

  • Dapsone 25–50 mg a day (up to 100 mg) controls the itch within days. It doesn't treat the gut; the GFD does.

Emerging treatments and trials

No drug is approved anywhere. FDA guidance on drugs as an add-on to the GFD is still a draft (2022).

Still standing

  • TAK-227 / ZED1227 (oral TG2 inhibitor):
    • Phase 2a (NEJM 2021, n=159, 3 g gluten a day for 6 weeks): protected the mucosa at all doses.
    • Phase 2b CEC-004 (n=397, villous atrophy despite GFD): missed the primary endpoint (27.1/16.9/25.3% vs 19.3% on placebo). 50 mg once daily improved histology. ICDS abstract, November 2026; not yet peer reviewed.
    • A phase 2 in non-responsive disease is recruiting.
  • DONQ52 (antibody against the HLA-DQ2.5–gluten complex): phase 1 (n=63) suppressed gluten-induced T-cell and IL-2 responses. ICDS abstract, 2026.
  • KAN-101 (tolerogenic, liver-targeted): phase 2 reported symptom benefit without numbers (2025). FDA Fast Track.
  • TPM502 (tolerising nanoparticle): phase 2a "positive" tolerance markers (2024); data not released.
  • IMU-856 (barrier regeneration): phase 1b (n=43) showed less villous height loss after gluten challenge (21–23 µm vs 60 µm). Post hoc and exploratory.
  • Ordesekimab (anti-IL-15): phase 2 in non-responsive disease (n=388) completed in 2024. No public results.

Fallen

  • Larazotide (tight-junction regulator): phase 3 CeDLara stopped in 2022; the sample size needed was too large.
  • Nexvax2 (peptide vaccine): stopped in 2019, no benefit over placebo.
  • Latiglutenase (CeliAction, n=494): missed its histology endpoint. Post hoc symptom signals in seropositive patients.
  • AMG 714 (anti-IL-15, n=64): primary endpoint negative.
  • TAK-062 (zamaglutenase) and TAK-101 (nanoparticle): discontinued by Takeda. TAK-101 cut interferon-γ responses by 88% but histology wasn't significant.

Theoretical frontiers

Hypotheses, not recommendations.

Causes: theories

  • A viral second hit: reovirus strain T1L broke oral tolerance to gluten in mice, and patients had higher anti-reovirus antibodies with higher IRF1 expression (Bouziat, Science 2017). Enterovirus in infant stool predicted later coeliac disease (aOR 1.49), adenovirus did not (Kahrs, BMJ 2019). Human data are associations only. Test: serial virome sequencing in HLA-risk birth cohorts timed against seroconversion.
  • Duodenal bacteria digest gluten differently: Pseudomonas aeruginosa from patients produced more immunogenic gluten fragments in mice, while Lactobacillus detoxified gluten (Caminero, Gastroenterology 2016). Test: does duodenal proteolytic activity before seroconversion predict disease?
  • Early antibiotics: 1.7 million Danish and Norwegian children, antibiotics in the first year OR 1.26, and 1.08 per extra course (Sander, Gastroenterology 2019). Adjusting for maternally reported infections weakened it to OR 1.18 (CI crossed 1). Test: sibling-controlled analyses with microbiome sampling.
  • It may not be only gluten: wheat amylase-trypsin inhibitors activate innate immune cells through TLR4, and TLR4-deficient mice were protected (Junker, J Exp Med 2012). Test: blinded challenge with ATI-depleted vs standard wheat in treated patients.
  • Microbial transglutaminase in processed food: Lerner and Matthias argue this bakery and meat-industry enzyme cross-links gluten into more immunogenic complexes. Evidence is ecological correlation; the authors concede as much. Test: challenge with enzyme-treated vs untreated food.
  • Modern wheat is probably not the culprit: average gluten content of wheat has changed little since the 1920s, but flour intake rose and added vital gluten roughly tripled from 1977 (Kasarda, J Agric Food Chem 2013). Dose beats breed as a hypothesis.
  • Season of birth: summer birth OR 1.06 overall, 1.17 for diagnosis before age 2 (Swedish national cohort, Arch Dis Child 2013). Consistent with infection timing, but a tiny effect.

Prevention: theoretical options

  • Cap the infant gluten dose: TEDDY's dose signal (HR 1.50 per g/day) is now being tested. GRAIN (Lund, n=1,141 HLA-DQ2 children) randomises under 3 g gluten a day to age 3 vs usual diet; primary completion is due 2030. The first prevention trial aimed at amount rather than timing.
  • Rotavirus immunisation: frequent rotavirus infection predicted coeliac autoimmunity in DAISY (rate ratio 1.94 per infection, n=1,931), and in TEDDY vaccinated infants given gluten before 6 months had HR 0.57. Against: UK primary care data (n=880,629) found HR 1.05. Any effect looks confined to a subgroup. Test: pre-specified coeliac outcomes in existing vaccine-era cohorts.
  • Gluten-degrading wheat as a population lever: CRISPR-edited wheat with up to 35 of 45 α-gliadin genes knocked out cut immunoreactivity 85% and was transgene-free (Sánchez-León, Plant Biotechnol J 2018). An RNAi line (E82) fed as bread for 3 days to 20 DQ2.5-positive patients produced no significant T-cell response, unlike standard bread (Nutrients 2021). Still about 2,226 ppm gluten, so nowhere near "gluten free". Test: a long challenge with histology.
  • Tolerance induction before disease: the drug pipeline's tolerising agents (KAN-101, TPM502) are all tested in established disease. The untried idea is primary tolerance in HLA-screened infants. Test: a safety-first phase 1 in screened at-risk children, with tTG seroconversion as the endpoint.
  • Screen, then watch: Italy now screens children aged 2–10 by law, and screening finds 1.5–2.9% positive. Whether treating screen-detected asymptomatic children prevents fracture, anaemia or growth failure is unknown. Test: randomise screen-detected asymptomatic cases to immediate vs deferred diet.

Treatment: theoretical options

  • Gluten-specific engineered Tregs: TCR-edited, HLA-DQ2.5-restricted regulatory T cells homed to gut lymphoid tissue after oral gluten and suppressed effector T cells against a second epitope (bystander suppression); polyclonal Tregs did not (Porret, Sci Transl Med 2025). Mouse and in-vitro only. Test: a first-in-human dose-escalation with gluten challenge and ELISPOT readouts.
  • Better enzymes, not more enzymes: latiglutenase failed on histology, but the engineered protease EP-B2 prevented clinical relapse during daily gluten challenge in a gluten-sensitive macaque (PLoS ONE 2008), and oral commensals (Rothia) make subtilisins that cleave the peptides mammalian proteases cannot (Am J Physiol 2016). Test: a glutenase judged on gluten immunogenic peptide excretion and histology, not symptoms.
  • Probiotics engineered to digest gluten in the duodenum: plausible from the Lactobacillus detoxification data, but no probiotic has yet shown mucosal protection in humans. Test: a colonisation-verified strain in a 6-week low-dose gluten challenge.
  • Oral antibody capture: AGY, an anti-gliadin IgY from egg yolk, is safe in patients and designed to bind gluten in the lumen. Efficacy trials are small and unreported. Test: a crossover challenge with GIP endpoints.
  • JAK inhibition for refractory type II disease: IL-15 signals through JAK–STAT, and an open-label study of tofacitinib reported enduring clinical remission in refractory type II disease (Dieckman, Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol 2024). Uncontrolled and tiny, in a condition with 58% 5-year survival. Test: a multicentre trial with aberrant clone burden as the endpoint.
  • Worms are out: hookworm (Necator americanus) larvae did not improve gluten tolerance in a 94-week RCT (n=54; completion 57% placebo vs 37–44% hookworm). A tidy hypothesis, a flat result.
  • AI as the quality-control step: a model trained on about 3,400 NHS duodenal biopsies reached roughly 97% accuracy, sensitivity above 95% and specificity near 98% on an unseen hospital's slides (NEJM AI 2025), against pathologist disagreement in over 1 in 5 cases. Test: prospective deployment measuring missed Marsh 3a and repeat-biopsy rates.

Gaps and open questions

Labelled hypotheses, not recommendations.

  • Screening: USPSTF (2017) found insufficient evidence for screening asymptomatic people.
    • Screening finds 1.5–2.9% positive in children (Colorado ASK 1.5%, Italian pilot 2.8–2.9%) and 2.6% in Danish adolescents.
    • Italy now screens children aged 2–10 by law.
    • Whether screen-detected, asymptomatic patients benefit from a lifelong diet is untested.
  • Hypothesis: the gluten threshold is individual. One small 90-day RCT underpins the 10–50 mg a day range. Some patients react at 10 mg.
  • Hypothesis: the end point is wrong. Persistent villous atrophy (43% in a Swedish cohort) carried higher lymphoma risk (102.4 vs 31.5 per 100,000 a year) but no excess mortality (HR 0.99). We don't know whether chasing mucosal healing improves hard outcomes.
  • Hypothesis: amount of early gluten matters, not timing. TEDDY's dose signal is observational. A dose-limiting infant trial in high-risk HLA children is the logical test.
  • Australian gaps: no adult no-biopsy consensus, no national prevalence data, and no EMA access in many labs.
  • Excluded populations: Asia, Africa and the Middle East are under-studied despite rising wheat intake; most trials enrol white Europeans and North Americans.
  • Hypothesis: drugs will be adjuncts, not cures. Every drug so far is aimed at reducing harm from accidental exposure on top of a GFD, not replacing it.

Prognosis and quality of life

  • Mortality (Lebwohl, JAMA 2020; 49,829 Swedish cases): 9.7 vs 8.6 deaths per 1,000 person-years, HR 1.21. HR 1.69 at age 18–39 and 2.34 in the first year after diagnosis.
  • Small bowel adenocarcinoma: about 3-fold risk but rare: 0.06% vs 0.02%, 1 extra case per 2,944 patients over 10 years.
  • Lymphoma: higher with persistent villous atrophy (102.4 vs 31.5 per 100,000 a year; general population 24.2).
  • Fracture: OR 1.43 (meta-analysis, 20,955 patients).
  • Pregnancy (untreated disease): low birthweight RR 1.75, fetal growth restriction 1.54, preterm birth 1.37, miscarriage 1.39. The excess risk isn't significant on a GFD. Coeliac disease is commoner in unexplained infertility (OR 5.06).
  • Mucosa: recovery in 34% at 2 years and 66% at 5 years in adults.
  • Gluten ataxia remains a contested entity: antigliadin antibodies in about 41% of sporadic idiopathic ataxia in one series.

Weird trivia

  • Gee's mussels: alongside his famous line about diet, Gee reported a child who did well on "a quart of the best Dutch mussels daily" — but could not keep it up beyond one season.
  • 200 bananas a week: Sidney Haas's 1924 banana diet (8 children) genuinely rescued children when about 30% of those affected died, and one 1950s patient was prescribed 200 bananas weekly. Haas had the wrong mechanism, blamed starch, and rejected the gluten-free diet for the rest of his career.
  • Communion is a canonical problem: the Vatican's 2017 circular letter states that completely gluten-free hosts are invalid matter for the Eucharist, while low-gluten hosts are valid — and that matter made with genetically modified organisms counts as valid. Ordinary wafers hold roughly 2–5 mg of gliadin.
  • Dogs get it too: gluten-sensitive enteropathy in a family of Irish setters fitted a single autosomal recessive locus, 56,250 times more likely than a dominant model (Garden, Am J Vet Res 2000). Border terriers have paroxysmal gluten-sensitive dyskinesia, with raised anti-gliadin IgG and anti-canine TG2 IgA (J Vet Intern Med 2018, 128 dogs).
  • And monkeys: gluten-sensitive rhesus macaques develop anti-gliadin and anti-transglutaminase antibodies, and an oral protease prevented relapse during daily gluten challenge in one animal (PLoS ONE 2008).
  • The enemy has a big genome: bread wheat is hexaploid — 42 chromosomes, about 17 billion base pairs, five times the human genome and roughly 80% repetitive. That is why editing gliadin genes out took CRISPR and 35 knockouts.
  • A biopsy tube started a specialty: Margot Shiner's 1956 peroral biopsy capsule is credited by John Walker-Smith with launching paediatric gastroenterology as a subspecialty.
