Types of incontinence

Leaking isn’t a normal part of ageing, having babies or being a man over 60. Working out which type you have matters, because the treatments are different, and most people improve.

The main types

  • Stress incontinence: leaks with coughing, sneezing, laughing, lifting or exercise. Weak pelvic floor or urethral support. Common after childbirth and menopause. See stress incontinence.
  • Urge incontinence (overactive bladder): a sudden, strong urge, then leaking before reaching the toilet; often frequency and getting up at night. See overactive bladder.
  • Mixed: both stress and urge; treat the more bothersome one first.
  • Overflow: the bladder doesn’t empty, so it dribbles; weak stream, straining. Common with an enlarged prostate, constipation, diabetes or some medicines. See overflow incontinence.
  • Functional: the bladder works but getting to the toilet is the problem: mobility, arthritis, dementia, distance.
  • Bowel (faecal) leakage: common, under-reported, and often helped by treating constipation or diarrhoea and pelvic floor training.

Helpful first steps

  • Keep a 3-day bladder diary: how much you drink, when you pass urine and when you leak.
  • See your GP for a urine test, examination and, for some, a scan of how well the bladder empties.
  • Continence physiotherapists and continence nurses are the specialists for most types.
  • Treat constipation, cough and weight; review medicines (diuretics, sedatives, anticholinergics).

Where to get help

  • National Continence Helpline: 1800 33 00 66 (free)
  • Your GP, a continence physio or continence nurse

Common questions

What are the types of urinary incontinence?

Stress (leaks with cough or exercise), urge (sudden urge then leak), mixed, overflow (bladder doesn’t empty) and functional (trouble getting to the toilet).

Is incontinence a normal part of ageing?

No. It’s common, but most people can improve or be cured with the right treatment.

Who treats incontinence?

Start with your GP. Continence physiotherapists and nurses treat most people; urologists and urogynaecologists help when needed.
