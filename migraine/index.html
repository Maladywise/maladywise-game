Fact sheet

Migraine

Repeated attacks of throbbing headache, often with nausea and a strong dislike of light and sound. Some people get an aura first. Very treatable: treat attacks early, and prevent them smartly.

The migraine ladder

Treat attacks early. If they hit 4 or more days a month, add prevention. Give each preventive 2 to 3 months before judging it.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careRoutine and a headache diaryRegular sleep, meals and water, steady caffeine, and a diary to spot triggers and count headache days.
    Works
    Helps some people have fewer attacks; evidence is modest
    Cost
    Free
    How long
    Keep the diary for 2–3 months
    Watch for
    Chasing every possible food trigger. Most people don’t need a restrictive diet.

    Migraine brains like boring. Same bedtime, same breakfast, same coffee count.

  2. Step 2 · PharmacyEarly pain relief plus an anti-nausea medicineAspirin, ibuprofen or paracetamol at the first sign of headache. Anti-nausea medicine eases sickness and helps the pain relief absorb.
    Works
    About half get good relief within 2 hours
    Cost
    About $5–15 a pack
    How long
    Take at the first sign, not once it’s a 9 out of 10
    Watch for
    Stomach upset with aspirin or ibuprofen. Pain relief on 15 or more days a month causes rebound headaches.

    “Wait and see if it gets bad” is a strategy. Just not a good one.

  3. Step 3 · PharmacyMagnesium or riboflavin (vitamin B2)Supplements that may reduce how often attacks happen.
    Works
    May cut attacks a little; evidence is weak to moderate
    Cost
    About $15–30 a month
    How long
    3 months to judge
    Watch for
    Magnesium: loose bowels. Riboflavin: bright yellow wee (harmless).

    Yes, the neon wee is normal. No, it’s not a superpower.

  4. Step 4 · PrescriptionTriptans (such as sumatriptan or rizatriptan)Migraine-specific tablets, wafers or sprays for attacks that simple pain relief doesn’t fix.
    Works
    About 6 in 10 get relief within 2 hours; about 3 in 10 are pain-free
    Cost
    Under the $25 PBS co-payment, about $20–25 for 4 tablets
    How long
    Take early in the headache. Can repeat once after 2 hours if it comes back
    Watch for
    Chest or throat tightness, tingling, drowsiness. Not with heart disease or past stroke. Keep to under 10 days a month.

    If one triptan flops, try another. They don’t all have the same personality.

  5. Step 5 · PrescriptionPreventive tabletsDaily tablets such as propranolol, amitriptyline, candesartan or topiramate, if attacks hit 4 or more days a month or are disabling.
    Works
    About 4 to 5 in 10 people have their migraine days halved
    Cost
    Usually under the $25 PBS co-payment, about $10–25 a month
    How long
    2–3 months to judge; often 6–12 months, then review
    Watch for
    Propranolol: tiredness, cold hands, not with asthma. Amitriptyline: drowsiness, dry mouth. Topiramate: tingling, not in pregnancy.

    Start low, go slow, and don’t judge it in week two.

  6. Step 6 · Allied healthPsychology: CBT, relaxation and biofeedbackSkills that calm the nervous system and lower attack frequency.
    Works
    Helps reduce how often attacks happen; evidence is moderate
    Cost
    About $150–250 a session; Medicare rebates may apply with a GP plan
    How long
    Usually 6–10 sessions
    Watch for
    Needs practice between sessions. It doesn’t mean the pain is “in your head”.

    A real treatment, not a consolation prize.

  7. Step 7 · SpecialistCGRP antibody injections or botulinum toxinFor frequent migraine when several preventives haven’t worked, through a neurologist.
    Works
    About half of people get their migraine days at least halved
    Cost
    $25 per script on the PBS ($7.70 concession) for chronic migraine after 3 preventives; otherwise several hundred dollars a month
    How long
    Monthly injections (CGRP) or every 12 weeks (botulinum toxin); reviewed after a few months
    Watch for
    Injection-site reactions and constipation with some CGRP medicines. Botulinum toxin: neck pain, droopy eyelid.

    The newer options are genuinely good. The paperwork is genuinely long.

Costs are rough Australian prices in October 2026 and vary by pharmacy. PBS co-payment: $25, or $7.70 with a concession card. Results are typical figures from trials.

What you can do

  • Treat early: pain relief at the first twinge, not the tenth.
  • Regular sleep, meals and water. Keep caffeine to 1 to 2 cups a day.
  • Keep a headache diary. It spots triggers and counts headache days, which decide your treatment.
  • Reaching for pain relief more than 2 to 3 days a week? That’s a prevention problem, not a tablet shortage.

See your GP if

  • Attacks are frequent or getting worse
  • You use pain relief more than 2 to 3 days a week
  • Headaches have changed pattern, or first started after age 50

Call 000 or go to the emergency department for a sudden severe “worst ever” headache, fever with a stiff neck, new weakness, confusion or trouble speaking.

Some migraine medicines, including topiramate, valproate and candesartan, aren’t safe in pregnancy, and the combined pill isn’t recommended with migraine with aura. Tell your doctor if you’re pregnant or planning to be.

Common questions

What is the best thing to take for a migraine?

Aspirin, ibuprofen or paracetamol taken at the first sign of headache, ideally with an anti-nausea medicine. If that isn’t enough, triptans are migraine-specific tablets, wafers or sprays on prescription.

When should I take a preventive medicine for migraine?

If attacks happen on 4 or more days a month or are disabling. Options include propranolol, amitriptyline, candesartan and topiramate. Give each one 2 to 3 months before judging it.

Can painkillers make headaches worse?

Yes. Simple pain relief on 15 or more days a month, or triptans on 10 or more, can cause rebound (medication-overuse) headaches.

When is a headache an emergency?

Call 000 or go to the emergency department for a sudden severe “worst ever” headache, fever with a stiff neck, new weakness, confusion or trouble speaking.
