Lowering Your Dementia Risk

Dementia isn’t an automatic part of ageing. About 45% of cases are linked to 14 things you can change. None are glamorous, all are doable, and it’s never too late to start.

The 14 risk factors. Early life: less education. Midlife: hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, head injury, inactivity, diabetes, smoking, high blood pressure, obesity, too much alcohol. Later life: social isolation, air pollution, untreated vision loss.

The dementia risk ladder

No pill prevents dementia, so this ladder is about stacking the odds. Every step helps on its own; together they add up.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careMove moreWalking, swimming, gardening, dancing: anything that gets you a bit puffed.
    Works
    Linked to lower dementia risk; trials show small boosts to thinking skills
    Cost
    Free (walking shoes optional)
    How long
    Aim for 150 minutes a week, for life. Any amount beats none.
    Watch for
    Start gently if you have heart or joint problems.

    The best exercise is the one you’ll still be doing next year.

  2. Step 2 · Self-careStop smoking, ease up on alcoholTwo of the big midlife risks, and both are fixable at any age.
    Works
    Risk falls after quitting, even later in life. Heavy drinking is clearly linked to higher risk.
    Cost
    Quitline (13 7848) is free; nicotine patches are PBS-subsidised. Quitting saves thousands a year.
    How long
    Ongoing. Most people need a few attempts to quit, which is normal.
    Watch for
    Keep alcohol to 10 standard drinks a week or less, with some alcohol-free days.

    A “brain-healthy” red wine is still mostly wine.

  3. Step 3 · Self-careStay connected and keep learningFriends, clubs, volunteering, a language, an instrument, a new job skill.
    Works
    Linked to lower risk; the evidence is from observation rather than trials
    Cost
    Free to cheap: libraries, community groups, U3A-style courses
    How long
    Ongoing. Regular beats occasional.
    Watch for
    Low mood or loneliness that sticks around is worth raising with your GP.

    Brain-training apps make you good at brain-training apps. Real-world learning travels further.

  4. Step 4 · Allied healthHearing test and hearing aidsHearing loss is one of the two biggest changeable risks.
    Works
    Wearing hearing aids is linked with about a fifth less cognitive decline; trial results are mixed but promising in higher-risk people
    Cost
    Free for eligible pensioners and veterans (Hearing Services Program); otherwise about $2,000–12,000 a pair
    How long
    Wear them every day. It takes a few weeks to get used to them.
    Watch for
    Earwax, ear pain or sudden hearing loss need a GP visit first.

    Hearing aids in the drawer don’t count. Sorry.

  5. Step 5 · Allied healthEye checks, glasses and cataract surgeryUntreated vision loss is a later-life risk factor.
    Works
    Treating vision loss, including cataract surgery, is linked with lower risk
    Cost
    Optometrist eye tests are usually bulk-billed; cataract surgery is free as a public patient (waits vary)
    How long
    Eye test every 1–2 years over 65, or as advised.
    Watch for
    Sudden vision loss or a curtain over your vision is an emergency.

    Squinting is not a personality trait. Book the eye test.

  6. Step 6 · PrescriptionTreat high blood pressureAim for a top (systolic) reading of 130 or less from about age 40.
    Works
    Lowering blood pressure modestly cuts dementia and memory problems in trials
    Cost
    Under the $25 PBS co-payment, often about $10–25 a month
    How long
    Long term. Lifestyle first; tablets if numbers stay high.
    Watch for
    Dizziness on standing, ankle swelling with some tablets. Don’t stop without talking to your GP.

    High blood pressure is silent. The cuff is the only way to know.

  7. Step 7 · PrescriptionLower high LDL cholesterol, manage diabetes and depressionUsually a statin for cholesterol, plus whatever keeps blood sugar and mood on track.
    Works
    High LDL from midlife is a major risk factor; statins lower LDL by about a third to a half
    Cost
    Statins: under the $25 PBS co-payment, about $10–20 a month
    How long
    Long term, with regular blood tests.
    Watch for
    Muscle aches (often not caused by the statin itself). Low blood sugars with some diabetes medicines.

    Your heart and your brain share the same plumbing. Look after one, help the other.

Costs are rough Australian prices in October 2026 and vary by pharmacy and provider. PBS co-payment: $25, or $7.70 with a concession card. Effects are from population studies and trials, not promises.

What you can do

  • Book a heart health check: from 45, or 30 for First Nations people. It covers blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar.
  • Get your hearing and eyes tested, even if you think they’re “fine”.
  • Helmets for bikes and horses. Take head knocks seriously.
  • Avoid smoke and traffic fumes where you can.
  • Skip the “brain health” supplements and spend the money on walking shoes or a course.

See your GP if

  • You or others notice worsening memory, getting lost in familiar places, trouble with money or medicines, or personality changes
  • You have trouble hearing conversations or seeing clearly
  • Low mood or loneliness is affecting you

Call 000 for sudden confusion, face drooping, arm weakness or slurred speech. See a doctor the same day for confusion after a head injury. For memory concerns or support, call the free National Dementia Helpline on 1800 100 500 (24/7).

Common questions

Can dementia be prevented?

Not always, but the 2024 Lancet Commission found about 45% of dementia is linked to 14 risk factors that can be changed. Acting on them can prevent or delay it, at any age.

Do hearing aids lower dementia risk?

Hearing loss is one of the two biggest risk factors, and wearing hearing aids is linked with less cognitive decline. Eligible pensioners and veterans can get free hearing aids through the Hearing Services Program.

Do brain-training apps prevent dementia?

There is little evidence they do. You get better at the games, but learning real-world skills and staying social are a better use of time.

Do supplements help prevent dementia?

No. Ginkgo, fish oil, vitamin E and “brain health” supplements haven’t prevented dementia in trials.
