Drug info

Low-dose naltrexone (LDN)

Opioid receptor blocker, tiny dose Prescription only (S4), compounded

Naltrexone at about a tenth of the usual dose, made up by compounding pharmacies. Popular for fibromyalgia, chronic pain and other conditions, with promising small studies but mixed results in bigger trials.

Quick answer: Naltrexone is approved at 50 mg to treat alcohol and opioid dependence. At low doses (usually 1 to 4.5 mg at night) it’s used off label for fibromyalgia, chronic pain, Crohn’s disease, long COVID, ME/CFS and other conditions. It’s cheap and usually well tolerated, but the evidence is still early, and it blocks opioid painkillers.

Brand names

  • Compounded low-dose naltrexone (no TGA-registered low-dose product). Full-dose brands: Revia and generics (50 mg)

How it works

At full dose, naltrexone blocks opioid receptors. At low doses it’s thought to work differently: a brief overnight block may lead the body to make more of its own endorphins, and it may calm overactive immune cells in the nervous system (microglia) through a receptor called TLR4. Both ideas are still being tested.

Used for

  • Fibromyalgia: a 2023 review in the RACGP’s journal (AJGP) found small studies (8 to 31 people each) all reporting less pain, and called the evidence preliminary. A larger 2023 randomised trial (Lancet Rheumatology, 6 mg) then found no difference from placebo
  • Crohn’s disease: a Cochrane review found too little evidence to be sure it helps
  • Long COVID and ME/CFS: mostly observational reports; randomised trials are under way
  • Chronic pain, CRPS and multiple sclerosis symptoms: small studies with mixed results
  • Used only off label and usually after standard treatments

Side effects

Usually mild and often settle within a couple of weeks. Taking it in the morning can help if dreams or sleep are a problem.

Get help urgently if

  • Severe stomach pain, yellow skin or eyes, or dark urine
  • Withdrawal symptoms if you take opioids (sweating, shaking, vomiting, agitation)
  • Signs of an allergic reaction

Good to know

  • Opioids (codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, morphine and others): LDN can weaken their pain relief and upset the gut. Full withdrawal is unlikely at low doses but possible, so only combine them if your doctor plans it.
  • Tell any doctor, dentist or hospital you take it, especially before surgery.
  • Use the same compounding pharmacy for consistent doses.
  • Take it at bedtime; switch to the morning if dreams or sleep are a problem.
  • Give it a fair trial (about 3 months), then review with your doctor whether it’s worth continuing.

Common questions

What is low-dose naltrexone used for?

It’s used off label for fibromyalgia, chronic pain, Crohn’s disease, long COVID, ME/CFS and some other conditions. It isn’t TGA-approved for any of these.

Does LDN work for fibromyalgia?

Small early studies looked promising, but a larger randomised trial in 2023 found no benefit over placebo. Some people still report it helps; the evidence is mixed.

Can I take LDN with opioid painkillers?

Only if your doctor plans it. Naltrexone blocks opioid receptors, so pain relief may be weaker and gut side effects more likely; withdrawal is unlikely at low doses but possible.

How much does LDN cost in Australia?

It isn’t on the PBS at low doses, so you pay the compounding pharmacy’s private price, typically a few dollars a day.
