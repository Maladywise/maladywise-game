Drug info

Guanfacine

Alpha-2A agonist (non-stimulant) Prescription only

A once-a-day non-stimulant ADHD medicine for children and teenagers. Not addictive, used alone or with a stimulant, and plays the long game rather than the quick hit.

Quick answer: The most common side effects of guanfacine are sleepiness, headache, tummy pain and tiredness. It can also cause low blood pressure or a slow pulse, so these are checked regularly. It’s a non-stimulant ADHD medicine that builds up over 2–4 weeks. Don’t stop it suddenly, as blood pressure can rebound. Get help urgently for fainting or a very slow pulse.

At a glance

For
ADHD in children and teens aged 6–17 (sometimes adults, off-label)
Works
In a key trial, about 6 in 10 children responded, versus about 4 in 10 on a dummy treatment. A smaller effect than stimulants
Cost
$25 a script on the PBS for ADHD with an authority, if diagnosed at age 6–17 and started by a paediatrician or psychiatrist ($7.70 concession); private scripts cost more
Kicks in
Builds over 2–4 weeks as the dose is stepped up
Taken for
Months to years, reviewed at least yearly
Watch for
Sleepiness, tiredness, headache, tummy pain, low blood pressure or slow pulse

There’s no instant effect, so judge it after a month, not a morning.

Brand names

  • Intuniv (extended-release tablets, taken once a day)

How it works

Guanfacine acts on alpha-2A receptors in the front of the brain, strengthening the signals that control attention, planning and impulses. It doesn’t work like a stimulant.

Used for

  • ADHD in children and teenagers aged 6 to 17, when stimulants aren’t suitable, aren’t tolerated or aren’t enough (TGA-approved)
  • Sometimes used in adults with ADHD by specialists (off-label)

Side effects

Full side-effect list (from the product information)

How often, from the official product information. Bold = serious.

Very common more than 1 in 10

  • Sleepiness
  • Headache
  • Tummy pain
  • Tiredness

Common 1 in 10 to 1 in 100

  • Low mood, anxiety or mood swings
  • Trouble sleeping or nightmares
  • Sedation, lethargy or dizziness
  • Slow heart rate
  • Low blood pressure, incl. on standing
  • Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea or constipation
  • Dry mouth
  • Rash
  • Bedwetting
  • Irritability
  • Less appetite, or weight gain

Uncommon 1 in 100 to 1 in 1,000

  • Allergic reactions
  • Agitation or seeing things that aren’t there
  • Seizures
  • Fainting
  • Faster or irregular heartbeat, first-degree heart block
  • Pale skin
  • Asthma
  • Indigestion
  • Itch
  • Passing urine more often
  • Chest pain
  • Weakness
  • Raised blood pressure

Rare 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000

  • High blood pressure, incl. hypertensive encephalopathy (severe headache and confusion), mainly after stopping suddenly
  • Oversleeping
  • Feeling generally unwell

Get help urgently if

  • Fainting or a very slow pulse
  • Chest pain or a racing, irregular heartbeat
  • Severe drowsiness, especially if other sedating medicines were taken

Good to know

  • Don’t stop suddenly. Blood pressure can rebound. The dose is reduced slowly.
  • Swallow whole; don’t crush or chew. Avoid taking it with a high-fat meal, which raises how much is absorbed.
  • Some medicines and grapefruit juice raise guanfacine levels. Check with your pharmacist.
  • Blood pressure, pulse, height and weight are checked regularly.

Common questions

Is guanfacine a stimulant?

No. It’s a non-stimulant ADHD medicine and isn’t addictive. It can be used alone or added to a stimulant.

How long does guanfacine take to work?

It builds up over 2 to 4 weeks as the dose is increased, so it’s judged over weeks, not days.

What are the side effects of guanfacine?

Sleepiness, headache, tummy pain and tiredness are very common. Don’t stop it suddenly, as blood pressure can rebound.
