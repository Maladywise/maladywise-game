Opioid pain medicines

Strong pain relievers Prescription only, some S8

Strong pain relievers for severe short-term pain, such as after surgery or a fracture. Poor for long-term pain, and they carry real risks of dependence and overdose.

Quick answer: Opioids such as codeine, tramadol, tapentadol, oxycodone and morphine act on the body’s opioid receptors to block pain. They work well for a few days of severe pain. For long-term pain (like back pain or arthritis) they help little over months and the risks build up, so guidelines advise using them sparingly, at the lowest dose, for the shortest time, with a plan to stop.

Brand names

  • Codeine (Panadeine Forte), tramadol (Tramal), tapentadol (Palexia), oxycodone (Endone, OxyContin, Targin), morphine (MS Contin, Ordine), buprenorphine patches (Norspan), fentanyl patches, hydromorphone

How it works

Opioids attach to opioid receptors in the brain, spinal cord and gut, dampening pain signals and how much pain bothers you. The same receptors control breathing and gut movement, which explains slowed breathing and constipation. With regular use the body adapts (tolerance), and stopping suddenly causes withdrawal.

Used for

  • Severe acute pain: after surgery, injuries, kidney stones (usually a few days)
  • Cancer pain and palliative care
  • Not recommended as first choice for long-term back pain, osteoarthritis, headaches or fibromyalgia
  • Since 2018, codeine needs a prescription in Australia

Side effects

Side effects are common, especially when starting or increasing the dose.

Full side-effect list (from the product information)

How often, from the official product information. Bold = serious.

Very common more than 1 in 10

  • Constipation
  • Nausea
  • Drowsiness, dizziness

Common 1 in 10 to 1 in 100

  • Itch
  • Dry mouth
  • Sweating
  • Confusion (especially in older people)
  • Low mood

Uncommon 1 in 100 to 1 in 1,000

  • Slowed breathing
  • Falls
  • Dependence and addiction
  • Low sex hormones with long-term use
  • Increased sensitivity to pain (opioid-induced hyperalgesia)

Rare 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000

  • Serotonin syndrome (tramadol, tapentadol)
  • Seizures (tramadol)

Get help urgently if

  • Very sleepy and hard to wake, or slow or shallow breathing: call 000 and give naloxone if you have it
  • Pinpoint pupils with confusion
  • Severe constipation with tummy swelling or vomiting

Good to know

  • Don’t mix with alcohol, benzodiazepines, pregabalin or gabapentin, or other sedating medicines: the combination is a leading cause of overdose deaths.
  • Keep free naloxone at home if you take opioids regularly (available from pharmacies without a prescription).
  • Don’t drive until you know how it affects you.
  • Take a laxative from the start.
  • Don’t stop suddenly after regular use: taper with your doctor.
  • Store safely away from children and return leftovers to a pharmacy.

Common questions

Are opioids good for chronic back pain?

Usually not. Trials show little benefit over months, and the risks of dependence, falls and overdose build up. Exercise, physio, other medicines and pain programs are preferred.

What is naloxone?

A medicine that reverses an opioid overdose. It’s free from pharmacies in Australia without a prescription, as a nasal spray or injection.

Is tramadol a weak opioid?

It’s weaker for pain but has extra risks: seizures, serotonin syndrome with some antidepressants, and dependence.

Why do opioids cause constipation?

Opioid receptors in the gut slow its movement. A laxative from the start helps.
