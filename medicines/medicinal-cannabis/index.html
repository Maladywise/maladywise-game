Medicinal cannabis

Prescription only Mostly unapproved products

Prescription cannabis products containing CBD, THC or both. Strong evidence for a few rare epilepsies; modest or uncertain benefit for most other uses, and real side effects, especially from THC.

Quick answer: In Australia, medicinal cannabis is legal on prescription. Only two products have been fully assessed and approved by the TGA; more than 99% of what’s prescribed is ‘unapproved’, meaning its quality, safety and benefit haven’t been checked by the regulator. The best evidence is for some severe childhood epilepsies. For chronic pain, chemotherapy nausea and MS muscle stiffness, benefit is small to moderate. For anxiety and sleep, good trials are lacking. You can’t legally drive with any THC in your system in most of Australia.

Not a miracle, not a menace. A medicine with a few clear uses and a lot of hype.

Brand names

  • Approved (ARTG registered): Epidyolex (CBD oral solution) and Sativex (nabiximols mouth spray)
  • Unapproved: hundreds of oils, capsules, flower (dried cannabis for vaporising) and others, prescribed through TGA access pathways

How it works

Cannabis contains many cannabinoids. THC acts on the body’s CB1 receptors in the brain and nerves, which affects pain, mood, appetite and nausea, and causes the ‘high’. CBD acts in several other ways; it isn’t intoxicating and reduces seizures in some epilepsies. Products differ hugely in how much THC and CBD they contain.

Used for

  • Severe childhood epilepsies (Dravet and Lennox–Gastaut syndromes): CBD reduces seizures. Epidyolex is PBS-listed for these, with conditions
  • Muscle stiffness (spasticity) in multiple sclerosis: Sativex helps some people a little
  • Nausea and vomiting from chemotherapy, when standard anti-sickness medicines haven’t worked
  • Chronic pain: on average a small benefit, and only for some people; not a first choice
  • Palliative care: appetite, nausea, pain and sleep
  • Anxiety, insomnia, PTSD: popular, but good-quality trials are lacking

Side effects

Side effects are common, especially with THC, and are more likely at higher doses and in older people.

Full side-effect list (from the product information)

How often, from the official product information. Bold = serious.

Very common more than 1 in 10

  • Drowsiness, tiredness
  • Dizziness
  • Dry mouth

Common 1 in 10 to 1 in 100

  • Nausea, diarrhoea (CBD), changes in appetite
  • Poor concentration and memory
  • Anxiety, low mood, feeling ‘high’ (THC)
  • Raised liver enzymes (CBD, especially with valproate)

Uncommon 1 in 100 to 1 in 1,000

  • Paranoia, hallucinations or psychosis (THC)
  • Fast heart rate, low blood pressure on standing
  • Falls in older people

Rare 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000

  • Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome: severe repeated vomiting with long-term THC use
  • Dependence and withdrawal with regular THC use

Get help urgently if

  • Chest pain, fainting or a racing heart
  • Hearing or seeing things, or feeling unusually suspicious or unsafe
  • Repeated severe vomiting, especially if hot showers ease it
  • Thoughts of harming yourself
  • Yellow skin or eyes (liver problems)

Good to know

  • Medicinal cannabis should never be a first-choice treatment. The Medical Board expects it only when there’s an evidence-supported reason and other treatments haven’t worked, with a plan to stop if it doesn’t help.
  • Expect a proper assessment, not a brief online chat. Medical history, mental health and other medicines all matter.
  • Avoid THC if you’re pregnant or breastfeeding, have had psychosis or bipolar disorder, have heart disease, or are under 25 (the developing brain is more sensitive).
  • Interactions: CBD and THC can change levels of other medicines, including clobazam, valproate, warfarin, some antidepressants and opioids. Tell your pharmacist.
  • Driving: it’s an offence in most states to drive with any THC detected, even with a prescription and even if you feel fine. CBD-only products are generally OK if you don’t feel impaired. See Medicinal cannabis and driving.
  • Travelling overseas with cannabis medicines can be illegal in your destination. Check before you go.
  • The TGA reviewed the unapproved access pathways in 2025 because of very high prescribing volumes and safety concerns, so rules may change.

Common questions

Is medicinal cannabis legal in Australia?

Yes, on prescription from a doctor or nurse practitioner. Buying or growing cannabis without a prescription is still illegal in most of Australia (the ACT allows a small amount of personal cultivation).

What’s the difference between CBD and THC?

THC causes the ‘high’ and most side effects such as drowsiness and anxiety. CBD doesn’t make you high; it has good evidence only for some rare epilepsies. Many products contain both.

Does medicinal cannabis work for chronic pain?

For most people, the benefit is small: a modest drop in pain for a minority, with common side effects. It isn’t recommended as a first-choice pain treatment.

Is medicinal cannabis on the PBS?

Only Epidyolex (CBD) for some severe childhood epilepsies, with conditions. Almost all other products aren’t subsidised and can cost hundreds of dollars a month.

Can I drive on medicinal cannabis?

Not with THC in most of Australia: any detectable THC is an offence, even with a prescription. In Victoria and Tasmania a court can take your prescription into account, but you can still be charged. CBD-only products are generally fine if you don’t feel impaired.

Can you get addicted to medicinal cannabis?

Yes, to THC: about 1 in 10 regular users develop dependence, with withdrawal (irritability, poor sleep, cravings) on stopping. CBD alone isn’t addictive.

Information only. Not a recommendation or endorsement of any product. Whether a medicine suits you depends on your health and other medicines, so talk to your doctor or pharmacist. In an emergency, call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number. From a mobile, 112 works in most countries.
