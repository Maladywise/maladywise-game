Anti-inflammatories (NSAIDs)

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory OTC and prescription

Medicines that ease pain, swelling and fever by blocking inflammatory chemicals. Very effective for sprains, period pain and gout, but they can upset the stomach, kidneys and heart.

Quick answer: NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and celecoxib reduce pain, inflammation and fever. They’re often better than paracetamol for period pain, dental pain, sprains and gout. Short courses at the lowest dose are safest; long-term use raises the risk of stomach bleeding, kidney problems and heart attack or stroke.

How it works

NSAIDs block COX enzymes, which make prostaglandins: chemicals that drive pain, swelling and fever. Prostaglandins also protect the stomach lining and keep blood flowing to the kidneys, which is where the side effects come from. Celecoxib targets COX-2 more, so it’s gentler on the stomach.

Used for

  • Acute pain: sprains, strains, dental and post-operative pain
  • Period pain (often the best first choice)
  • Gout attacks and arthritis flares
  • Back and neck pain (short courses)
  • Fever
  • Gels on the skin work well for sore knees and hands with fewer side effects

Side effects

Most people take short courses without problems. Risks rise with higher doses, longer use, older age, and other medicines.

Full side-effect list (from the product information)

How often, from the official product information. Bold = serious.

Common 1 in 10 to 1 in 100

  • Indigestion, heartburn, nausea
  • Headache, dizziness
  • Fluid retention and swollen ankles
  • Raised blood pressure

Uncommon 1 in 100 to 1 in 1,000

  • Stomach ulcer or bleeding
  • Kidney problems
  • Worsening asthma in some people
  • Rash

Rare 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000

  • Heart attack or stroke (higher with long-term use)
  • Severe allergic reactions

Get help urgently if

  • Black, tarry stools or vomiting blood
  • Chest pain, sudden weakness or trouble speaking: call 000
  • Wheezing or breathlessness after a dose
  • Passing much less urine, or swollen legs

Good to know

  • Take with food, at the lowest dose for the shortest time.
  • Avoid or ask first if you have a stomach ulcer history, kidney disease, heart failure, heart disease, or take blood thinners.
  • The ‘triple whammy’: NSAIDs with a blood pressure ACE inhibitor or sartan and a diuretic can injure the kidneys, especially when dehydrated.
  • Avoid in the last part of pregnancy.
  • Don’t take two NSAIDs together.

Common questions

Is ibuprofen or paracetamol better for pain?

For period pain, dental pain, sprains and gout, NSAIDs like ibuprofen are usually better. Paracetamol is gentler on the stomach. Together they work better than either alone.

Are anti-inflammatories bad for your kidneys?

They can reduce blood flow to the kidneys, especially with dehydration, older age, kidney disease, or with certain blood pressure medicines and diuretics. Short courses are usually fine for healthy people.

Can I take ibuprofen with asthma?

Most people with asthma can, but in a small number it triggers attacks. If it has ever worsened your breathing, avoid it.

Is anti-inflammatory gel safer than tablets?

Yes. Gels put much less medicine into the bloodstream, so stomach and kidney side effects are much less likely.
