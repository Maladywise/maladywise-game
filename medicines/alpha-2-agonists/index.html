Drug info

Alpha-2 agonists

Alpha-2 agonist Prescription only

Medicines that turn down the body’s adrenaline-style “fight or flight” signals. Born as blood pressure tablets, now better known for ADHD, tics and sleep. Calming, but clingy: they don’t like being dumped suddenly.

At a glance

For
ADHD, tics, blood pressure, menopausal hot flushes, withdrawal symptoms, sedation in hospital
Works
ADHD: a modest benefit, smaller than stimulants (in a key guanfacine trial, about 6 in 10 children responded versus 4 in 10 on a dummy treatment). Hot flushes: in small studies, clonidine cut flushes by 38% versus 24% on a dummy treatment
Cost
Clonidine: on the PBS. Guanfacine: PBS for ADHD in children and teens, with an authority
Kicks in
Drowsiness within hours; ADHD benefit builds over 2–4 weeks
Taken for
Months to years, reviewed regularly
Watch for
Sleepiness, dizziness on standing, low blood pressure, slow pulse, dry mouth

They calm the nervous system, not the breakup. Stop them suddenly and blood pressure can bounce back with interest.

Brand names

  • Clonidine: Catapres, plus generics. Clonidine guide
  • Guanfacine: Intuniv (slow-release tablets). Guanfacine guide
  • Methyldopa: generics
  • Moxonidine: Physiotens
  • Dexmedetomidine: Precedex (hospital only)

How it works

Alpha-2 receptors act as a brake on the release of noradrenaline, the body’s own adrenaline-type messenger. Switching them on slows the heart, lowers blood pressure and has a calming, sedating effect. Guanfacine is more targeted to the type found in the front of the brain, which handles attention and impulse control.

Used for

  • ADHD, alone or added to a stimulant (guanfacine; clonidine off-label in Australia)
  • Trouble getting to sleep with ADHD, usually in children (clonidine, off-label; evidence limited)
  • Tics and Tourette syndrome, especially with ADHD (off-label)
  • High blood pressure (clonidine, moxonidine; methyldopa in pregnancy)
  • Menopausal hot flushes (clonidine)
  • Easing withdrawal from opioids or alcohol, under supervision (clonidine, off-label)
  • Sedation in intensive care and during procedures (dexmedetomidine)

Clonidine and guanfacine do most of the work outside hospital. The others are niche.

Also in the family: lofexidine (opioid withdrawal, approved in the US and UK) and brimonidine eye drops (glaucoma).

Not the same as alpha blockers. Prazosin and tamsulosin block a different receptor (alpha-1) to relax blood vessels or the prostate. Different family, different job.

Side effects

Sleepiness, tiredness, dizziness on standing and a dry mouth are the usual ones. They often ease after the first few weeks.

Get help urgently if

  • Fainting, a very slow pulse or feeling very faint
  • Extreme sleepiness or slowed breathing
  • Chest pain or a racing, irregular heartbeat
  • A child swallows any tablets not meant for them: call 000 or the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26 (Australia)

Good to know

  • Don’t stop suddenly. Blood pressure can rebound, with agitation, headache and a racing heart. Doses are reduced slowly.
  • Alcohol, sleeping tablets and other sedating medicines add to the drowsiness. Take care driving until you know how it affects you.
  • Blood pressure, pulse and, in children, height and weight are checked regularly.
  • Store locked away from children.

Common questions

Is clonidine or guanfacine better for ADHD?

Both help less than stimulants. Guanfacine is the one approved for ADHD in Australia and is taken once a day; clonidine is used off-label, often when sleep is a problem. The choice depends on symptoms, timing, side effects and other conditions.

Can I stop clonidine or guanfacine suddenly?

No. Blood pressure can rebound and cause agitation, headache and a racing heart. Your doctor will reduce the dose slowly.

Are alpha-2 agonists the same as alpha blockers?

No. Alpha blockers such as prazosin and tamsulosin block alpha-1 receptors to relax blood vessels or the prostate. Alpha-2 agonists turn down the brain’s adrenaline-type signals.

Is clonidine a sleeping tablet?

It isn’t approved as one. It’s sometimes used off-label to help children with ADHD fall asleep, but the evidence is limited.
