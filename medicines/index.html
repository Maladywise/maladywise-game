Medicines

Plain-English medicine guides: brand names, how they work, what they’re for and side effects.

  • Blood pressure and heart medicineACE inhibitorsA widely used family of tablets that relax blood vessels. Used for blood pressure, heart failure, after a heart attack and to protect the kidneys.
  • Bone-strengthening medicineAlendronate and bisphosphonatesThe first-line osteoporosis treatment. Weekly tablets or a yearly drip that cut fracture risk. Take tablets exactly right.
  • Urate-lowering therapyAllopurinolThe long-term fix for gout: lowers uric acid so crystals dissolve and attacks stop. Start low, increase until the urate target is reached, and keep taking it.
  • Medicine familyAlpha-2 agonistsClonidine, guanfacine and relatives compared. Calm the body’s “fight or flight” signals; used for ADHD, tics, blood pressure and hot flushes.
  • Tricyclic antidepressantAmitriptylineAn older antidepressant now mostly used in low doses for nerve pain, migraine prevention and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). Dangerous in overdose.
  • Penicillin antibioticAmoxicillin and AugmentinThe most prescribed antibiotic in Australia, from the penicillin family. Augmentin adds clavulanic acid to beat bacteria that would otherwise destroy amoxicillin.
  • Non-steroidal anti-inflammatoryAnti-inflammatories (NSAIDs)Medicines that ease pain, swelling and fever by blocking inflammatory chemicals. Very effective for sprains, period pain and gout, but they can upset the stomach, kidneys and heart.
  • AntibioticsAntibiotics for urine infections (UTI)Short courses of antibiotic clear most uncomplicated bladder infections in women within a few days. Pharmacists in several states can now supply some of them.
  • H1 antihistaminesAntihistaminesBlock histamine, the chemical behind itchy eyes, sneezing and hives. The modern ones barely cause drowsiness; the old ones knock you out.
  • AntiplateletAntiplateletsMedicines that stop platelets sticking together, to prevent heart attacks and strokes in people who’ve already had one or have a stent. No longer recommended for most healthy people.
  • Food supplementApple cider vinegarA social media favourite. Weight loss evidence is weak; it can erode teeth and burn the throat.
  • HerbalAshwagandha (withania)Hugely popular for stress. Small trials look positive, but they’re short and low quality, and liver injury has been reported.
  • Non-stimulant ADHD medicineAtomoxetineA non-stimulant ADHD capsule that works all day. Slower than stimulants, with no peaks, crashes or S8 paperwork.
  • ImmunomodulatorAzathioprine and mercaptopurine (thiopurines)Steroid-sparing tablets that keep IBD in remission, alone or alongside a biologic. Slow to work, and need regular blood tests.
  • HerbalBacopa (brahmi)Small trials suggest a modest memory boost after about 12 weeks. Gut side effects are common.
  • Sedative (benzodiazepine)BenzodiazepinesFast, calming and habit-forming. Useful for days to a couple of weeks; risky as a long-term habit.
  • HerbalBerberineCalled ‘nature’s Ozempic’ online. It modestly lowers blood sugar and cholesterol, but weight loss is small, and it interacts with many medicines.
  • Heart and blood pressure medicineBeta blockersA family of medicines that slow the heart and blunt the effects of adrenaline. Used for heart conditions, blood pressure, migraine and tremor.
  • VitaminBiotinPopular for hair and nails, but only helps if you’re deficient, which is rare. High doses can make blood tests wrong, including heart attack tests.
  • AnticoagulantBlood thinners (anticoagulants)Medicines that slow blood clotting, to prevent strokes in atrial fibrillation and to treat or prevent clots in the legs and lungs. They save lives, at the cost of a higher bleeding risk.
  • Gut-targeted corticosteroidBudesonide for IBDA steroid released in the bowel and largely broken down by the liver, so it calms ileal Crohn’s flares with fewer body-wide side effects than prednisolone.
  • Smoking cessation aidBupropionApproved here for quitting smoking; an antidepressant overseas. Off label for low mood, ADHD and SSRI sexual side effects.
  • Blood pressure medicineCalcium channel blockersSome of the most prescribed blood pressure medicines in the world. They relax artery walls; some also slow the heart. Ankle swelling is the classic side effect.
  • MineralCalcium supplementsFood first. Supplements help when your diet falls short, especially with osteoporosis treatment, but more isn’t better.
  • Cephalosporin antibioticCefalexinA first-generation cephalosporin antibiotic, commonly used for skin infections like cellulitis, and some urine infections, including in pregnancy.
  • Alpha-2 agonistClonidineAn older blood pressure tablet that calms the body’s “fight or flight” signals. Now used more for ADHD, sleep and tics than for blood pressure.
  • SupplementCoenzyme Q10 (CoQ10)Often taken with statins for muscle aches, but trials mostly show no benefit there. There’s some evidence in heart failure and migraine.
  • Anti-inflammatory (gout)ColchicineAn old plant-derived medicine for gout attacks. Low doses work as well as high doses, with far fewer side effects. Overdose is dangerous.
  • PeptideCollagen peptidesThe most popular peptide supplement. Industry-funded trials look good; independent, high-quality trials show no benefit for skin.
  • HerbalCranberry for UTIsCranberry reduces repeat UTIs in women who get them often. It doesn’t treat an infection you already have.
  • Sports supplementCreatineOne of the best-studied supplements. With resistance training it adds strength and muscle, and it’s safe in healthy adults.
  • RANKL antibodyDenosumab (Prolia)A twice-yearly injection that strongly protects bones, but its effect wears off fast. Never just stop it: you need a plan.
  • Stimulant (amfetamine)DexamfetamineA stimulant tablet for ADHD and narcolepsy. A controlled (Schedule 8) medicine with strict prescribing rules.
  • Water tabletDiuretics (water tablets)Medicines that make the kidneys pass more salt and water. Used for blood pressure, heart failure and fluid build-up, and (spironolactone) acne and excess hair.
  • Tetracycline antibioticDoxycyclineA versatile antibiotic used for chest infections, chlamydia, acne, rosacea and malaria prevention. Take it upright with plenty of water, and watch the sun.
  • SNRI antidepressantDuloxetineAn antidepressant that also turns down pain. For depression, anxiety, nerve pain, fibromyalgia and arthritis pain.
  • 5-alpha reductase inhibitor + alpha blockerDuodart (dutasteride and tamsulosin)Two prostate medicines in one capsule: tamsulosin eases flow quickly, dutasteride slowly shrinks the prostate. Best for larger prostates.
  • HerbalEchinaceaMay slightly reduce the chance of catching a cold. Doesn’t reliably shorten one you already have.
  • HerbalElderberrySmall trials suggest it may shorten flu symptoms, but the evidence is low quality. Raw berries are toxic.
  • SSRI antidepressantEscitalopramA widely used antidepressant for depression and anxiety disorders. Taken once a day. Usually well tolerated.
  • HerbalEvening primrose oilLong popular for eczema, breast pain and hot flushes. Good-quality trials show it doesn’t work for any of them.
  • 5-alpha reductase inhibitorFinasterideBlocks the hormone that shrinks hair follicles and enlarges the prostate. Effective for both, with a small risk of sexual and mood side effects.
  • SupplementFish oil and krill oil (omega-3)Australia’s favourite supplement. Great for very high triglycerides at high doses; for most other claims, the big trials disappoint.
  • SSRI antidepressantFluoxetineA long-established antidepressant. Its very long half-life means fewer withdrawal symptoms but slow wash-out. Sometimes used in young people, with specialist guidance.
  • Nerve pain and epilepsy medicineGabapentinA medicine for nerve pain and seizures, closely related to pregabalin. Helps some people with nerve pain, but not most other pain, and it can cause sleepiness, dizziness and dependence.
  • HerbalGarlic supplementsGarlic modestly lowers blood pressure in people with high blood pressure. Effects on cholesterol are small.
  • PeptideGHK-Cu (copper peptide)In skin creams, GHK-Cu is a cosmetic ingredient with small studies behind it. Injectable GHK-Cu is unapproved and the TGA warns against it.
  • HerbalGingerA reasonable option for pregnancy nausea, backed by trials and Australian guidance. Less clear for motion sickness.
  • HerbalGinkgo bilobaSold for memory, but large trials found it doesn’t prevent dementia or improve memory in healthy people.
  • Medicine familyGLP-1 medicinesSemaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide and dulaglutide compared. Gut-hormone copies for type 2 diabetes and weight.
  • GuideGLP-1 medicines guideSide effects in numbers, stopping and weight regain, muscle, pregnancy, surgery, fakes, PBS cost and rare risks.
  • Joint supplementGlucosamine and chondroitinOne of Australia’s best-selling supplements. Large trials show little or no benefit over placebo for most people.
  • HerbalGreen tea extractSold for weight loss, with tiny effects at best. Concentrated extracts can cause liver injury: Australian labels carry a warning.
  • Alpha-2A agonist (non-stimulant)GuanfacineA non-stimulant ADHD medicine for children and teenagers. Not addictive. Can be used alone or added to a stimulant.
  • Antimalarial DMARDHydroxychloroquineA gentle, long-term immune modulator for lupus and arthritis. Safe for most, with yearly eye checks after 5 years.
  • Biologic (TNF blocker)Infliximab and adalimumab (anti-TNF)The first biologics for IBD, and still among the most effective, especially for fistulising Crohn’s. Infliximab is a drip; adalimumab is a self-injection.
  • Iron supplementIron tabletsCheap and effective for iron deficiency, if you take them in a way your gut can absorb and tolerate.
  • Oral retinoidIsotretinoinThe most effective acne treatment, often clearing it for good. Strict pregnancy prevention is essential.
  • HerbalKavaMay ease anxiety short term, but the best recent trial found no benefit. It can damage the liver; Australia limits kava products.
  • Anticonvulsant and mood stabiliserLamotrigineA seizure medicine that also prevents bipolar depression. Easy to take, as long as it’s started slowly.
  • Thyroid hormoneLevothyroxine (thyroxine)Replaces the hormone an underactive thyroid can’t make. One small tablet a day, for life, with a blood test to get the dose right.
  • Opioid receptor blocker, tiny doseLow-dose naltrexone (LDN)Naltrexone at about a tenth of the usual dose, made up by compounding pharmacies. Popular for fibromyalgia, chronic pain and other conditions, with promising small studies but mixed results in bigger trials.
  • SupplementLysine for cold soresPopular for cold sores. Trials are small and inconsistent; antiviral medicines work better.
  • MineralMagnesium supplementsHugely popular for cramps and sleep, where the evidence is weak. It does help constipation, migraine prevention and genuine deficiency.
  • CannabinoidMedicinal cannabisPrescription cannabis products containing CBD, THC or both. Strong evidence for a few rare epilepsies; modest or uncertain benefit for most other uses, and real side effects, especially from THC.
  • Sleep hormoneMelatoninThe body’s ‘darkness’ hormone. Mild help for falling asleep, more useful for body-clock problems like jet lag than for general insomnia.
  • NMDA receptor blockerMemantineA dementia medicine that dampens overactive glutamate signalling. Also used off label, mostly as an add-on for OCD.
  • Oestrogen ± progestogenMenopause hormone therapy (MHT)The most effective treatment for hot flushes and night sweats. For most healthy women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause, benefits outweigh risks.
  • 5-aminosalicylate (5-ASA)Mesalazine and aminosalicylatesThe mainstay for mild to moderate ulcerative colitis, acting directly on the bowel lining. Much less useful in Crohn’s disease.
  • Biguanide (diabetes tablet)MetforminThe usual first tablet for type 2 diabetes. Cheap, well proven, and doesn’t cause hypos or weight gain on its own.
  • Disease-modifying medicine (DMARD)MethotrexateThe cornerstone of rheumatoid arthritis treatment. Taken ONCE A WEEK: daily dosing by mistake can be fatal.
  • Stimulant (methylphenidate)MethylphenidateThe most widely used ADHD medicine, in short- and long-acting forms. A controlled (Schedule 8) medicine with PBS age-at-diagnosis rules.
  • HerbalMilk thistle (silymarin)Sold as a liver ‘detox’. Trials in liver disease show no clear benefit. Its one real hospital use is IV silibinin for death cap poisoning.
  • Hair growth stimulantMinoxidilThe most used hair-loss treatment. Topical foam, or a low-dose tablet that’s become popular. Works only while you keep using it.
  • Antidepressant (NaSSA)MirtazapineAn antidepressant taken at night. Often causes drowsiness and a bigger appetite, which can help some people and be a problem for others.
  • Wakefulness-promoting agentModafinilA tablet that helps people stay awake. Used for excessive daytime sleepiness from narcolepsy, sleep apnoea or shift work.
  • UnapprovedPeptides (BPC-157, TB-500)Injections sold for healing, muscle and anti-ageing. Not approved in Australia, mostly tested only in animals.
  • Leukotriene receptor antagonistMontelukastA daily tablet that blocks leukotrienes, another inflammation chemical in asthma. Handy for some children and exercise asthma, but watch for mood and sleep changes.
  • VitaminMultivitaminsFor most well-fed adults, a multivitamin is expensive urine. It makes sense for pregnancy, restricted diets, and some older adults.
  • Opioid antagonistNaltrexone (for alcohol)A daily tablet that takes the reward out of drinking, so it’s easier to stop at one or not start. Underused and effective.
  • HerbalOlive leaf extractHeavily marketed for immunity. Human evidence is very limited.
  • 5-HT3 antagonist anti-nauseaOndansetronA highly effective anti-nausea medicine, often as a melt-in-the-mouth wafer. Common in emergency departments for gastro in children.
  • Strong pain relieversOpioid pain medicinesStrong pain relievers for severe short-term pain, such as after surgery or a fracture. Poor for long-term pain, and they carry real risks of dependence and overdose.
  • Anticholinergics and beta-3 agonistsOveractive bladder medicinesFor urgency, frequency and leaks from an overactive bladder, after bladder training. Mirabegron avoids the memory concerns of older anticholinergics.
  • Pain and fever relieverParacetamolAustralia’s most-used pain and fever medicine. Gentle on the stomach and safe at the right dose, but one of the commonest causes of serious overdose.
  • Medicine familyPDE5 inhibitorsSildenafil, tadalafil and vardenafil compared. For erectile dysfunction, prostate symptoms and lung blood pressure.
  • HerbalPeppermint oil capsulesOne of the better-proven options for IBS: enteric-coated capsules ease tummy pain and bloating.
  • Alpha-1 blockerPrazosinAn older alpha blocker, now mostly used at night for prostate symptoms, Raynaud’s or trauma nightmares. The first dose can make you faint, so start low at bedtime.
  • Oral corticosteroidPrednisolone and prednisonePowerful anti-inflammatory tablets. Short courses are usually safe; long-term use needs care, tapering and bone protection.
  • Gabapentinoid (nerve pain)PregabalinA nerve-pain capsule that helps a minority a lot. Doesn’t help sciatica. Dizziness, sleepiness and misuse risk.
  • VitaminPregnancy supplements (folic acid and iodine)Two supplements matter most in pregnancy: folic acid (from before you conceive) and iodine. Both are evidence based and cheap.
  • Inhaled corticosteroidPreventer inhalers (inhaled corticosteroids)The puffers that calm airway inflammation, so attacks happen less. Low doses, tiny amounts reaching the body, and the backbone of asthma care.
  • ProbioticProbioticsSome specific strains help prevent antibiotic-related diarrhoea. For general ‘gut health’, the evidence is thin, and results depend on the exact strain.
  • Acid-reducing medicineProton pump inhibitors (PPIs)Tablets that turn down stomach acid. Used for reflux, ulcers and H. pylori. Best at the lowest dose for the shortest time.
  • FibrePsyllium (Metamucil) and fibre supplementsCheap, well studied and useful: helps constipation, IBS and cholesterol. Start low and drink plenty of water.
  • Atypical antipsychoticQuetiapineAn antipsychotic for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. Low doses for sleep or anxiety are not approved and not recommended.
  • Short-acting relieverSalbutamol (blue reliever puffer)The blue puffer. Opens the airways within minutes. Brilliant in an attack, but needing it often is a sign your asthma needs a preventer.
  • Blood pressure medicineSartans (ARBs)Blood pressure medicines that block the hormone angiotensin II. They protect the kidneys and heart, and unlike ACE inhibitors rarely cause a cough.
  • HerbalSaw palmettoPopular for prostate symptoms, but large trials and a Cochrane review found it no better than placebo.
  • MineralSeleniumHelps mild thyroid eye disease. Doesn’t prevent cancer, and too much is toxic.
  • GLP-1 receptor agonistSemaglutideCopies a natural gut hormone that controls blood sugar and appetite. Used for type 2 diabetes and weight management.
  • SSRI antidepressantSertralineA widely used antidepressant for depression, OCD, panic and other anxiety conditions. One of the best-studied in pregnancy and breastfeeding.
  • Medicine familySGLT2 inhibitorsEmpagliflozin, dapagliflozin and relatives compared. Diabetes tablets that also protect the heart and kidneys.
  • HerbalSt John’s wortWorks for mild to moderate depression about as well as antidepressants in trials, but interacts with a huge number of medicines.
  • Cholesterol-lowering medicineStatinsA family of tablets that lower LDL cholesterol and cut heart attacks and strokes. Muscle aches are usually not the statin.
  • Alpha blockerTamsulosinRelaxes the muscle at the prostate and bladder neck, so urine flows more easily. Also helps kidney stones pass.
  • AndrogenTestosterone therapyReplaces testosterone when the testes or pituitary truly can’t make enough. Not a fix for ageing or low energy with normal levels.
  • Combined hormonal contraceptiveThe combined pillOestrogen plus progestogen, taken daily. Over 99% effective if perfect, about 93% in real life. Also helps heavy, painful periods and acne.
  • GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonistTirzepatideCopies two natural gut hormones that control blood sugar and appetite. Used for type 2 diabetes, weight management and sleep apnoea with obesity.
  • HerbalTurmeric and curcuminMay modestly ease osteoarthritis pain. The TGA has warned about rare liver injury from turmeric and curcumin products.
  • JAK inhibitor (small molecule)Upadacitinib and JAK inhibitorsA once-daily tablet that works fast for moderate to severe Crohn’s and colitis, with specific risks that need monitoring.
  • Biologic (interleukin blocker)Ustekinumab and IL-23 biologicsBiologics that block interleukin-23, a key inflammation signal in Crohn’s and colitis. An IV start, then injections every 4 to 8 weeks, with a good safety record.
  • Local oestrogenVaginal oestrogenTiny doses of oestrogen applied to the vagina. Treats dryness and painful sex after menopause and cuts recurrent UTIs, with very little reaching the bloodstream.
  • HerbalValerianA popular sleep herb with inconsistent evidence. Better-designed trials mostly show little or no benefit.
  • Smoking cessation medicineVareniclineThe most effective quit-smoking tablet. Reduces cravings and makes cigarettes less satisfying.
  • Biologic (anti-integrin)VedolizumabA biologic that stops inflammatory cells entering the gut, so it works mainly in the bowel with little effect on the rest of the immune system.
  • SNRI antidepressantVenlafaxine and desvenlafaxine (SNRIs)Antidepressants that boost serotonin and noradrenaline. Effective for depression and anxiety, but stopping suddenly causes notorious withdrawal symptoms.
  • Progestogen for endometriosisVisanne (Dienogest)A daily progestogen tablet for endometriosis pain. As good as hormone shut-down for pain, with fewer side effects.
  • VitaminVitamin B12 supplementsEssential for vegans and people with low B12. High-dose tablets (1,000 mcg) work for most people, even many with absorption problems.
  • VitaminVitamin C supplementsTaking vitamin C every day shortens colds by about a day a year for most adults. Starting it when you’re already sick doesn’t reliably help.
  • Cholecalciferol (vitamin D3)Vitamin D supplementsTreatment doses for a low level are often higher than people expect: Australian guidance suggests 3,000 to 5,000 IU a day for 6 to 12 weeks for moderate deficiency.
  • Medicines comparedWeight-loss medicinesEvery approved weight-loss medicine side by side: trial results, side effects and what happens when you stop.
  • MineralZinc supplementsZinc lozenges started early may shorten a cold a little. Long-term high doses can cause copper deficiency and nerve problems.
  • GnRH agonist implantZoladex (Goserelin)An implant that switches off sex hormones for 1 or 3 months. For prostate and breast cancer, endometriosis and fibroids.
  • Z-drug hypnoticZolpidem and zopiclone (Z-drugs)Fast-acting sleeping tablets for short-term use. Known for complex sleep behaviours like sleepwalking, eating and even driving while asleep.
