Fact sheet

PTSD

PTSD is when the mind stays on high alert after a frightening event: flashbacks, nightmares, avoiding reminders, feeling on edge. It’s common, it’s not a sign of weakness, and trauma-focused therapy works well.

Quick answer: Trauma-focused therapy works best for PTSD: trauma-focused CBT, cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure or EMDR. It usually takes 8–12 weekly sessions, and about 2 in 3 people no longer have PTSD after a course. Antidepressants help when therapy isn’t enough. See your GP if symptoms last more than a month after a trauma.

The PTSD ladder

Strong reactions in the first few weeks are normal and often ease. If they last beyond a month, trauma-focused therapy is the main treatment. Medicines help when therapy isn’t available, isn’t wanted or isn’t enough.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careSupport and watchful waiting (first month)Practical help, people you trust, routine, sleep, and a check-in with your GP if you’re struggling.
    Works
    Not tested as a treatment, but most people’s distress eases over the first weeks without one
    Cost
    Free
    How long
    About 4 weeks, then review if symptoms are still there
    Watch for
    Leaning on alcohol or drugs, or avoiding more and more of life

    You don’t have to tell the story to everyone. Or anyone, yet.

  2. Step 2 · Self-careGP visit and guided online CBTYour GP checks for depression, sleep and alcohol problems, can write a mental health treatment plan, and can prescribe free online programs such as THIS WAY UP or MindSpot.
    Works
    Moderate drop in symptoms vs a waiting list in small trials; low-quality evidence, and works best with clinician support
    Cost
    GP visit often bulk billed or about $40–80 gap; online programs free with a clinician’s referral
    How long
    About 8 weeks for MindSpot; THIS WAY UP is 8 modules over a few months
    Watch for
    Some modules can stir up memories. Not designed for childhood or complex trauma

    Therapy in your pyjamas, with someone checking you’re okay.

  3. Step 3 · Allied healthTrauma-focused therapyThe first-choice treatment: trauma-focused CBT, cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure or EMDR with a trained psychologist or therapist.
    Works
    About 2 in 3 no longer have PTSD after a course, vs about 1 in 8 on a waiting list. Around half in veterans
    Cost
    About $200–300 a session; Medicare pays back about $100–150 for up to 10 sessions a year with a GP mental health treatment plan. Free for eligible veterans through DVA or Open Arms
    How long
    Usually 8–12 weekly sessions, sometimes more
    Watch for
    Feeling worse for a session or two before it gets better. Ask your therapist about their trauma training

    The memory doesn’t vanish. It just stops running the show.

  4. Step 4 · PrescriptionAntidepressant medicine (sertraline, paroxetine, fluoxetine or venlafaxine)When therapy isn’t available, isn’t wanted or isn’t enough, or when depression is also present.
    Works
    About 6 in 10 improve, vs about 3 to 4 in 10 on a dummy treatment
    Cost
    Generics under the $25 PBS co-payment, about $20–25, when PBS-listed for you (such as with depression); for PTSD alone it may be a private script, about $20–40 a month
    How long
    4–8 weeks to work; if it helps, usually continued for at least 12 months
    Watch for
    Nausea, sleep changes, sexual side effects. Under 25s: watch for worsening mood early on. Don’t stop suddenly

    Not a personality transplant. Just turns the alarm down a notch.

  5. Step 5 · SpecialistPsychiatrist or specialist trauma serviceFor severe or complex PTSD, when two treatments haven’t helped, or when there’s risk, psychosis or heavy substance use.
    Works
    Not a trial question: reviews the diagnosis and medicines and coordinates more intensive care
    Cost
    Private psychiatrist about $300–600 for a first visit, with partial Medicare rebates; public mental health services are free
    How long
    Waits can be weeks to months
    Watch for
    Long waitlists. Keep seeing your GP and therapist while you wait

    Join the queue now. Future you will thank you.

  6. Step 6 · SpecialistDay programs and intensive treatmentStructured group programs over several weeks, such as PTSD treatment groups, STAIR (skills for emotions and relationships), CBT or ACT, often at private hospitals.
    Works
    Promising results in studies, but few fair comparisons with standard therapy
    Cost
    Often covered by private hospital cover that includes psychiatric care (check waiting periods and excess). DVA funds eligible veterans
    How long
    Usually 1–2 days a week for several weeks
    Watch for
    You need a referral from a GP or psychiatrist. Check eligibility and your fund cover first

    Group therapy: harder to cancel, easier to keep going.

Costs are rough Australian prices in October 2026 and vary by provider. PBS co-payment: $25, or $7.70 with a concession card. Better Access: up to 10 rebated individual sessions a calendar year with a GP mental health treatment plan. Results are averages from trials, and recovery is rarely a straight line.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Single-session debriefing straight after traumaGoing over the event in detail in one session doesn’t prevent PTSD, and one trial found more PTSD a year later. Practical support and kindness are fine.
  • Benzodiazepines (such as diazepam or alprazolam)Don’t treat PTSD, may raise the chance of developing it after a recent trauma, and can cause dependence. Guidelines advise against them.
  • Medicinal cannabis and CBDA large 2026 review found it no better than a dummy treatment for PTSD, and guidelines advise against it. Expensive, too.
  • MDMA-assisted therapy (for now)Early trials look promising, but US regulators said in 2024 the data weren’t enough. In Australia it averages about $20,000 and very few psychiatrists can offer it. Watch this space.
  • “Stress” and “trauma” supplementsNo good trials in PTSD. The label is calmer than the evidence.

What you can do

  • Keep a routine: regular meals, movement and the same wake-up time, even after a bad night.
  • Stay connected to people you trust. You don’t have to talk about the trauma to feel less alone.
  • Go easy on alcohol and drugs. They numb things tonight and make PTSD worse over time.
  • For a surge of panic: breathe in slowly, then out slowly while saying “calm”, for a few minutes.
  • Supporting someone? Listen, don’t push for details, and help with the practical stuff.

See your GP if

  • Flashbacks, nightmares, avoidance or feeling on edge last more than a month after a trauma
  • Symptoms affect your sleep, work, study or relationships, at any stage
  • You’re using alcohol, drugs or gambling to cope
  • Low mood, anger or guilt won’t lift, or you feel cut off from people
  • Childhood or past trauma is still affecting you now, even years later

If you’re thinking about suicide or are unsafe, call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number. From a mobile, 112 works in most countries. For support any time, call Lifeline on 13 11 14 (24/7). Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people can call 13YARN on 13 92 76 (24/7). Veterans and their families can call Open Arms on 1800 011 046 (24/7). Outside Australia, find a free helpline at findahelpline.com.

Support lines

If you or someone else is in danger right now, call your emergency number. To talk to someone, any time:

  • Australia: Lifeline 13 11 14 (24/7, or text 0477 13 11 14), Suicide Call Back Service 1300 659 467, Beyond Blue 1300 22 4636, 13YARN 13 92 76 for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. Emergency: 000.
  • Young people in Australia: Kids Helpline 1800 55 1800 (24/7, ages 5 to 25) or headspace (ages 12 to 25).
  • New Zealand: call or text 1737. Emergency: 111.
  • UK and Ireland: Samaritans 116 123. Emergency: 999 (or 112).
  • US and Canada: call or text 988. Emergency: 911.
  • Anywhere else: findahelpline.com lists free helplines in more than 100 countries.

Common questions

What are the symptoms of PTSD?

Unwanted memories, flashbacks or nightmares about the event; avoiding reminders; feeling numb, guilty, angry or cut off; and feeling on edge, jumpy or unable to sleep or concentrate. PTSD is considered when these last more than a month and get in the way of life.

Is PTSD only something soldiers get?

No. Anyone can develop PTSD after a frightening event, such as an assault, a car crash, a disaster, a difficult birth or abuse. About 5 to 10 in 100 Australians have PTSD at some point in their lives.

Can PTSD be treated years after the trauma?

Yes. Trauma-focused therapy and medicines can work even when the trauma happened long ago. It is never too late to get help.

Do I have to talk about the trauma in detail?

Trauma-focused therapy does involve working with the memory, because that is what helps it lose its power. You go at a pace agreed with your therapist, who helps you feel safe first. EMDR involves less talking than other therapies.

What types of therapy work for PTSD?

Trauma-focused therapies have the best evidence: trauma-focused CBT, cognitive processing therapy (CPT), prolonged exposure (PE) and EMDR (eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing). They usually take 8 to 12 weekly sessions, and about 2 in 3 people no longer have PTSD after a course. Guided online CBT can help while you wait. Ask a therapist about their trauma training.

Is there PTSD rehab or inpatient treatment?

Yes. Many private psychiatric hospitals run PTSD day programs, and some run inpatient programs, usually over several weeks. You need a referral from a GP or psychiatrist, and private hospital cover that includes psychiatric care; eligible veterans can be funded through DVA. For most people, weekly trauma-focused therapy is the first step.

Helpful resources

  • Phoenix Australia: Your recoveryAustralia’s national centre for posttraumatic mental health. Plain-English info on PTSD and treatments, self-help tips, and guides for family and friends. Free.
  • THIS WAY UP Post-Traumatic Stress Program8-module online CBT course for trauma in adulthood. Free with a referral from your GP or psychologist, who keeps an eye on progress. Australia.
  • MindSpot PTSD CourseFree 8-week online CBT course with optional weekly therapist support. Adults in Australia; good if face-to-face help is hard to reach.
  • Blue Knot HelplineTrauma-specialist phone support for adults affected by childhood trauma or abuse, and the people supporting them. 1300 657 380, 9 am–5 pm, 7 days. Australia.
  • Medicare Mental Health phone serviceFree advice and referral to local mental health services on 1800 595 212 (weekdays). Formerly Head to Health. Not a crisis line. Australia.
