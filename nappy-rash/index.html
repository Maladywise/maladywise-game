Nappy Rash

Red, sore skin under the nappy and a baby who protests at every change. Nappy rash affects more than half of babies by their first birthday. Most clears with simple changes and a thick layer of barrier cream.

Why it happens: wee and poo keep the skin wet, and the nappy rubs it. That irritation is the most common cause. Diarrhoea, antibiotics, fragranced wipes, soaps and detergents can make it worse.

Thrush (candida): a yeast infection that likes skin folds. It often looks bright red in the creases with small spots or pimples around the edges, and may take longer to clear or come back.

Other causes: eczema, psoriasis, seborrhoeic dermatitis, impetigo and a strep infection around the bottom can look like nappy rash. A rash that doesn’t respond to the usual care needs a doctor’s look.

The nappy rash ladder

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Talcum powderNot recommended for nappy rash.
  • Antiseptic creamsNot recommended. They can irritate the skin further.
  • Soap, bubble bath and fragranced productsThey irritate sore skin. Products with plant or nut oils can too.
  • Rubbing the skin dryPat dry instead. Rubbing damages already sore skin.

What you can do

  • Change nappies as soon as they are wet or dirty.
  • Give nappy-free time on a towel when you can.
  • Use water and a soft cloth. If you use wipes, choose fragrance-free and alcohol-free ones (Australian hospital advice is to avoid wipes on a sore bottom).
  • Apply a thick barrier cream at every change.
  • One warm bath a day with a fragrance-free wash is plenty.
  • For dry, itchy skin elsewhere, see our eczema fact sheet.

See your GP if

  • The rash hasn’t improved after a week of good care
  • It is severe, spreading or more painful than usual
  • You see blisters, pus, yellow crusts or bright red, shiny skin around the bottom
  • Your baby has a fever or seems unwell
  • The rash keeps coming back

See a doctor urgently if

  • Your baby has a fever and seems unwell with the rash
  • The rash is spreading quickly, or has blisters or open sores
  • Your baby is in a lot of pain, can’t settle, or isn’t feeding

Call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number if your baby is floppy, very drowsy or hard to wake, struggling to breathe, or has a rash that doesn’t fade when you press a glass on it.

Common questions

How do I get rid of nappy rash fast?

Change nappies often, give nappy-free time, clean gently with water and apply a thick barrier cream at every change. Most irritant nappy rash improves within days. See a GP if it isn’t better in a week.

How can I tell if nappy rash is thrush?

Thrush is often bright red in the skin folds with small red spots around the edges, and doesn’t improve with barrier cream alone. It needs an antifungal cream.

Should I wipe off all the barrier cream at each change?

No. Remove the soiled part and put more on top. Scrubbing it off damages the skin.

Are cloth nappies or disposables better for nappy rash?

Good-quality disposable nappies absorb moisture better. If you use cloth nappies, rinse them well and avoid plastic pants.

How long does nappy rash take to clear?

Mild nappy rash usually improves within 2 to 3 days with frequent changes, nappy-free time and a barrier cream. If it isn’t better after 3 to 4 days, it may be thrush or another rash that needs different treatment.

Does teething cause nappy rash?

There’s no good evidence that teething causes nappy rash. Diarrhoea, which has many causes, can inflame the skin quickly, so extra changes and barrier cream help during bouts of runny poos.

When should I see a doctor about nappy rash?

If there are blisters, pus, open sores or bright red spots spreading beyond the nappy area, your baby has a fever or seems unwell, or the rash isn’t improving after a few days of good care.
