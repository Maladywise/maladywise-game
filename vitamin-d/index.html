Fact sheet

Vitamin D Deficiency

Vitamin D helps your body use calcium to keep bones and muscles strong. Most of it is made in your skin from sunlight. Low levels are common and easy to fix, but extra vitamin D on top of normal levels won’t make you live longer.

The vitamin D ladder

Most people with normal risk get enough from a little sun. People at higher risk, or with low levels, add a daily supplement. Bigger doses are only for proven deficiency.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careA little safe sunIn summer in Australia, a few minutes of mid-morning or mid-afternoon sun on your arms and hands most days. In winter in southern states, time outdoors around midday with some skin uncovered.
    Works
    Enough for most people at normal risk
    Cost
    Free
    How long
    Ongoing, adjusted for season
    Watch for
    Longer sun doesn’t raise vitamin D further, just your skin cancer risk. Use sun protection when the UV index is 3 or above and you’ll be out for more than a few minutes

    You need minutes, not a tan.

  2. Step 2 · Self-careFood sourcesOily fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel), eggs, and foods with added vitamin D such as some milks, spreads and cereals.
    Works
    Helps, but it’s hard to get enough from food alone
    Cost
    Your usual grocery bill
    How long
    Ongoing
    Watch for
    Food alone rarely fixes a true deficiency

    Food is the side dish. Sunshine is the main.

  3. Step 3 · PharmacyDaily vitamin D3 supplementFor mild deficiency (30–49 nmol/L), or year-round if you’re at higher risk and can’t get safe sun: usually 1,000–2,000 IU a day for adults.
    Works
    Brings levels up over about 3 months. Combined with calcium, cuts hip fractures by about 15% in older people, mostly in aged care
    Cost
    About $10–20 for several months
    How long
    Often ongoing for at-risk groups; recheck after 3 months if you were low
    Watch for
    More isn’t better. Don’t exceed 4,000 IU (100 micrograms) a day unless a doctor advises it

    Daily and boring beats big and occasional.

  4. Step 4 · Doctor-guidedHigher doses for moderate or severe deficiencyFor levels under 30 nmol/L: usually 3,000–5,000 IU a day for adults for 6–12 weeks, then a maintenance dose.
    Works
    Corrects deficiency reliably; bone pain and muscle weakness from deficiency improve over weeks to months
    Cost
    About $10–30
    How long
    Weeks, then a lower ongoing dose and a recheck at about 3 months
    Watch for
    Children, pregnancy and breastfeeding have their own doses: follow your doctor’s plan

    A fix with a finish line, then a maintenance plan.

  5. Step 5 · SpecialistLooking for a hidden causeWhen levels stay low despite supplements, or with bone softening, rickets or abnormal calcium. Causes include coeliac disease, bowel disease, weight-loss surgery, kidney or liver disease and some medicines.
    Works
    Treating the cause often fixes the vitamin D too
    Cost
    Public is free (with waits); private specialist fees vary, with Medicare rebates on referral
    How long
    Depends on the cause
    Watch for
    Some seizure medicines and rifampicin lower vitamin D

    If topping up doesn’t fill the tank, look for the leak.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • High doses for everyone to prevent cancer, heart disease or early deathAn Australian trial of 21,315 people aged 60 and over found monthly vitamin D didn’t help them live longer. Good for bones when low; not a longevity pill.
  • Vitamin D to stop colds and fluA 2025 analysis of 49 trials with over 60,000 people found no clear protection against chest and throat infections.
  • Once-a-year mega-dosesOne large trial found a single big yearly dose increased falls and fractures in older women. Regular modest doses are safer.
  • Routine testing in healthy peopleNot recommended. In Australia, Medicare covers the test only for people at higher risk.
  • Vitamin D injections and IV dripsTablets or liquid work for nearly everyone and are far cheaper.

What you can do

  • Get outdoors in daylight most days, with sensible sun protection when the UV index is 3 or above.
  • If you’re at higher risk (see below), talk to your GP about a daily supplement rather than waiting for a test.
  • Take vitamin D with a meal that has some fat in it. It’s fat-soluble.
  • Look after the rest of your bone health: calcium-rich food and strength exercise. See our osteoporosis sheet.
  • Outside Australia, check local advice. In the UK, everyone is advised to consider 10 micrograms (400 IU) a day in autumn and winter.

See your GP if

  • You’re at higher risk: dark skin, little time outdoors (housebound, in residential care, night shift), covering most of your skin outdoors, a body mass index over 30, or conditions that affect absorption such as coeliac or bowel disease
  • You have bone pain, muscle weakness (such as trouble getting out of a chair) or have broken a bone from a minor fall
  • You’re pregnant or breastfeeding with risk factors
  • Your baby is exclusively breastfed and you have risk factors or low vitamin D yourself

See a doctor the same day if you take high-dose vitamin D and develop severe thirst, frequent urination, vomiting, confusion or weakness: signs of too much calcium. Call 000 (or your local emergency number) for a baby or child with a seizure or muscle spasms, which can happen with very low calcium.

Common questions

What level counts as low vitamin D?

Australian guidance: 50 nmol/L or more is adequate. 30–49 is mild deficiency, 12.5–29 moderate, and under 12.5 severe. Levels may be best kept at 75 or more with osteoporosis. Your level is often a little lower at the end of winter.

What are the symptoms of low vitamin D?

Usually none. Severe or long-lasting deficiency can cause bone pain and tenderness, muscle weakness and aches, and bone softening (osteomalacia in adults, rickets in children, with bowed legs and delayed growth).

Who is at risk of low vitamin D?

People with dark skin, those who get little sun (indoor or night-shift workers, people who are housebound or in residential care, or who cover their skin), people with a higher body weight, people with coeliac disease, bowel disease, kidney or liver disease, or after weight-loss surgery, and exclusively breastfed babies of mothers with low vitamin D.

Should everyone take a vitamin D supplement?

Not in sunny countries if you get regular safe sun. People at higher risk usually do best with a daily supplement. In the UK, where sunlight is weak from October to March, everyone is advised to consider one in autumn and winter.

Can you have too much vitamin D?

Not from the sun, but yes from supplements. Too much raises calcium, which can harm the kidneys, heart and bones. Adults shouldn’t exceed 4,000 IU (100 micrograms) a day unless a doctor advises it. Blood levels above 125 nmol/L are best avoided.

Do babies need vitamin D drops?

In Australia, breastfed babies at higher risk (for example, if their mother has low vitamin D or dark skin) are usually given 400 IU a day for the first year. In the UK, all breastfed babies are advised to have 8.5–10 micrograms a day. Formula already contains vitamin D.

How much sun do I need for vitamin D?

In summer, a few minutes of sun on the arms and hands mid-morning or mid-afternoon on most days is enough for most people. In winter in southern Australia, about 2 to 3 hours of midday sun spread over the week may be needed. People with darker skin need more.

When is the best time to take vitamin D?

Any time of day suits, as long as it’s regular. Taking it with a meal that contains some fat helps absorption. Weekly or monthly doses work too, if your doctor recommends them.
