  1. #15 · Monday 9 November 2026 Easy

    Patient: Daniel, 44, male

    Severe upper abdominal pain boring through to his back since last night, with vomiting. It was a big weekend on the drink.

    Key results: Lipase 2380 U/L (H), ref <60; GGT 140 U/L (H), ref <50

    Diagnosis: Acute pancreatitis

    Why: Lipase more than three times the upper limit plus typical pain (upper abdomen, boring through to the back, with vomiting) makes acute pancreatitis. Normal bilirubin and ALT make a gallstone a bit less likely; a raised GGT after a heavy weekend points to alcohol, the other big cause.

    Teaching point: Gallstones and alcohol cause most acute pancreatitis. How high the lipase goes doesn't predict how sick someone will get; a high urea, low calcium and rising CRP matter more.

  2. #14 · Sunday 8 November 2026 Genius

    Patient: Norma, 81, female

    Feels well. Routine bloods for her blood pressure tablets, taken at home by a new collector. Her ECG is normal.

    Key results: Potassium 7.9 mmol/L (H), ref 3.5–5.2; Calcium 0.61 mmol/L (L), ref 2.10–2.60; Magnesium 0.32 mmol/L (L), ref 0.70–1.10; ALP 14 U/L (L), ref 30–110

    Diagnosis: EDTA contamination of the sample

    Why: A dangerous potassium plus a calcium that would normally cause spasms and seizures, in a well woman with a normal ECG, doesn't add up. Purple-top (FBC) tubes contain potassium EDTA, which adds potassium and grabs calcium, magnesium and zinc. ALP needs zinc and magnesium to work, so it reads falsely low too. The usual culprit is the wrong order of draw, or pouring blood between tubes. A haemolysed sample raises potassium but doesn't crash the calcium.

    Teaching point: Spurious hyperkalaemia has several causes: haemolysis, EDTA contamination, delays in processing, fist clenching, and very high white cell or platelet counts. If the numbers and the patient don't match, check the ECG and get a careful, prompt recollection.

  3. #13 · Saturday 7 November 2026 Hard

    Patient: Mariam, 34, female

    Months of fatigue, weight loss, salt cravings and dizziness on standing. Her skin creases and gums have darkened. Has Hashimoto's, on thyroxine.

    Key results: Sodium 127 mmol/L (L), ref 135–145; Potassium 5.9 mmol/L (H), ref 3.5–5.2; Cortisol (9 am) 48 nmol/L (L), ref 140–550

    Diagnosis: Addison's disease (primary adrenal insufficiency)

    Why: Low sodium with high potassium suggests missing aldosterone, and a very low 9 am cortisol confirms adrenal insufficiency. Raised eosinophils and a dry-looking urea fit. Darker skin creases and gums mean ACTH is very high (it shares a precursor with melanocyte-stimulating hormone), so the adrenals themselves have failed: primary adrenal insufficiency, usually autoimmune, and she already has one autoimmune condition. In pituitary failure, aldosterone is spared, potassium stays normal and the skin is pale.

    Teaching point: An adrenal crisis can be fatal and can be triggered by infection, surgery or vomiting. People with Addison's carry steroid emergency cards and learn 'sick-day rules'.

  4. #12 · Friday 6 November 2026 Hard

    Patient: Eleni, 27, female

    Planning a pregnancy, so her GP ordered 'pre-pregnancy bloods'. Feels well. Her family is from Cyprus, and a cousin has 'thin blood'.

    Key results: Red cell count 5.9 ×10¹²/L (H), ref 3.8–5.8; MCV 63 fL (L), ref 80–100; Ferritin 74 µg/L, ref 30–200; HbA2 5.4 % (H), ref 2.0–3.3

    Diagnosis: Beta thalassaemia trait

    Why: Very small cells (MCV 63) but a high red cell count and only mild anaemia. MCV ÷ red cell count = 10.7; a Mentzer index under 13 favours thalassaemia over iron deficiency, and the normal ferritin and RDW agree. A raised HbA2 (above about 3.5%) is the signature of beta thalassaemia trait; alpha thalassaemia trait usually has a normal HbA2.

    Teaching point: Thalassaemia trait matters most before pregnancy: if both partners carry a trait, their baby could have a serious thalassaemia, which is why partner testing is offered. Iron tablets won't fix thalassaemia's small cells.

  5. #11 · Thursday 5 November 2026 Medium

    Patient: Mele, 66, female

    Type 2 diabetes. Woke with nausea, jaw ache and breathlessness. No chest pain, and she thinks it's reflux. ECG shows no ST elevation.

    Key results: hs-Troponin T (0 h) 38 ng/L (H), ref <14; hs-Troponin T (3 h) 214 ng/L (H), ref <14

    Diagnosis: NSTEMI (heart attack)

    Why: A troponin climbing from 38 to 214 ng/L in three hours means acute heart muscle injury. With this story, that's a heart attack until proven otherwise, and without ST elevation it's an NSTEMI. Unstable angina, by definition, has no troponin rise. Normal kidney function, D-dimer and lipase make kidney-related troponin, PE and pancreatitis unlikely.

    Teaching point: Women, older people and people with diabetes are more likely to have heart attacks without classic chest pain: nausea, jaw or back ache, breathlessness or exhaustion. Women's heart attacks are still more likely to be missed or treated late.

  6. #10 · Wednesday 4 November 2026 Medium

    Patient: Liam, 48, male

    Aching knuckles (index and middle fingers), tired, and a low libido. Has two beers most nights. His dad had 'liver trouble'.

    Key results: Transferrin saturation 86 % (H), ref 15–45; Ferritin 1480 µg/L (H), ref 30–500; ALT 74 U/L (H), ref <40

    Diagnosis: Hereditary haemochromatosis

    Why: Ferritin rises in lots of conditions (inflammation, alcohol, fatty liver). The tell is transferrin saturation: at 86% the iron transport protein is overloaded, the hallmark of true iron overload. With arthritis in the index and middle knuckles, abnormal liver tests, a creeping fasting glucose, low libido and a family history, hereditary haemochromatosis (usually HFE C282Y) leads the list. Two beers a night doesn't push saturation to 86%, and the CRP is normal.

    Teaching point: Haemochromatosis is one of the commonest genetic conditions in people of northern European ancestry. Men tend to show it earlier than women, as periods and pregnancy offload iron. Found early, complications like cirrhosis are largely preventable.

  7. #9 · Tuesday 3 November 2026 Easy

    Patient: Grace, 41, female

    Ran her first marathon yesterday. Drank at every water station ‘to be safe’ and kept drinking afterwards. Now headache, nausea and puffy fingers, and she weighs 1.5 kg more than before the race.

    Key results: Sodium 124 mmol/L (L), ref 135–145; Osmolality 258 mOsm/kg (L), ref 275–295

    Diagnosis: Exercise-associated hyponatraemia

    Why: A sodium of 124 with a low measured osmolality is true, dilutional hyponatraemia. Gaining weight over a race means she took in more water than she sweated and passed, and race-day stress hormones (ADH) stop the kidneys clearing the excess. Dehydration would push sodium and urea up, and pseudohyponatraemia has a normal osmolality. A CK of 620 is typical after a marathon, nowhere near rhabdomyolysis territory.

    Teaching point: Exercise-associated hyponatraemia is mostly about drinking too much, not losing salt, and slower runners who stop at every water station are most at risk. Sports medicine consensus statements now say to drink to thirst. Confusion or a seizure after a race is an emergency.

  8. #8 · Monday 2 November 2026 Easy

    Patient: Siobhan, 19, female

    A week of severe sore throat, fevers and exhaustion, with swollen glands in her neck. Just started uni.

    Key results: Lymphocytes 9.2 ×10⁹/L (H), ref 1.0–4.0; Blood film Many atypical (reactive) lymphocytes (!); Heterophile antibody (Monospot) Positive (!), ref Negative

    Diagnosis: Glandular fever (EBV)

    Why: Lots of lymphocytes, many of them atypical (reactive), mildly raised liver enzymes and a positive heterophile antibody test, in a young adult with fever, sore throat and big neck glands. That's glandular fever (infectious mononucleosis), caused by Epstein–Barr virus. Bacterial tonsillitis usually raises neutrophils, not lymphocytes.

    Teaching point: The spleen often swells in glandular fever, which is why contact sport is usually off for a few weeks. The Monospot can be negative early on and in young children; EBV serology helps then.

  9. #7 · Sunday 1 November 2026 Genius

    Patient: Desmond, 70, male

    Back pain for three months, tired, and his third chest infection this winter. The lab has flagged his sodium.

    Key results: Osmolality 289 mOsm/kg, ref 275–295; Globulin 84 g/L (H), ref 25–45; Corrected calcium 2.94 mmol/L (H), ref 2.10–2.60

    Diagnosis: Multiple myeloma (pseudohyponatraemia)

    Why: A low sodium with a normal measured osmolality is pseudohyponatraemia: a huge load of protein takes up plasma volume, and many lab methods then under-read the sodium. The extra protein is globulin, 84 g/L. Add anaemia, high calcium, kidney impairment, bone pain, recurrent infections and an ESR over 100, and this is myeloma until proven otherwise. Sneaky bonus clue: the anion gap is only 6 (128 − 98 − 24), because positively charged paraprotein fills the gap.

    Teaching point: CRAB: high Calcium, Renal impairment, Anaemia, Bone lesions. A big gap between total protein and albumin should prompt serum protein electrophoresis and free light chains.

  1. #6 · Saturday 31 October 2026 Hard

    Patient: Yusuf, 24, male

    Dark, cola-coloured urine and exhaustion, two days after a big feed of fresh broad beans (fava beans). Mild yellow tinge to his eyes.

    Key results: Haptoglobin <0.1 g/L (L), ref 0.3–2.0; Direct antiglobulin test Negative , ref Negative; Blood film Bite and blister cells (!)

    Diagnosis: G6PD deficiency

    Why: Haemolysis everywhere: falling Hb, lots of young red cells (retics), unconjugated bilirubin and LDH up, and haptoglobin used up mopping free haemoglobin, which also darkens the urine. A negative DAT argues against autoimmune haemolysis, and the liver enzymes are normal. Bite and blister cells mean oxidative damage, and fava beans are the classic trigger in G6PD deficiency.

    Teaching point: G6PD deficiency is X-linked and common in people with African, Mediterranean, Middle Eastern and Asian ancestry. The G6PD level can look falsely normal during an attack because the oldest, most deficient cells have already burst, so it's re-tested a few months later.

  2. #5 · Friday 30 October 2026 Hard

    Patient: Helen, 58, female

    Breast cancer treated six years ago. Two weeks of thirst, constipation and new back pain. Her family say she's 'foggy'.

    Key results: Corrected calcium 3.48 mmol/L (H), ref 2.10–2.60; PTH 0.5 pmol/L (L), ref 1.6–6.9; ALP 236 U/L (H), ref 30–110

    Diagnosis: Hypercalcaemia of malignancy

    Why: Corrected calcium is 3.48 mmol/L, high enough to cause thirst, constipation, confusion and kidney injury. PTH is appropriately switched off, which rules out primary hyperparathyroidism (where PTH is high or inappropriately normal). With previous breast cancer, new back pain and a raised ALP from busy bone, the likely driver is cancer: bone metastases, PTH-related peptide, or both.

    Teaching point: 'Stones, bones, abdominal groans and psychic moans.' The first question with any high calcium is: what's the PTH? High or normal points to the parathyroids. Suppressed sends you looking elsewhere, especially for cancer.

  3. #4 · Thursday 29 October 2026 Medium

    Patient: Rangi, 61, male

    Brief weakness of his right arm last week, fully resolved. Ruddy face, and itchy after hot showers. Never smoked.

    Key results: Haematocrit 0.59 L/L (H), ref 0.40–0.54; Platelets 640 ×10⁹/L (H), ref 150–400; Erythropoietin 1.4 IU/L (L), ref 4.0–24.0

    Diagnosis: Polycythaemia vera

    Why: All three cell lines are up, the red cell mass is high and erythropoietin (EPO) is suppressed: the marrow is making red cells on its own, without being asked. That's a myeloproliferative neoplasm, and with this red cell picture it's polycythaemia vera. Low ferritin and small cells are common in PV because the expanding red cell mass soaks up the iron. Itch after hot water (aquagenic pruritus) is a classic clue.

    Teaching point: Thick blood clots. Polycythaemia vera raises the risk of stroke, TIA and other clots, so a high haematocrit is worth noticing in anyone with a vascular event. Almost everyone with PV carries a JAK2 mutation, which helps confirm it.

  4. #3 · Wednesday 28 October 2026 Medium

    Patient: Grace, 64, female

    Pins and needles in both feet, unsteady in the dark, and a sore, smooth tongue. Has vitiligo.

    Key results: MCV 114 fL (H), ref 80–100; Vitamin B12 92 pmol/L (L), ref 150–700; Intrinsic factor antibodies Positive (!), ref Negative

    Diagnosis: Pernicious anaemia (B12 deficiency)

    Why: Macrocytic anaemia with low white cells and platelets, a low B12 and a normal folate. Hypersegmented neutrophils mean megaloblastic change. The high LDH and bilirubin come from red cells dying in the marrow before they're released (ineffective erythropoiesis), not from true haemolysis. Intrinsic factor antibodies confirm pernicious anaemia, an autoimmune condition that loves company: vitiligo, thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes.

    Teaching point: B12 deficiency can damage nerves before anaemia even appears, and the damage can become permanent if missed. Folate alone can fix the blood count while the nerve damage carries on, which is why B12 is always checked alongside it.

  5. #2 · Tuesday 27 October 2026 Easy

    Patient: Leilani, 34, female

    Has Down syndrome and lives in a shared house. Her occupational therapist notices her morning routine (showering, dressing, making breakfast) now takes twice as long. She feels the cold, is constipated and has gained weight.

    Key results: TSH 38.5 mU/L (H), ref 0.40–4.00; Free T4 6.2 pmol/L (L), ref 10.0–20.0; Thyroid peroxidase antibodies 460 IU/mL (H), ref <35

    Diagnosis: Primary hypothyroidism (Hashimoto's)

    Why: TSH is sky-high because the pituitary is shouting at a thyroid that isn't answering, and free T4 is low. That's primary hypothyroidism. Positive thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies point to Hashimoto's, the autoimmune cause. Raised cholesterol and a mildly raised CK are common knock-on effects that usually improve once thyroid levels are back to normal.

    Teaching point: People with Down syndrome have a much higher risk of thyroid disease, so regular thyroid checks are recommended throughout life. A drop in everyday function shouldn't be put down to 'just Down syndrome': OTs and support workers are often first to notice the change.

  6. #1 · Monday 26 October 2026 Easy

    Patient: Amara, 31, female

    Tired for months and puffed on the stairs. Her periods have been heavy since she stopped the pill.

    Key results: MCV 72 fL (L), ref 80–100; Ferritin 6 µg/L (L), ref 30–200; Transferrin saturation 4 % (L), ref 15–45

    Diagnosis: Iron deficiency anaemia

    Why: Small, pale red cells (microcytic, hypochromic) with a rock-bottom ferritin. A ferritin this low means the iron stores are empty, and nothing else does that. Transferrin rises as the body goes hunting for iron, while saturation falls. A raised RDW (mixed cell sizes) and a mildly high platelet count are classic sidekicks.

    Teaching point: Heavy periods are the commonest cause of iron deficiency in premenopausal women, but diet, coeliac disease and gut blood loss are always worth considering. In men and post-menopausal women, iron deficiency means looking for a gut cause.
