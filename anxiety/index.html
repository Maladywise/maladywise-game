Fact sheet

Anxiety

Worry, fear or panic that’s out of proportion, hard to switch off and getting in the way of life. Anxiety disorders are the most common mental health condition in Australia, affecting about 1 in 6 people aged 16–85 each year. They respond well to treatment.

If you’re thinking about suicide or don’t feel safe, call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number now. From a mobile, 112 works in most countries. For support any time, call Lifeline on 13 11 14 or Beyond Blue on 1300 22 4636 (both 24/7). Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people can call 13YARN on 13 92 76 (24/7). Young people aged 5–25 can call Kids Helpline on 1800 55 1800 (24/7). Outside Australia, find a free helpline at findahelpline.com. Feelings this heavy can and do ease with support. You don’t have to wait until things are worse to reach out.

Take a free anxiety self-check (GAD-7): 7 questions, private, about 2 minutes.

The anxiety ladder

Start with self-care and online CBT. If that isn’t enough, CBT with a therapist and medicine both work, with no clear winner, so choose what suits you. Many people use both.

    Works
    Low-risk and helpful for many, but tested mostly in small trials, so not a stand-alone fix for a diagnosed anxiety disorder
    Cost
    Free
    How long
    Ongoing
    Watch for
    Avoidance feels great today and makes anxiety bigger next month.

    Cost
    GP visit often bulk billed or about $40–80 gap; MindSpot free; THIS WAY UP Generalised Anxiety Program $59 to self-enrol, or free with a clinician’s prescription
    How long
    THIS WAY UP: 6 lessons. MindSpot Wellbeing Course: 8 weeks
    Watch for

    Homework you can do in bed. Admittedly, sitting up helps.

  3. Step 3 · Allied healthCognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) with a therapistWith a psychologist or other trained therapist, including gradual exposure for panic, phobias and social anxiety.
    Works
    In generalised anxiety, about 46 in 100 improved after CBT, vs about 14 in 100 on a waiting list or usual care
    Cost
    About $200–300 a session; Medicare pays back about $100–150 for up to 10 sessions a year with a GP mental health treatment plan
    How long
    Usually weekly for a few months
    Watch for

    Like learning to swim: you get in the pool, shallow end first.

  4. Step 4 · PrescriptionAntidepressant medicine (such as sertraline or escitalopram)SSRIs, or related medicines like venlafaxine, are the first-choice tablets for ongoing anxiety. Taken daily, not as needed.
    Works
    In generalised anxiety, people were about 40% more likely to improve than on a dummy tablet (2025 review of 37 trials)
    Cost
    Most generics under the $25 PBS co-payment ($7.70 concession), when PBS-listed for your condition
    How long
    Often a few weeks to start helping. Usually continued for months after you feel well, then reduced slowly with your GP
    Watch for
    Anxiety can feel a little worse in the first week or two. Nausea, sleep changes, sexual side effects. Under 25s: check in often early on. Don’t stop suddenly.

    Turns the smoke alarm down so it stops going off for toast.

  5. Step 5 · SpecialistPsychiatrist or specialist anxiety serviceFor severe anxiety, when two treatments haven’t helped, or when there’s risk, heavy alcohol or drug use, or other conditions in the mix.
    Works
    Not a trial question: reviews the diagnosis and medicines and coordinates more intensive care
    Cost
    Private psychiatrist about $300–600 for a first visit, with partial Medicare rebates; public services are free
    How long
    Waits can be weeks to months
    Watch for
    Keep seeing your GP and therapist while you wait.

    Bringing in a specialist isn’t a failure. It’s calling in the cavalry.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Benzodiazepines (such as diazepam or alprazolam) for ongoing anxietyCalm things fast but bring tolerance and dependence. UK guidelines advise against them for generalised anxiety, except briefly in a crisis.
  • Medicinal cannabis and CBDA 2026 review of 54 trials found no evidence it helps anxiety. Expensive, and may make things worse.
  • KavaIn a 16-week trial in generalised anxiety, kava was no better than a dummy tablet and caused more liver test changes.
  • “Calm” and “stress” supplement blendsFew have been tested in good trials for anxiety disorders. The packaging is more relaxed than the evidence.

What you can do

  • When anxiety spikes, breathe in slowly, then breathe out even more slowly, for a few minutes.
  • Try a daily “worry time”: park worries in a notebook and come back to them at a set time.
  • Keep moving. Regular exercise burns off some of the adrenaline.
  • Cut back on caffeine, energy drinks and alcohol.
  • Notice what you’re avoiding, then take small, regular steps back towards it.
  • Protect your sleep. See our trouble sleeping page.

See your GP if

  • Worry, fear or panic on most days for several weeks, and it’s getting in the way of life
  • Panic attacks, or avoiding places or people because of them
  • You’re using alcohol, drugs or gambling to cope
  • Low mood as well as anxiety (see our depression page)
  • Physical symptoms you’re unsure about, such as palpitations or tummy upsets

Call 000 or your local emergency number for chest pain, trouble breathing or fainting that’s new, different or not settling. Panic can feel like a heart attack, so if you’re not sure, get checked.

Support lines

If you or someone else is in danger right now, call your emergency number. To talk to someone, any time:

  • Australia: Lifeline 13 11 14 (24/7, or text 0477 13 11 14), Suicide Call Back Service 1300 659 467, Beyond Blue 1300 22 4636, 13YARN 13 92 76 for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. Emergency: 000.
  • Young people in Australia: Kids Helpline 1800 55 1800 (24/7, ages 5 to 25) or headspace (ages 12 to 25).
  • New Zealand: call or text 1737. Emergency: 111.
  • UK and Ireland: Samaritans 116 123. Emergency: 999 (or 112).
  • US and Canada: call or text 988. Emergency: 911.
  • Anywhere else: findahelpline.com lists free helplines in more than 100 countries.

Common questions

What are the symptoms of anxiety?

Constant or excessive worry, feeling restless or on edge, trouble sleeping or concentrating, irritability and muscle tension. The body joins in: racing heart, sweating, shaking, breathlessness, dizziness or an upset stomach. In a panic attack these come on suddenly and peak within minutes.

What are the main types of anxiety?

Generalised anxiety (worrying about lots of things, most days), panic disorder (repeated panic attacks and fear of the next one), social anxiety (intense fear of being judged), specific phobias, and health anxiety. Many people have more than one, often alongside depression.

What causes anxiety?

Usually a mix: family history, personality, stressful or traumatic events, ongoing stress, physical illness, and alcohol, caffeine or drug use. Some medicines and conditions such as an overactive thyroid can cause similar symptoms.

How is anxiety diagnosed?

By talking with your GP about your symptoms, how long they’ve lasted and how they affect you, often with a short questionnaire. Your GP may examine you or order tests to rule out physical causes.

Can I just take something when I feel anxious?

Fast-acting sedatives such as benzodiazepines can cause dependence and don’t fix the problem, so they’re only for short crises. Daily medicines and skills like CBT work better over time.

Can anxiety cause chest pain?

Yes. Anxiety and panic can cause chest tightness, a racing heart and breathlessness. But don’t assume chest pain is anxiety: call 000 for new chest pain, pain with exertion, sweating, or pain spreading to the arm, neck or jaw.

How do I calm anxiety fast?

Slow breathing helps, with the out-breath longer than the in-breath (for example in for 4, out for 6). Grounding, such as naming 5 things you can see and 4 you can hear, can help too. A panic attack usually peaks within about 10 minutes and then eases.

Can anxiety go away?

Yes, anxiety is very treatable. Around half of people improve a lot with cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT), and many others improve partly. Symptoms can come back under stress, but the skills learned in therapy tend to keep working.
