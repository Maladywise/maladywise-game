Tiredwise protocol: what to try

For the patient with overlapping ME/CFS, POTS, mast cell symptoms, fibromyalgia, FND, chronic pain and neurodivergence, who has fallen through the gaps. Proven options first, then the evolving ones, each graded so you and your patient can decide together.

Before you start

Work through the red flags and criteria in the Tiredwise workup first. Overlap labels come after serious causes are excluded. Off-label use needs informed consent, documented reasoning and a stop rule.

How to use it

  1. Name the phenotypes. Most patients fit 2–4 modules below. Untick the ones that don’t apply.
  2. Foundations first. Pacing, sleep, iron, orthostatic basics and a medicine review help everyone.
  3. One change at a time. Start low, go slow: this group is often drug-sensitive. Change one thing every 2–4 weeks.
  4. Measure it. Pick 2–3 targets (e.g. hours upright, crash days per week, standing heart rate, 0–10 symptom score). Judge each trial at 4–8 weeks, then keep it or stop it.
  5. Review the whole list every 3–6 months. Stop what isn’t clearly helping. Recovery is often slow and uneven.

Evidence grades

  • A Strong: several good trials or a strong guideline
  • B Moderate: at least one decent RCT, or a guideline recommendation
  • C Emerging: small or crossover trials, cohorts, plausible mechanism
  • D Experimental: expert practice or case reports only
  • Avoid Tested and failed, or harm outweighs benefit

Doses are usual adult starting and target ranges for an Australian GP; check product information, interactions, pregnancy and kidney function. Off-label = not TGA-approved for this use. Specialist = start with or after specialist input.

Show modules

🧱 Foundations: everyone11

Start here for every overlap patient, before or alongside any medicine. Most of the gains come from this layer.

  • BPacing inside the energy envelope

    Plan the day under the crash threshold. Break tasks up, rest before you need to, and count thinking, talking and sensory load as effort too.

    Push–crash cycles keep people stuck. Expect a plateau before improvement.

    [1]

  • AvoidNo graded exercise therapy when PEM is present

    Fixed, incremental exercise targets regardless of symptoms. Use symptom-led activity instead.

    Harm reported by patients; removed from NICE 2021.

    [1]

  • DHeart-rate-guided pacing

    A wearable alarm set roughly 15 beats above resting heart rate, or about 55% of (220 − age), as a rough guide to the crash zone.

    An estimate only. Beta-blockers and ivabradine blunt heart rate, so adjust or rely on symptoms.

  • DRest lying down

    Recline or lie flat for rest breaks, especially with orthostatic symptoms. Sitting is not full rest.

  • BIron if ferritin is under 50 µg/L

    Oral iron for 12 weeks, alternate days if gut upset (e.g. 60–100 mg elemental). IV iron if not tolerated or not absorbed.

    Fatigue improved in non-anaemic women with low ferritin. Recheck ferritin; stop when replete.

    [2]

  • AScreen sleep properly

    STOP-Bang and sleep study when apnoea is possible; CBT for insomnia, adapted to energy limits; fixed wake time.

    Sleep apnoea and insomnia are treatable drivers hiding in plain sight.

  • BMedicine and substance review

    Look for sedatives, anticholinergics, opioids, gabapentinoids, beta-blockers, alcohol and cannabis that add fatigue or brain fog.

    Deprescribing one item can beat adding three.

  • ATreat mood and anxiety when present

    Depression and anxiety are common alongside, not instead of, these conditions. Treat them on their own merits.

    Don’t let a mood diagnosis end the search for physical drivers.

  • DSmall, frequent meals

    Smaller meals, fewer large carbohydrate loads, especially before standing tasks.

    Big meals pool blood in the gut and worsen orthostatic symptoms.

  • BPractical supports

    Work or study adjustments, disability and carer supports, written plans, and a named clinician to coordinate.

    Function and income protection matter as much as any drug.

  • CProtect against re-infection

    Up-to-date vaccines; plan for the next infection (rest early, avoid pushing through).

    Infections commonly trigger relapses.

🔋 ME/CFS and post-exertional malaise9

Delayed crash 12–72 hours after effort, unrefreshing sleep, brain fog, for 6 months or more (IOM 2015). Check with the Tiredwise workup tool.

  • BPacing and activity diary

    Track activity, symptoms and crashes for 2–4 weeks to find the envelope. Reduce to a stable baseline before any increase.

    [1]

  • CCoQ10 200 mg + NADH 20 mg daily

    Trial for 8–12 weeks.

    Two Spanish RCTs showed small fatigue gains. Cost adds up; stop if no change.

    [3]

  • CLow-dose naltrexone (LDN)Off-label

    Compounded. Start 0.5–1.5 mg at night, increase by 0.5–1.5 mg every 1–2 weeks to 3–4.5 mg. Morning dosing if vivid dreams.

    No opioids at the same time. Early insomnia or headache. Evidence is mostly observational; placebo-controlled trials are under way.

    [4]

  • CLow-dose aripiprazoleOff-label

    0.25–2 mg daily, usually as a compounded liquid or capsule. Start at 0.25–0.5 mg.

    About 3 in 4 reported benefit in a retrospective series, with no control group. Watch for akathisia, insomnia, weight gain and impulse-control changes.

    [5]

  • CPyridostigmineOff-label

    30 mg up to three times daily, increasing to 60 mg if tolerated.

    A single dose improved peak oxygen uptake in an invasive exercise RCT. Gut cramps and diarrhoea; caution with asthma and slow heart rate.

    [6]

  • DStimulants or modafinil for cognitive fatigueOff-label

    Only in selected patients with stable pacing, at low doses.

    They mask the warning signs and invite push–crash. ADHD is a separate, stronger reason (see Neurodivergence).

  • CLow-dose amitriptyline at nightOff-label

    5–10 mg, increasing to 25 mg, 2 hours before bed, for sleep and pain.

    Morning hangover, dry mouth, constipation, faster heart rate (watch with POTS).

  • AvoidRituximab

    A large RCT showed no benefit, with more side effects.

    [7]

  • AvoidImmunoadsorption, BC 007 and other antibody-targeted therapiesSpecialist

    Trials only.

    Repeated failures in proper trials so far.

    [8] [9]

🫀 POTS and orthostatic intolerance14

Heart rate up 30+ (40+ if aged 12–19) within 10 minutes of standing, with symptoms for 3 months, and no orthostatic hypotension. Worse with heat, meals, showers and standing still.

  • CFluids 2–3 L a day plus salt

    Add salt to reach about 6–10 g a day (2.4–4 g sodium) if blood pressure and kidneys are normal. A 500 mL drink before getting up helps mornings.

    Avoid with hypertension, kidney disease or heart failure.

    [10]

  • CCompression

    Waist-high 20–30 mmHg stockings, or an abdominal binder, on upright days.

    Calf-only stockings do little; the abdomen holds more blood.

    [10]

  • CCounter-manoeuvres and positioning

    Leg crossing, muscle tensing, squatting when symptomatic; head of the bed raised 10–15 cm.

    [10]

  • BRecumbent exercise, within the envelope

    Recumbent bike, rowing or swimming, building very slowly.

    Helps deconditioning in POTS, but with PEM only symptom-led activity is safe.

    [10]

  • BLow-dose propranololOff-label

    10–20 mg up to three or four times daily.

    Low doses worked better than high doses. Avoid with asthma; can worsen fatigue or low mood.

    [11]

  • BIvabradineOff-label

    2.5 mg twice daily, up to 7.5 mg twice daily. Usually a private prescription for this use.

    Lowers heart rate without lowering blood pressure. Not in pregnancy (needs contraception); flashes of light in vision.

    [12]

  • BMidodrineOff-label

    2.5 mg, increasing to 10 mg, three times daily while upright; last dose at least 4 hours before lying down.

    Best for low-resistance (neuropathic) POTS. Lying blood pressure can rise; scalp tingling; urinary retention.

    [13]

  • CFludrocortisoneOff-label

    0.05–0.1 mg daily, up to 0.2 mg.

    Check potassium and blood pressure at 2 weeks. Headache, swelling.

    [10]

  • CPyridostigmineOff-label

    30–60 mg up to three times daily.

    Useful when constipation is also a problem. Diarrhoea and cramps.

    [10]

  • CDesmopressin for bad daysOff-label

    0.1–0.2 mg occasionally, not daily.

    Check sodium; restrict fluids that evening.

    [14]

  • DClonidine or guanfacine for hyperadrenergic POTSOff-label

    Low doses at night, increasing slowly.

    Can drop blood pressure and add tiredness.

    [10]

  • CIV saline for flaresSpecialist

    1–2 L for severe flares or before procedures.

    Short-term relief only. Avoid long-term lines and ports: clot and infection risk.

    [10]

  • CEar vagus nerve stimulationOff-label

    Tragus clip stimulation, 1 hour daily (devices vary).

    A small sham-controlled trial lowered postural heart rise. Cheap and low risk; consumer devices aren’t standardised.

    [15]

  • AvoidIVIG for ‘autoimmune POTS’Specialist

    Trials only.

    The iSTAND RCT showed no benefit over albumin.

    [16]

🌡️ Mast cell symptoms (MCAS)9

Episodes of flushing, hives, swelling, wheeze, cramps or low blood pressure in 2+ systems at once. Strict ASCIA 2025 criteria need a tryptase rise and a treatment response; many patients have mast-cell-type symptoms without meeting them. A structured treatment trial is still reasonable.

  • BNon-sedating H1 antihistamineOff-label

    Cetirizine 10 mg or fexofenadine 180 mg daily, up to twice daily; urticaria guidelines allow up to 4 times the standard dose.

    Judge after 4–6 weeks with a symptom diary.

    [17] [18]

  • CAdd an H2 blockerOff-label

    Famotidine 20 mg twice daily.

    Most useful for gut and flushing symptoms.

    [18]

  • CMontelukastOff-label

    10 mg at night.

    Neuropsychiatric warning: nightmares, mood change, suicidal thoughts. Ask about these at every review.

    [18]

  • COral ketotifenOff-label

    Compounded. 0.5–1 mg at night, up to 1–2 mg twice daily.

    Sedation and weight gain. The Australian product is eye drops only.

    [18]

  • COral sodium cromoglicateOff-labelSpecialist

    100–200 mg four times daily before meals and bed, via compounding or Special Access. Check availability.

    Gut symptoms respond best. Takes 4–6 weeks.

    [18]

  • CTrigger review

    Heat, alcohol, friction, NSAIDs, opioids, stress, specific foods: a diary beats a blanket ban.

    [18]

  • DLow-histamine diet trial

    4 weeks maximum, then reintroduce foods one at a time, ideally with a dietitian.

    Easily becomes over-restriction. Avoid with any history of disordered eating or ARFID.

  • AAdrenaline autoinjector

    If there has been anaphylaxis, with an action plan.

    [18]

  • DOmalizumabSpecialistOff-label

    Immunologist only, for severe refractory cases.

    Case series only outside mastocytosis and urticaria.

    [18]

⚡ Fibromyalgia and widespread pain8

Widespread pain for 3+ months (ACR 2016), with tiredness, sleep and thinking symptoms. Check for small fibre neuropathy, hypermobility and inflammatory disease.

  • BExplain it as nervous system sensitisation

    Real pain from an over-amplified pain system, with known small-fibre and immune changes in some patients. It is not ‘in your head’.

    Validation itself lowers distress and improves engagement.

    [19]

  • AGentle movement: tai chi, hydrotherapy, walking

    Exercise is the only ‘strong for’ EULAR recommendation. Tai chi matched or beat aerobic exercise in a 226-person RCT.

    If PEM is present, movement must be symptom-led and tiny to start.

    [19] [20]

  • BCBT, ACT or pain psychology

    For coping, sleep and mood: not as a cure for ‘wrong beliefs’.

    [19]

  • BLow-dose amitriptylineOff-label

    5–25 mg at night.

    Sleep and pain. Watch heart rate with POTS.

    [19]

  • BDuloxetineOff-label

    30 mg, increasing to 60 mg daily.

    Nausea early; helps mood too. Not with other serotonergic drugs without checking.

    [19]

  • BPregabalinOff-label

    25–75 mg at night, slowly up to 150 mg twice daily.

    Modest benefit. Dizziness, weight gain, dependence; worse brain fog for some.

    [19]

  • CLow-dose naltrexoneOff-label

    As in the ME/CFS section.

    The largest RCT (n=99) found no benefit over placebo; smaller earlier trials were positive. A fair trial if other options fail.

    [21]

  • AvoidStrong opioids and tramadol long term

    Little benefit, rising doses, worse sensitisation and fatigue.

    [19]

🔥 Burning pain and small fibre neuropathy5

Burning, prickling or electric pain, often in feet and hands or patchy, with skin sensitivity, sweating or colour changes; nerve conduction studies are normal.

  • AFind a cause

    HbA1c (or glucose tolerance), B12, coeliac serology, TSH, ANA and ENA, protein electrophoresis, alcohol history, and vitamin B6 level.

    Excess B6 from supplements and energy drinks causes neuropathy; Australia has had many cases.

  • BSkin biopsySpecialist

    Intra-epidermal nerve fibre density, via neurology.

    Limited availability; turns a vague label into an objective one.

  • ANeuropathic pain medicines

    Amitriptyline, duloxetine or pregabalin, as above.

  • CTopical options

    Lidocaine patches or cream; high-strength capsaicin in a pain clinic.

  • CIVIGSpecialistOff-label

    Neurology only, for antibody-positive or clearly autoimmune cases.

    Trials are small and mixed.

    [22]

🧠 Functional neurological disorder (FND)5

Weakness, tremor, gait change, seizures or sensory loss with positive signs of FND (e.g. Hoover’s sign, entrainment). A rule-in diagnosis, not a diagnosis of exclusion.

  • BExplain the diagnosis with the positive signs

    Show the patient their own signs; name it as a problem of brain function, not damage and not ‘faking’. neurosymptoms.org is a good handout.

    A confident explanation is a treatment in itself.

  • BFND-informed physiotherapySpecialist

    Retraining automatic movement, attention diversion, self-management plan.

    Physio4FMD (n=355): no difference in physical function at 12 months vs community neuro physio, but more patients rated their symptoms better.

    [23]

  • BPsychological therapy (CBT-based)Specialist

    Especially for dissociative (functional) seizures.

    CODES (n=368): seizure frequency not significantly lower, but less bothersome seizures and better function and quality of life.

    [24]

  • BTreat the passengers

    Pain, fatigue, sleep, dissociation, trauma and mood each need their own plan.

  • AvoidAnti-seizure medicines for functional seizures

    No benefit for functional seizures; side effects and wasted time. Taper with a neurologist if epilepsy has been excluded.

🧩 Neurodivergence (ADHD, autism)6

Lifelong attention, sensory, social or routine differences. Common in this group, often undiagnosed in women and in adults, and it changes how symptoms show and which plans stick.

  • BAssess for ADHD and autism

    ASRS screener for ADHD; referral for adult assessment where it would change care.

    Undiagnosed ADHD looks like fatigue and brain fog; autistic burnout looks like ME/CFS.

  • ATreat ADHD

    Stimulants or non-stimulants as per ADHD guidelines.

    Stimulants raise heart rate: check orthostatic vitals. Guanfacine lowers blood pressure, so caution with POTS.

  • CAutistic burnout plan

    Cut demands and sensory load, more recovery time, predictable routines, ‘spoons’ or energy accounting.

    Often mistaken for depression or ME/CFS; they can co-exist.

  • CSensory accommodations

    Noise-cancelling headphones, lighting, clothing, quiet waiting rooms.

    Sensory load uses energy and can trigger crashes.

  • BMelatonin for delayed sleepOff-label

    2 mg modified-release, 1–2 hours before the target bedtime.

  • DObjective measures

    Interoception can differ, so track heart rate, step counts and a simple 0–10 diary rather than recall.

🤸 Hypermobility (hEDS and hypermobility spectrum)4

Beighton score 5+ (adults), joint instability, easy bruising, stretchy skin; often travels with POTS, MCAS, gut symptoms and pain.

  • BSlow strength and proprioception physio

    Closed-chain strengthening, joint control, very gradual progression.

    Hypermobility-aware physio; slower if PEM.

  • COT and joint protection

    Splints for specific tasks, pacing, aids.

  • CThink twice before surgery for instabilitySpecialist

    Physio first; surgical outcomes are less predictable.

  • CScreen for the overlaps

    Orthostatic vitals, mast cell symptoms, gut motility, pelvic floor.

🫃 Gut and bladder4

Bloating, pain, altered bowel habit, early fullness or bladder pain, common in all of the above.

  • ARule out coeliac and bile acid diarrhoea

    Coeliac serology before any gluten-free trial; bile acid malabsorption if diarrhoea predominant.

  • ALow-dose amitriptyline for IBSOff-label

    10 mg at night, up to 30 mg.

    Best for diarrhoea and pain; ATLANTIS showed benefit in primary care.

    [25]

  • BLow-FODMAP diet with a dietitian

    Short elimination, then structured reintroduction.

    Not without a dietitian if eating is already restricted.

  • CSmall meals for slow emptying

    Smaller, lower-fat meals; liquids later in the day.

    Common with POTS.

🦠 Long COVID specifics3

Symptoms 3+ months after COVID-19 (NASEM 2024). Treat the components (PEM, POTS, MCAS, sleep, mood) as above.

  • CHyperbaric oxygenSpecialistOff-label

    40 sessions in a single-centre sham-controlled trial.

    Improved thinking, energy and pain in one small trial (n=73). Costly and not funded for this use in Australia.

    [26]

  • BMetformin during a new COVID infectionOff-label

    In the COVID-OUT trial, 14 days of metformin started early lowered later long COVID by about 40%.

    Prevention for the next infection, not treatment of existing long COVID.

    [27]

  • AvoidPaxlovid as a treatment for established long COVID

    STOP-PASC: no benefit over placebo.

    [28]

🚫 Don’t: tested and failed, or harmful
  • Graded exercise therapy with PEM. NICE 2021 removed it; patient reports of harm.
  • Rituximab, IVIG, immunoadsorption or antibody therapies outside trials. Repeated null trials.
  • Long-term opioids and benzodiazepines. Tolerance, sensitisation, dependence, worse fatigue.
  • Unvalidated tests. IgG food panels, urine mycotoxins, provoked heavy-metal tests, private ‘chronic Lyme’ panels. They create false diagnoses and costly, sometimes harmful treatment.
  • Chelation, ozone, stem cell clinics, high-dose IV vitamin drips. No benefit shown; real risks.
  • Megadose supplements. Especially vitamin B6 (neuropathy) and stacks of 10+ products with no tracking.
  • Permanent central lines for IV fluids. Clot and infection risk.
