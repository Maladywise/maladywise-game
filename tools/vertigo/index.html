Clinician tool · for registered health professionals only

Vertigo Sorter

Sort the dizzy patient by timing and triggers (the TiTrATE approach), then do the matching bedside tests.

Red flags: send to emergency

  • New severe or sudden headache, or neck pain (think dissection)
  • Any new neuro sign: double vision, slurred speech, trouble swallowing, facial or limb weakness or numbness, limb ataxia
  • Can’t walk or stand unaided (truncal ataxia)
  • Sudden hearing loss, especially with vertigo
  • Continuous vertigo in someone with vascular risk factors, or central signs on HINTS
HINTS exam in brief
  • Only for continuous vertigo (acute vestibular syndrome) with nystagmus present at the time of the exam. Not for episodic or positional dizziness.
  • Head impulse: a corrective catch-up saccade is reassuring (peripheral). A normal head impulse in an acutely vertiginous patient is worrying (central).
  • Nystagmus: one direction, horizontal, dampened by fixation is peripheral. Direction-changing on gaze, vertical or purely torsional is central.
  • Test of skew: alternate cover test. Any vertical refixation is central.
  • Any one central finding means stroke until proven otherwise. Adding new hearing loss (“HINTS plus”) raises sensitivity.
  • Highly accurate in trained hands, but it needs training and practice. If unsure, refer.
  • Early CT misses most posterior fossa strokes, and even early MRI can be falsely negative.
Positional tests and manoeuvres
  • Dix-Hallpike (posterior canal): brief delay, then up-beating torsional nystagmus towards the lower ear, fading within a minute. Treat with the Epley manoeuvre on the affected side.
  • Supine roll test (horizontal canal): do it if the Dix-Hallpike is negative but the story fits. Horizontal nystagmus on turning either way. Treat with the barbecue (Lempert) roll or Gufoni manoeuvre.
  • Repeat the manoeuvre if symptoms persist. Atypical nystagmus or failure after a few goes means reconsider the diagnosis.
  • Vestibular physiotherapy helps recurrent or residual symptoms.

For registered health professionals, education only. Not for patients or the public. A teaching aid, not a diagnosis. It doesn’t replace clinical judgement or local stroke pathways.
