Fact sheet

ADHD in Adults

ADHD doesn’t stop at 18, and many adults, especially women, are only diagnosed in their 20s, 40s or later. It can look like chronic lateness, half-finished projects and a brain with 40 tabs open, alongside creativity, energy and the power to hyperfocus. Diagnosis and treatment can be life-changing.

The adult ADHD ladder

Most adults do best with a mix: systems that suit an ADHD brain, adjustments at work or study, and medicine for many. For the overview and costs, see our ADHD fact sheet.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careBuild an external brainOne calendar, alarms for everything, visible to-do lists, body doubling (working alongside someone), plus regular exercise and sleep.
    Works
    Little trial evidence for the systems themselves; exercise and sleep help mood and focus, and many adults rely on these tools
    Cost
    Free to cheap
    How long
    Ongoing. Expect to rebuild the system when life changes
    Watch for
    Buying a new planner instead of using the old one

    Memory is optional when your phone has 14 alarms.

  2. Step 2 · Allied healthCBT for adult ADHDPractical therapy for planning, procrastination, time-blindness and the self-criticism that years of “you’re so capable, if only…” leave behind.
    Works
    Moderate benefit in trials, especially alongside medicine
    Cost
    Psychologist about $200–300 a session, with Medicare rebates on a GP mental health plan
    How long
    Usually 8–12 sessions
    Watch for
    Choose someone experienced with adult ADHD. Coaching is unregulated, so check training

    Medicine helps you start. Skills help you finish.

  3. Step 3 · Work and studyReasonable adjustmentsWritten as well as spoken instructions, a quieter workspace or headphones, flexible hours, regular check-ins and help breaking big projects into steps.
    Works
    Little formal research, but recommended in guidelines and often the difference between coping and thriving
    Cost
    Free to you. In Australia, JobAccess gives free advice and may fund equipment; uni disability services arrange study adjustments
    How long
    Reviewed as roles change
    Watch for
    You don’t have to tell your employer, but you usually need to share something to get adjustments

    Open-plan offices: designed by someone who has never lost a thought mid-sentence.

  4. Step 4 · PrescriptionStimulant medicine (lisdexamfetamine, dexamfetamine or methylphenidate)First-choice medicine for adults when ADHD causes real problems. See dexamfetamine and methylphenidate.
    Works
    Amphetamine-type medicines were the best first choice for adults in a large 2018 comparison. In a big US study, crash risk was about 40% lower in months people took their ADHD medicine
    Cost
    PBS rules for adults depend on the medicine and when ADHD was diagnosed; some people pay privately. See our ADHD sheet for prices
    How long
    Works within about an hour; the dose is adjusted over weeks
    Watch for
    Poor appetite, trouble sleeping, faster pulse, higher blood pressure, anxiety. Never share it: it’s a tightly controlled medicine

    It doesn’t make you a different person. Most people say they finally feel like themselves.

  5. Step 5 · PrescriptionNon-stimulant medicine (atomoxetine, or guanfacine off-label)When stimulants don’t suit you, aren’t safe for you, or don’t help enough. See guanfacine.
    Works
    About half respond well; on average about two-thirds as effective as stimulants
    Cost
    Often private for adults, about $60–150 a month
    How long
    Daily; 4–12 weeks to work fully
    Watch for
    Atomoxetine: nausea, tiredness, rarely mood changes. Guanfacine: sleepiness, low blood pressure; don’t stop it suddenly

    Slow and steady. No peaks, no crashes, no clock-watching.

  6. Step 6 · SpecialistPsychiatristFor diagnosis, and when anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, substance use or autism are part of the picture.
    Works
    Not a treatment as such: confirms the diagnosis and treats everything that comes with it
    Cost
    Assessments often cost several hundred to over a thousand dollars, with partial Medicare rebates
    How long
    Waits can be months. In NSW, trained GPs can continue treatment and some can now diagnose and start it
    Watch for
    Bring school reports, and if you can, someone who knew you as a child

    Old report cards: finally useful.

Costs are rough Australian prices in November 2026 and vary by pharmacy and provider. PBS co-payment: $25, or $7.70 with a concession card. Responses are averages from trials, and brains are famously individual.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Online quizzes as a diagnosisScreening questionnaires are a good start for a GP conversation, nothing more. Plenty of things mimic ADHD, from poor sleep to thyroid problems.
  • “Nootropics” and focus supplementsNo good trials showing they treat ADHD, and some contain stimulants that aren’t on the label.
  • Brain scans, qEEG or genetic “ADHD profiles”Australian guidelines found they don’t improve diagnosis. Pricey pictures, same old history needed.
  • NeurofeedbackEvidence in adults is inconclusive, and courses cost thousands.

What you can do

  • Try a free adult ADHD screening questionnaire, then book a long GP appointment to talk it through.
  • Gather evidence from childhood: school reports, and a chat with a parent or sibling about what you were like before 12.
  • Be honest with your doctor about alcohol, cannabis or other drugs. Many adults use them to cope, and it changes the treatment plan.
  • Use your strengths. Hyperfocus, quick thinking and calm in a crisis suit many jobs; build your role around them where you can.
  • If you drive, tell your doctor if attention or medicine affects it. Licensing rules vary by country (in the UK, you must tell the DVLA if it affects your driving).
  • Store medicine safely and never share it.

See your GP if

  • You’ve struggled with focus, organisation, restlessness or impulses since childhood, and it affects work, study, money or relationships
  • You have low mood, anxiety, sleep problems or are using alcohol or drugs to cope
  • Your child has been diagnosed with ADHD and you recognise yourself
  • You’re pregnant or planning pregnancy while on ADHD medicine (don’t stop suddenly; talk it through)
  • You have side effects such as a racing heart, high blood pressure, weight loss or mood changes

Call 000 (or your local emergency number) for chest pain, fainting, or a racing heart that won’t settle. See a doctor the same day for hallucinations, paranoia or severe agitation on ADHD medicine. Adults with ADHD have a higher risk of suicide: if you have thoughts of ending your life, call Lifeline on 13 11 14 (24/7, Australia), or 000 if you are in danger.

Common questions

What does ADHD look like in adults?

Often less running around and more inner restlessness. Common signs: losing track of time, missing deadlines, forgetting appointments, starting many things and finishing few, losing keys and phones, interrupting, impulse spending and a short fuse. Many adults also describe intense focus on things that interest them.

How is ADHD diagnosed in adults?

A psychiatrist or trained doctor takes a detailed history. Signs need to have started before age 12, though they may not have been noticed then, and to cause problems in more than one area of life, such as work and home. Adults need at least 5 signs from a list (children need 6). Other causes such as sleep problems, thyroid disease, anaemia, anxiety or substance use are ruled out. There’s no scan or blood test for it.

Why are women often diagnosed later?

Women more often have the inattentive type, which is quieter and easier to miss. Many work hard to cover their struggles, and anxiety or depression is often diagnosed first. Guidelines now stress that ADHD should be considered at any age.

Can I drive on ADHD medicine?

Yes, for most people, and treatment often helps. A large US study found crash risk was about 40% lower in months people took their medicine. Don’t drive if you feel drowsy or unwell, and follow your local licensing rules.

Do I have to tell my employer?

No. It’s your choice. But to ask for adjustments you usually need to share some information about what you need. In Australia, employers must make reasonable adjustments for disability, and JobAccess can advise both of you for free.

Should I say “has ADHD” or “is an ADHDer”?

Either is fine. Some people prefer person-first language (“a child with ADHD”); many in the ADHD community prefer identity-first (“an ADHDer”, “an ADHD kid”), because ADHD is part of how they think, not something they carry around. We use both. If you’re not sure, ask the person what they prefer.
