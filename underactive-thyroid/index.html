Fact sheet

Underactive Thyroid

A thyroid that’s running slow, so everything else runs slow too: tiredness, feeling cold, constipation, low mood. Diagnosed with a simple blood test and treated with one small tablet a day, usually for life.

Typical signs: tiredness, feeling cold, weight gain, constipation, poor concentration, low mood, dry skin and hair, a hoarse voice, and irregular or heavy periods. Often mild and slow to build.

Why it happens: most often Hashimoto’s disease, where the immune system attacks the thyroid. It’s most common in women and often starts between 30 and 50. Other causes are thyroid surgery, radioactive iodine or radiotherapy, some medicines and, rarely, low iodine.

Diagnosis: blood tests for TSH and T4. A high TSH means the brain is shouting at a sluggish thyroid. A mildly high TSH with normal T4 is called subclinical, and often doesn’t need treatment.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Adding T3 (liothyronine)Guidelines don’t routinely recommend it: benefits over levothyroxine alone aren’t established and long-term effects are uncertain.
  • “Natural” or desiccated thyroid extractNot recommended: inconsistent and of uncertain safety. Natural isn’t the same as reliable.
  • Treating every slightly high TSHMany borderline results don’t need treatment, especially without symptoms. Recheck first.

What you can do

  • Take your tablet at the same time each day, on an empty stomach.
  • Keep iron, calcium and antacids a few hours away from your dose.
  • Tell your GP early if you’re pregnant or planning a pregnancy.
  • Mention biotin or “hair and nail” supplements before blood tests.
  • Report palpitations, shakiness or poor sleep: the dose may be too high.

See your GP if

  • You have several of the symptoms above
  • You’re on levothyroxine and still feel unwell, or feel jittery
  • You’re pregnant or planning to be
  • You have type 1 diabetes or another autoimmune condition and haven’t been tested
  • You notice a lump or swelling in your neck

Pregnant or planning? Tell your GP early: the dose often needs to rise, and untreated hypothyroidism raises the risk of miscarriage, pre-eclampsia and premature birth. Get emergency help (call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number. From a mobile, 112 works in most countries.) for a high temperature with a fast or irregular heartbeat on levothyroxine, or severe drowsiness and confusion in someone with untreated hypothyroidism.

Common questions

Is levothyroxine for life?

Usually, yes. Most people with an underactive thyroid take it for life and live a normal life. The dose may change over time, so regular blood tests are needed.

Can I take levothyroxine with coffee?

Take it with water on an empty stomach, 30 to 60 minutes before food, coffee or tea. Coffee and food reduce how much you absorb.

How long until the dose is right?

TSH is checked every 3 months until it is stable, then once a year. After a very high starting TSH it can take up to 6 months to settle.

Should I take T3 or natural thyroid extract?

Guidelines do not routinely recommend T3 or natural thyroid extract, because they have not been shown to work better than levothyroxine and their long-term safety is uncertain.

What are the symptoms of an underactive thyroid?

Tiredness, feeling cold, weight gain, constipation, dry skin, thinning hair, low mood, slowed thinking and heavier periods. Symptoms come on slowly and overlap with many other conditions, so a blood test (TSH) is needed to tell.

What causes an underactive thyroid?

In Australia the most common cause is Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, where the immune system attacks the thyroid. Others include thyroid surgery or radioactive iodine treatment, some medicines (such as amiodarone and lithium) and inflammation after pregnancy.

Does an underactive thyroid cause weight gain?

It can cause a modest gain, usually a few kilos and partly fluid. Treatment with levothyroxine reverses that part, but it doesn’t usually lead to large weight loss on its own.
