Tennis Elbow Exercises

Tennis elbow is a tendon problem, not inflammation, and it usually gets better with time and the right load. Most people don’t play tennis.

Quick answer: For tennis elbow, start with wrist isometric holds, then slow wrist-lifting exercises with a light weight, 3 times a week for at least 12 weeks. Ease off the gripping and lifting that flares it, but don’t rest it completely. Most people (about 8 to 9 in 10) are better within a year. Steroid injections ease pain short-term but make relapse more likely.

How to use this
  • Who it’s for: adults with pain on the outer elbow when gripping, lifting or twisting (kettle, mouse, tools, racquet).
  • Pain rules: mild pain (up to about 3–4 out of 10) during exercise is OK if it settles by the next day.
  • How often: isometrics daily when sore; strength work 3 times a week for at least 12 weeks.
  • How many: see each exercise. Progress weight slowly, about every 1–2 weeks.
  • Based on: clinical trials of exercise and injections for lateral elbow tendinopathy (including Coombes et al, JAMA 2013) and current reviews.

Your exercises

  1. Isometric wrist holdsForearm resting on a table, palm down. Push the back of your hand up against your other hand without moving, at a moderate effort.5 × 45 seconds, daily (good pain relief)
  2. Slow wrist liftsForearm on a table, palm down, light weight in hand. Lift the wrist (help with the other hand if needed), then lower slowly over 3–4 seconds.3 × 15, 3 times a week
  3. Forearm turnsForearm on a table, hold a hammer or weight upright and slowly turn the palm up and down.3 × 15, 3 times a week
  4. Grip squeezesSqueeze a soft ball or rolled towel, hold 3 seconds, relax. Add once gripping is less painful.3 × 15, 3 times a week
  5. Band rowsPull a band towards your ribs, squeezing your shoulder blades. Strong shoulders take load off the elbow.3 × 15, 3 times a week
  6. Wall push-up plusDo a push-up against a wall, then push a little further to spread the shoulder blades at the end.3 × 12, 3 times a week

Extras that can help: Lift with your palm facing up where you can, use a lighter mouse or a thicker tool grip, and take breaks from repetitive gripping. A forearm strap (counterforce brace) helps some people. Paracetamol or an anti-inflammatory gel can ease flares.

Stop and see your GP or physio if

  • Elbow pain after a fall, or you can’t straighten or bend the elbow
  • A hot, red, swollen elbow, or fever
  • Numbness or tingling in the hand, or weakness
  • Pain spreading from the neck down the arm
  • No improvement after 3 months of regular exercise

Want a supervised program?

A physio can tailor the exercises and progress them safely. An exercise physiologist can help with gym loading. If you have a chronic condition, ask your GP about a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan (GPCCMP): it gives a Medicare rebate for up to 5 allied health visits a year (physio, exercise physiologist or podiatrist).

Common questions

Should I have a steroid injection?

Injections can ease pain for a few weeks, but in trials people were more likely to relapse and slower to recover over a year than with exercise or waiting.

How long does tennis elbow last?

Usually 6–12 months, and most people (about 8 to 9 in 10) are better within a year.

Should I rest my arm?

Not completely. Cut back on what flares it, but keep using the arm and do the exercises.

Do I need a scan?

Usually not. The diagnosis is made by examination. Scans are for pain that isn’t improving or an unclear diagnosis.

Evidence and more information

  • Coombes et al, JAMA 2013: Corticosteroid injection, physiotherapy, or both for lateral epicondylalgia
  • Bisset et al, BMJ 2006: Mobilisation with movement and exercise, corticosteroid injection, or wait and see for tennis elbow
  • Healthdirect: Tennis elbow
