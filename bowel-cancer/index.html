Bowel Cancer

Bowel cancer is one of Australia’s most common cancers: about 14,800 new cases a year. Found early, it’s very treatable, and a free poo test at home can find it before you have any symptoms.

The free kit is a stick, a tube and some dignity. Two of those come in the envelope.

See your GP soon for any of these: blood in your poo or bleeding from the bottom, a change in your bowel habit that lasts more than a few weeks, unexplained weight loss, tiredness from low iron, or a lump or pain in the tummy. Don’t wait for a screening kit if you have symptoms.

The numbers

  • About 14,800 Australians are diagnosed each year, men and women in similar numbers
  • About 1 in 23 people are diagnosed by age 85
  • 72% are alive 5 years after diagnosis, up from 52% in the late 1980s
  • Cases in people under 50 are rising, which is why screening now starts at 45

Screening: the free test

  • The National Bowel Cancer Screening Program mails a free poo test (FIT) every 2 years from 50 to 74
  • From 45 to 49 you can ask for a kit yourself, free
  • It finds tiny amounts of blood you can’t see. A positive test means a colonoscopy, not a diagnosis
  • Most positive tests aren’t cancer: often polyps or piles. Removing polyps prevents cancer

Most bowel cancers start as polyps that sit quietly for years. Screening catches them while they’re still just squatters.

Risk factors

  • Age, and a family history of bowel cancer (a parent, brother or sister, especially if diagnosed young)
  • Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn’s or ulcerative colitis) for many years
  • Smoking, alcohol, being above a healthy weight and not moving much
  • Lots of red and processed meat; little fibre
  • Inherited syndromes such as Lynch syndrome (rare, but important to know about)

Tests and treatment

  • Colonoscopy is the main test: a camera looks at the whole bowel and can remove polyps or take samples
  • CT scans check whether it has spread
  • Surgery removes the cancer in most people with early bowel cancer
  • Chemotherapy after surgery lowers the chance of it coming back in some stages
  • Rectal cancer may need radiotherapy and chemotherapy before surgery
  • Advanced cancer: chemotherapy, targeted drugs and immunotherapy for some tumour types

‘I’m too young for bowel cancer’ is a sentence a lot of young people with bowel cancer have heard. Blood in your poo deserves a proper look at any age.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Colon cleanses and detox kitsThey don’t prevent bowel cancer and can upset your gut and your salts.
  • Blood tests that ‘detect cancer’ instead of the free kitNot a replacement for screening. Do the free test.

Common questions

What are the first signs of bowel cancer?

Often none. When there are symptoms, they include blood in the poo, a lasting change in bowel habit, unexplained weight loss, tiredness from low iron and tummy pain.

At what age should I start bowel cancer screening?

In Australia the free test is mailed every 2 years from 50 to 74, and people aged 45 to 49 can request one. People with a strong family history may need colonoscopy instead: ask your GP.

Is bowel cancer curable?

Yes, especially when found early. About 72% of people are alive 5 years after diagnosis, and many more when it’s caught at an early stage.
