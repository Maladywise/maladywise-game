Fever in Children

A hot, grumpy child and a thermometer that says 39. Fever is one of the most common reasons parents call a doctor. Most fevers are the body doing its job against a virus. How your child looks and acts matters more than the number.

What counts: a temperature of 38°C or more. A digital thermometer under the armpit works well for young children.

Why it happens: fever is a normal response to infection, and it helps the immune system. Viruses such as colds and flu are by far the most common cause. Ear, throat and urine infections, pneumonia and, rarely, meningitis can also cause it, as can some vaccines.

How long: most fevers settle within 1 to 4 days.

Febrile seizures: some children aged 6 months to 6 years have a fit when they have a fever, often as the temperature rises. They look frightening and are usually harmless, but a child who has one should see a doctor to check the cause of the fever.

The fever ladder

Fever medicine is for comfort, not for the number. It doesn’t make the illness go away faster. Watching your child is the main job.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careFluids, light clothes and restOffer small drinks often, and extra breastfeeds or formula for babies. Dress your child in light clothes. A lukewarm cloth on the forehead can feel nice.
    Works
    Keeps your child comfortable and hydrated, which is what matters most
    Cost
    Free
    How long
    1 to 4 days for most fevers
    Watch for
    Fewer wet nappies or less wee can mean dehydration.

    The fever isn’t the enemy. It’s the bouncer.

  2. Step 2 · PharmacyParacetamol or ibuprofen if uncomfortableOnly if the fever is making your child miserable or they have pain, such as a sore throat. For paracetamol, check the age and dose on the pack (Australian infant drops start from 1 month), and ask a pharmacist or doctor first for a baby under 3 months, who also needs a doctor to check any fever. Ibuprofen is not for babies under 3 months, children who are dehydrated or have chickenpox, and children with asthma unless a doctor says it’s fine.
    Works
    Improves comfort. Lowering the temperature doesn’t speed up recovery. Don’t alternate the two unless a health professional advises it
    Cost
    A few dollars
    How long
    As needed, following the label
    Watch for
    Use the dose for your child’s age and weight. Never give aspirin to children.

    Treat the child, not the thermometer.

  3. Step 3 · GPA doctor’s checkA GP looks for the cause, such as an ear, throat or urine infection, and checks for signs of something more serious.
    Works
    Finds the few fevers that need treatment, such as antibiotics for a bacterial infection
    Cost
    Often bulk billed for children; varies
    How long
    Same day or next day, depending on how your child is
    Watch for

    Ninety-nine viruses and one bacterium. Your GP’s job is finding the one.

    Works
    Young babies can be seriously unwell without looking it, so they are checked carefully
    Cost
    Free in Australian public hospitals
    How long
    Same day
    Watch for
    Go to the nearest emergency department; don’t wait to see if it settles.

    Under 3 months, there’s no “wait and see”.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Cold baths, cold sponging or stripping your child offNot recommended. Fever is a normal response to infection, and cooling a child down this way just makes them uncomfortable.
  • Giving medicine just to bring the number downIf your child is happy and playing, they don’t need medicine. It doesn’t help them recover faster.
  • Blaming teethingTeething doesn’t cause a fever. Look for another cause.
  • AspirinNever give aspirin to children under 16.

What you can do

  • Check how your child looks and behaves, not just the temperature.
  • Offer drinks often and keep an eye on wet nappies or wees.
  • Dress them in light clothes with a sheet or light blanket.
  • Check on your child regularly, including overnight.
  • Keep them home from childcare or school while they have a fever.
  • In Australia, call the healthdirect helpline on 1800 022 222 for advice from a nurse, any time.

See your GP if

  • A fever has lasted more than 2 days
  • Your child is vomiting and can’t keep fluids down
  • They have an ongoing headache or tummy pain
  • They have had a febrile seizure
  • They have travelled overseas recently, or been near someone with a serious infection

See a doctor urgently if

  • A baby under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C or more
  • A temperature above 40°C
  • A rash, especially one that doesn’t fade when you press a glass on it
  • Unusual sleepiness, or your child is getting worse
  • A child with a weak immune system who has a temperature of 38°C or more

Call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number if your child is drowsy and hard to wake, has trouble breathing, has a stiff neck or dislikes bright light, has a fit that won’t stop, or has cold hands and feet with pale, blue or blotchy skin.

Common questions

What temperature is a fever in a child?

38°C or higher. Most fevers are caused by viruses and settle within a few days.

Should I give paracetamol for every fever?

No. Medicine is only needed if your child is uncomfortable. Bringing the temperature down doesn’t help them get better faster.

Can I alternate paracetamol and ibuprofen?

Not unless a doctor, nurse or pharmacist advises it. Choose one and follow the label for your child’s age and weight.

Is a high fever dangerous?

The number is less important than how your child looks and behaves. Still, see a doctor for a temperature above 40°C, any fever in a baby under 3 months, or a fever lasting more than 2 days.

How long does a fever last in children?

Most fevers from viral infections last 2 to 3 days. See a GP if a fever lasts more than 2 days, or sooner if your child seems very unwell. Any baby under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C or more needs to see a doctor straight away.

What is a febrile convulsion?

A seizure triggered by a fever, affecting about 1 in 30 children between 6 months and 6 years. It’s frightening but usually harmless and doesn’t cause brain damage. Lie your child on their side, time it and call 000 if it lasts more than 5 minutes or they don’t wake up normally.

What is the best way to take a child’s temperature?

A digital thermometer under the arm is best for children under 5. Ear thermometers are fine for older babies and children if used correctly. Forehead strips aren’t reliable.
