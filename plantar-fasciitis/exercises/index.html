Plantar Fasciitis: exercise plan

That first-step-of-the-morning stab is miserable, but heel pain responds well to stretching, strength and smarter loading. Patience is part of the prescription.

How to use this
  • Pain rules: some discomfort during the exercises is OK. If your first steps the next morning are clearly worse, drop back a level.
  • How often: stretches every day; heel raises every second day.
  • How long: 3 months of heel raises. In the trial, people doing them felt better by 3 months than people who only stretched; by a year, both groups had improved.
  • Progress when: following the trial’s schedule: add weight after 2 weeks, add more after 4 weeks, then keep adding as you get stronger.
  • Based on: Rathleff’s heavy heel-raise trial (2015) and the 2023 US physiotherapy heel pain guideline (JOSPT).

Your exercises

  1. Arch stretchSit and cross the sore foot over your knee. Pull your toes back towards your shin until you feel a stretch in the arch. Do one round before your first steps in the morning.10 stretches of 10 seconds, 3 times a day
  2. Calf stretch (straight knee)Hands on a wall, sore leg back, knee straight, heel down. Lean forward until you feel the calf stretch.3 × 30 seconds, twice a day
  3. Calf stretch (bent knee)Same position, but bend the back knee to stretch the lower calf.3 × 30 seconds, twice a day
  4. Heavy heel raise (Rathleff)Stand on the sore foot on a step, heel hanging off, with a rolled towel under your toes so they bend right back at the top. Rise up over 3 seconds, pause 2 seconds, lower over 3 seconds. Use a load you can only just lift the set number of times: your body weight at first, then a backpack with books.Weeks 1–2: 3 × 12. Weeks 3–4: 4 × 10 with a backpack. From week 5: 5 × 8, adding books as you get stronger. Every second day

Taping and inserts: taping the foot eases pain over the first few weeks. Soft gel heel inserts (used in the trial) or arch supports can help alongside exercise, but not on their own. If your first steps in the morning stay painful, ask a physio about a night splint (worn for 1–3 months).

Stop and see your GP or physio if

  • Heel pain after a fall or jump, or you can’t take weight
  • Numbness, tingling or burning in the foot
  • Swelling, redness or heat in the heel, or pain in other joints
  • No improvement after 3 months of regular exercise

Want a supervised program?

A physio or podiatrist can tailor the program, add taping or inserts and fit a night splint. MyHEEL, an online resource from La Trobe University, explains which heel pain treatments work. If you have a chronic condition, ask your GP about a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan (GPCCMP): it gives a Medicare rebate for up to 5 allied health visits a year (physio, exercise physiologist or podiatrist).

Evidence and more information

  • JOSPT 2023: Heel pain clinical practice guideline
  • Rathleff et al, 2015: High-load strength training trial (PEDro record)
  • BJSM blog: Rathleff explains the heel-raise and stretching programs used in the trial
