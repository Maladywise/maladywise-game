Explaining ADHD and Autism to Kids, Strengths First

How you explain ADHD or autism to a child shapes how they see themselves for years. Lead with what their brain is great at, be honest about what it finds hard, and make it clear nothing is wrong with them.

  • Tell them. Autistic adults who learned their diagnosis as children report better wellbeing than those who found out later.
  • Strengths first, then challenges, then what helps. In that order.
  • Different, not less. Brains come in different styles, like left- and right-handedness.
  • Keep it ongoing: one big talk is less useful than lots of small ones.

Every brain comes with a factory settings sheet. Theirs just wasn’t in the box.

The “brain style” idea

For younger children: “Everyone’s brain works a bit differently. Your brain has a style called ADHD (or autism). It means some things are extra easy for you, and some things take more energy. Lots of brilliant people have brains like yours.”

ADHD strengths to name

  • Energy and enthusiasm: you bring the fun and get things moving.
  • Curiosity and creativity: you notice ideas other people miss.
  • Hyperfocus: when something interests you, you can dive deeper than almost anyone.
  • Calm in a crisis: many ADHD brains think fast when things get exciting or urgent.
  • Big heart and humour: quick, funny, kind and quick to forgive.

What it finds harder: starting boring tasks, waiting, remembering several steps, sitting still for long. “That’s why we use timers, lists and movement breaks: they’re tools, like glasses.”

Autism strengths to name

  • Deep interests: you can become a real expert in the things you love.
  • Honesty and fairness: you say what’s true and care about what’s right.
  • Noticing details and patterns other people miss.
  • Loyalty: you’re a true friend once you trust someone.
  • Logical thinking and a great memory for facts.

What it finds harder: loud or busy places, sudden changes, guessing what people mean when they don’t say it. “That’s why we use headphones, warnings before changes and clear words.”

How to have the talk

  • Choose a calm moment and a familiar place. Short is fine.
  • Use their interests: “Your brain is like a race car: very fast, needs good brakes.” or “like a specialist computer: amazing at some jobs, needs the right settings.”
  • Normalise: name people they admire who think differently, if that’s been shared publicly.
  • Invite questions, and come back to it often. Teens may prefer to read first, then talk.
  • Never use the diagnosis as a punishment or excuse (“that’s just your ADHD”).

Words that help (and hurt)

  • Try: “different”, “your brain style”, “things that take more energy”, “tools that help”.
  • Avoid: “broken”, “naughty brain”, “suffers from”, “normal kids”.
  • Many autistic people prefer “autistic person”; others prefer “person with autism”. Ask your child what they like as they get older.

Tell school too

  • Share strengths and what helps, not just the diagnosis. A one-page “all about me” sheet works well.
  • Ask about adjustments: movement breaks, quiet space, visual timetables, extra time, warnings before changes.

Common questions

Should I tell my child they have ADHD or autism?

Yes, in age-appropriate words. Research in autistic adults found that learning the diagnosis earlier was linked to better wellbeing, and children usually already know they’re different.

How do I explain autism to a child?

Describe it as a brain style with real strengths, such as deep interests, honesty and noticing details, and some things that take more energy, such as noisy places or sudden changes. Then talk about the tools that help.

How do I explain ADHD positively?

Lead with strengths like energy, curiosity, creativity and hyperfocus, then explain what’s harder, like starting boring tasks or waiting, and the tools that help, like timers and movement breaks.
