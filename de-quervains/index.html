Fact sheet

De Quervain’s Tenosynovitis

Pain on the thumb side of the wrist when you grip, pinch, lift or twist. The thumb tendons are squeezed in their tunnel. Common in new parents. Rest, a splint and, if needed, an injection usually sort it.

Typical signs: pain and sometimes swelling at the base of the thumb and side of the wrist, worse lifting a baby, opening jars, wringing or texting. There may be creaking or catching as the thumb moves.

Why it happens: the two tendons that move the thumb get irritated where they run through a tight tunnel at the wrist. It’s about 3 times more common in women and often follows pregnancy and the early months of lifting and feeding a baby. Repetitive gripping with the wrist bent is another trigger. Often there’s no clear reason.

Diagnosis: from the story and simple thumb and wrist movement tests, such as tucking the thumb into a fist and tilting the wrist down. Scans or X-rays are rarely needed.

The thumb-side wrist pain ladder

Most people improve with load changes and a splint. A steroid injection is the best-tested next step.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Scans for typical casesAn ultrasound isn’t needed to diagnose it and rarely changes treatment.
  • Laser and therapeutic ultrasoundNo good evidence they help.
  • Kinesiology tape alonePopular colours, little evidence. A proper splint does the job better.
  • Stopping breastfeeding to fix your wristNot needed. Change your holding position and support the baby’s weight with pillows instead.

What you can do

  • Support your baby’s weight with your forearms and pillows, not a pinched thumb.
  • Wear the splint at night and for tasks that flare it.
  • Break up long sessions of texting, gaming, typing or gripping tools.
  • Once pain has settled, gentle thumb and wrist strengthening helps you get back to full use.

See your GP if

  • Pain isn’t improving after 2–4 weeks of rest and a splint
  • Pain stops you caring for a baby, working or sleeping
  • Numbness or tingling in the thumb or fingers
  • Several joints are swollen or stiff in the morning

See a doctor the same day for a hot, red, swollen wrist, especially with fever or after a cut or bite, or wrist pain after a fall (a broken scaphoid bone can be missed on first look).

Common questions

Why do new parents get de Quervain’s?

It often follows pregnancy and the first year with a baby. Hormone changes and the constant lifting and feeding may both play a part. It usually settles as lifting changes and with treatment.

Is a steroid injection safe while breastfeeding?

Yes, a single local injection is generally considered compatible with breastfeeding. Ask your doctor if you have concerns.

How long does de Quervain’s last?

Mild cases can settle in weeks with rest and a splint. An injection usually helps within days to weeks. Persistent cases may need a second injection or, rarely, surgery.

Is it the same as arthritis at the base of the thumb?

No, although they hurt in a similar area. Thumb-base arthritis affects the joint itself and is more common in older people. Your GP can tell them apart.

What is the Finkelstein test?

A simple check for de Quervain’s: you tuck your thumb into your palm, close your fingers over it and bend your wrist towards your little finger. Sharp pain on the thumb side of the wrist suggests de Quervain’s. Don’t keep repeating it if it hurts.

Does a steroid injection work for de Quervain’s?

Usually, yes. A single injection settles symptoms in around 7 to 8 in 10 people. A thumb splint is often worn as well, though it’s not clear it adds much. A second injection or minor surgery is an option if it comes back.

Will de Quervain’s go away without treatment?

It often settles over a few months, especially when the repetitive lifting or gripping eases. In new parents it often improves as the baby gets older or breastfeeding stops. A splint and changing how you lift help it settle faster.
