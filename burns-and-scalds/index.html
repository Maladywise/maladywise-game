Fact sheet

Burns and scalds

The single most useful thing you can do for a burn is cool running water for 20 minutes. It helps even up to 3 hours after the burn.

First aid

  1. Stop the burning. Move away from the heat; for clothing on fire, stop, drop and roll.
  2. Cool running water for 20 minutes. Tap water, not ice. Keep the rest of the person warm, especially children.
  3. Remove jewellery and clothing near the burn, unless it’s stuck to the skin.
  4. Cover loosely with cling wrap or a clean, non-stick dressing.
  5. Pain relief: paracetamol or ibuprofen.

Never put on a burn

  • Ice or iced water (can deepen the damage)
  • Butter, oil, toothpaste, flour or egg white
  • Fluffy cotton wool that sticks

Get medical help if

  • The burn is bigger than the person’s palm, or blisters, or looks white, charred or leathery
  • It’s on the face, hands, feet, genitals or over a joint, or goes all the way around a limb
  • It’s a chemical or electrical burn, or the person breathed in smoke
  • The person is a baby or young child, an older adult, or pregnant
  • Signs of infection later: spreading redness, swelling, pus or fever

Call 000 for large burns, burns to the airway (singed nose hair, a hoarse voice, coughing soot), electrical burns, or if the person is unwell.

Preventing burns

  • Hot drinks are the most common cause of scalds in young children: a cuppa can scald a child long after it’s poured. Keep them out of reach and never drink one with a child on your lap.
  • Turn pot handles inwards; keep children away from the barbecue, fire pits and campfires (coals stay hot for hours: put them out with water, not sand).
  • Set hot water to no more than 50 °C at the tap in the bathroom; run cold water first in the bath.
  • Sparklers and fireworks burn far hotter than a cooking flame.
  • Check your smoke alarms.

Common questions

What is the best first aid for a burn?

Cool running water for 20 minutes, as soon as possible and up to 3 hours after the burn. Then remove jewellery, cover loosely with cling wrap and get help if needed.

Should I put ice on a burn?

No. Ice can make the damage deeper and can cause hypothermia in children. Use cool running tap water instead.

Should I pop a burn blister?

No. Leave blisters alone and see a doctor or nurse, especially if they’re large or on the hands, face or feet.

When should a burn be seen in hospital?

Burns bigger than the person’s palm, deep burns, burns to the face, hands, feet, genitals or joints, chemical or electrical burns, smoke inhalation, and burns in babies and young children.
