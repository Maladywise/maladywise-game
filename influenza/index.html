Flu (Influenza)

Flu hits suddenly: fever, aches, exhaustion and a dry cough, often wiping you out for a week. Most people recover at home, but it can be dangerous for babies, older people, pregnant women and people with chronic illness. The yearly vaccine is the best protection.

Symptoms

  • Sudden fever and chills, muscle aches, headache and exhaustion
  • Dry cough and sore throat; children may have vomiting or diarrhoea
  • Worst for 3–4 days; tiredness and cough can linger for 2 weeks or more

Call 000 or go to an emergency department for trouble breathing, chest pain, confusion, a seizure, blue lips, not passing urine, or a baby who is floppy, not feeding or hard to wake. Getting better then suddenly worse again also needs urgent care.

Real flu flattens you. If you went to work, it probably wasn't flu.

Who should see a doctor early (within 48 hours)

  • People 65 and over, pregnant women, babies and young children
  • Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people
  • Anyone with heart, lung, kidney or liver disease, diabetes, obesity or a weak immune system
  • Residents of aged care

The flu shot changes every year because the virus does. Last year's doesn't cover this year's.

The flu vaccine

  • A new vaccine each year, ideally from April so you’re protected for the winter peak.
  • Free under the National Immunisation Program for children 6 months to under 5, people 65 and over, pregnant women (any stage), Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people from 6 months, and people with certain medical conditions. Some states fund it more widely.
  • Protection varies year to year, roughly 40–60%, and it also makes illness milder.
  • You can’t catch flu from the vaccine. A sore arm and feeling a bit flat for a day are common.

Common questions

How is the flu different from a cold?

Flu starts suddenly with fever, aches and exhaustion, and often knocks you out for days. A cold comes on gradually, is mainly a runny nose and sore throat, and you can usually keep going.

Who gets a free flu vaccine in Australia?

Children 6 months to under 5, people 65 and over, pregnant women, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people from 6 months, and people with certain medical conditions. Some states fund it for more people.

Should I take Tamiflu?

It’s mainly for people at higher risk or who are very unwell, started within 48 hours. On average it shortens symptoms by about a day.
