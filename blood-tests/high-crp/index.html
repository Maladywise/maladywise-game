Blood test results

High CRP (C-Reactive Protein)

CRP is made by your liver when there’s inflammation anywhere in the body. It rises within hours of an infection or injury and falls quickly as you get better. It tells you something is going on, not what or where.

Quick answer: Most labs call CRP under 5 mg/L normal. Mild rises of 5–10 are common with colds, smoking, extra weight or poor sleep. Most infections and inflammatory flares push it over 10, and results in the hundreds suggest bacterial infection or major inflammation. hs-CRP over 3 mg/L signals higher heart risk.

What the test measures

C-reactive protein is an acute-phase protein: the liver pumps it out within hours of infection, injury or an inflammatory flare, and levels can rise several hundred-fold. Because it falls within days once the cause settles, it’s useful for tracking whether treatment is working. It doesn’t show where the inflammation is.

Standard CRP vs hs-CRP. It’s the same protein. The high-sensitivity (hs-CRP) test measures very low levels accurately and is used to fine-tune heart disease risk in people who are otherwise well.

Typical adult ranges

  • Standard CRP: under 5 mg/L in most Australian labs (some use under 3 or under 10).
  • US labs often report mg/dL: 10 mg/L is 1 mg/dL.
  • hs-CRP and heart risk (AHA/CDC): under 1 mg/L lower risk, 1–3 average, over 3 higher. An hs-CRP over 10 mg/L isn’t used for heart risk: it points to infection or inflammation instead.

What a high result usually means

Common causes:

  • Mild rises (about 5–10 mg/L): a cold, extra weight, smoking, poor sleep, diabetes, pregnancy, gum disease, and oral oestrogen (the pill or HRT tablets).
  • Infections: chest, urine, skin and throat infections, and viruses. Most infections push CRP over 10 mg/L.
  • Injury and surgery: CRP rises for several days after an operation.
  • Inflammatory conditions: rheumatoid arthritis, polymyalgia rheumatica, gout flares, inflammatory bowel disease.

Less common but important:

  • Abscesses, bone or heart valve infections.
  • Giant cell arteritis and other vasculitis.
  • Pancreatitis and some cancers.

Very high results, in the hundreds, are more typical of bacterial infection, major injury or surgery than of viruses, but there’s overlap.

Symptoms

CRP causes no symptoms itself. What matters is the cause: fever, pain, swelling, cough, burning urine, joint stiffness, weight loss or night sweats.

What happens next

  • Interpret it with your symptoms. A CRP of 15 in someone with a cold and a CRP of 15 in someone with unexplained weight loss mean different things.
  • Repeat it in a few weeks if it’s mildly high with no obvious cause and you feel well.
  • Other tests may include a full blood count, ESR, urine test, kidney and liver tests, or a chest X-ray, guided by symptoms.
  • For hs-CRP, two results at least 2 weeks apart are averaged, taken when you’re well.

Get urgent help (000 in Australia, or your local emergency number) for a high CRP with signs of sepsis: confusion, fast breathing, shaking chills, mottled or very pale skin, or feeling the sickest you’ve ever felt. See a doctor the same day if you’re over 50 with a new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw pain when chewing or any change in vision: this can be giant cell arteritis, which can threaten sight. See your GP soon for a raised CRP with weight loss, night sweats or persistent pain.

Common questions

Can stress raise CRP?

A little. Long-term stress, depression and poor sleep are linked with slightly higher CRP, usually in the low single digits. Stress won’t explain a CRP of 30 or 100: that needs a proper look for infection or inflammation.

What CRP level is concerning?

It depends on how you are. Most infections and inflammatory flares push CRP over 10 mg/L, and results in the hundreds suggest bacterial infection or major inflammation. A mildly raised CRP in someone who feels well is often from a recent cold, smoking or extra weight.

Is a CRP of 7 bad?

It’s mildly raised and usually not worrying on its own. A recent cold, carrying extra weight, smoking, poor sleep or the contraceptive pill can all do it. If you feel well, your doctor may repeat it in a few weeks.

What’s the difference between CRP and hs-CRP?

They measure the same protein. Standard CRP looks for infection and inflammation; hs-CRP measures very low levels to help estimate heart disease risk in otherwise healthy people. Under 1 mg/L is lower risk, 1–3 average and over 3 higher.

Can CRP be high without an infection?

Yes. Injury, surgery, autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and polymyalgia rheumatica, gout, inflammatory bowel disease, some cancers and even obesity can raise CRP.

How fast does CRP go down?

Quickly. CRP falls within days once the cause starts to settle, which is why doctors use it to check whether treatment is working. A CRP that isn’t falling after a few days of treatment can be a sign the infection isn’t responding.
