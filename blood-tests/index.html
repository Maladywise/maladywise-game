Blood Test Results Explained

Got a result flagged H or L and a few days until your appointment? These guides explain what each test measures, the usual reasons it’s high or low, what happens next, and the numbers that can’t wait.

Quick answer: A result outside the reference range isn’t automatically a problem: ranges cover about 95% of healthy people, so some healthy results land just outside. What matters is how far out it is, your symptoms and the trend. Each guide below lists common causes first, and the numbers that need same-day care.

How to read a result

  • The range is on your report. Labs use slightly different methods, so always compare with the range printed next to your result.
  • H and L flags mean outside that range, not necessarily abnormal for you. A slightly high or low result is often repeated rather than acted on.
  • Trends beat single numbers. A result that’s stable over years means something different from one that’s changed fast.
  • Context matters. Recent illness, exercise, pregnancy, medicines and supplements all shift results.
  • Units differ by country. Australia, the UK and most of the world use SI units (mmol/L, µmol/L); the US often uses mg/dL. Each guide gives both where it helps.

Common questions

What do H and L mean on a blood test?

H means your result is above the lab’s reference range and L means below it. Reference ranges are set to include about 95% of healthy people, so roughly 1 in 20 healthy people will have any given result just outside the range.

Why do reference ranges differ between labs?

Labs use different machines and methods, and some set ranges for their local population. That’s why you should always compare your result with the range printed on your own report.

Should I worry about a slightly abnormal result?

Usually not. Slightly high or low results are common and often return to normal on a repeat test. Your doctor will look at how far out it is, your symptoms, your other results and any change over time.

Do I need to fast for blood tests?

Most of the tests on these pages don’t need fasting. Iron studies are best done fasting in the morning, and some glucose and cholesterol tests need fasting. Your request form or lab will tell you.

Can I get my results explained here?

These guides give general information, not advice about your own results. Your GP can interpret them with your history and examination, and decide what, if anything, needs doing.
