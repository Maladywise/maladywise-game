High Platelets (Thrombocytosis)

Platelets are tiny cell fragments that help blood clot. A high count is usually the bone marrow responding to something else going on in the body, such as an infection, inflammation, surgery or low iron. Less often, the marrow itself is overactive.

Quick answer: Platelets above 400–450 × 10⁹/L are high. About 9 in 10 high counts are reactive: a response to infection, inflammation, surgery, bleeding or iron deficiency, and they settle when the cause does. A count that stays high without a reason needs checking for a bone marrow condition such as essential thrombocythaemia.

What the test measures

The platelet count is part of a full blood count (FBC). Platelets are made in the bone marrow and live about a week. The body makes more when it’s inflamed, injured or short of iron, so a high count is often a sign of something else rather than a problem in itself. A high count is called thrombocytosis.

Typical adult ranges

  • Normal: 150–400 × 10⁹/L (150,000–400,000 per microlitre in US units)
  • Raised: most labs flag over 400. Guidelines use 450 or more when looking for a bone marrow cause.
  • Extreme: over 1000 × 10⁹/L.

What a high result usually means

Common causes (reactive thrombocytosis, about 9 in 10 high counts):

  • Tissue damage: recent surgery, injury, fractures or burns. This was the biggest single group in a large hospital study.
  • Infection: chest, urine, skin or bone infections, and the recovery phase afterwards.
  • Inflammation: rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, polymyalgia rheumatica.
  • Iron deficiency, often from bleeding. See low ferritin.
  • No spleen: after the spleen is removed, platelets often stay a little high for life.
  • Rebound after a low count, for example after stopping heavy drinking or after chemotherapy.

Less common but important:

  • Cancer. A raised count can be an early clue. In a large UK primary care study, 11.6% of men and 6.2% of women with platelets over 400 were diagnosed with cancer within a year, compared with 4.1% and 2.2% of those with normal counts. Lung and bowel cancers were among the commonest.
  • Essential thrombocythaemia (ET): the bone marrow makes too many platelets. Most people with ET have a change in the JAK2, CALR or MPL gene.
  • Other myeloproliferative neoplasms: polycythaemia vera, myelofibrosis and chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML).
  • Some types of myelodysplastic syndrome.

Symptoms

  • Usually none from the count itself. Reactive thrombocytosis causes the symptoms of whatever triggered it.
  • In ET and related conditions: headaches, visual disturbance, burning, red and painful hands or feet (erythromelalgia), and clots such as stroke, heart attack or deep vein thrombosis.
  • At very high counts, easy bruising or bleeding, because the platelets don’t work properly.

What happens next

  • Repeat the full blood count, with a blood film, a few weeks after any illness has settled. Most reactive counts come back down.
  • Look for a cause: CRP or ESR, iron studies, and other tests guided by symptoms, such as a chest X-ray.
  • Haematology referral for a count that stays at 450 or more for 2–3 months with no clear cause, or one with other abnormal blood counts, a clot or an enlarged spleen. Tests include JAK2, CALR, MPL and BCR::ABL1 gene tests, and sometimes a bone marrow biopsy.
  • Reactive thrombocytosis rarely causes clots on its own and usually needs no treatment beyond treating the cause.
  • ET is treated according to clot risk: many people take low-dose aspirin, and some need medicine to lower the count.

Get urgent help (000 in Australia, or your local emergency number) for signs of stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech), chest pain, a painful swollen leg with breathlessness, or heavy bleeding. See your GP within days if your platelets are over 1000 × 10⁹/L (1,000,000 per microlitre): at these levels, people with a bone marrow cause become more likely to bleed, and prompt haematology advice is needed. See your GP soon for a count that stays high, or a high count with weight loss, a persistent cough, coughing blood, ongoing tummy pain or a change in bowel habit.

Common questions

What platelet count is too high?

Most labs flag a count over 400 × 10⁹/L, and guidelines investigate counts that stay at 450 or more for a bone marrow cause. Counts over 1000 × 10⁹/L are called extreme and need prompt medical advice, whatever the cause.

Is a platelet count of 450 bad?

Usually not. A mildly high count is most often a reaction to a recent infection, inflammation, injury or low iron, and it settles once the cause does. Your GP will usually repeat it after a few weeks and check your iron.

Can low iron cause high platelets?

Yes. Iron deficiency is one of the commonest causes of a raised platelet count, often from heavy periods or bleeding from the gut. The count usually returns to normal once iron is replaced, but the cause of the iron loss still needs finding.

Can high platelets be a sign of cancer?

Sometimes. In a large UK study, about 1 in 9 men and 1 in 16 women with a platelet count over 400 were diagnosed with cancer within a year, so doctors take an unexplained high count seriously. Most people with high platelets don’t have cancer.

What is essential thrombocythaemia?

Essential thrombocythaemia (ET) is a slow-growing bone marrow condition where too many platelets are made. Most people have a change in the JAK2, CALR or MPL gene. Treatment aims to prevent clots, often with low-dose aspirin, and many people live a normal lifespan.

Do high platelets cause blood clots?

Reactive thrombocytosis rarely causes clots on its own. Bone marrow conditions such as essential thrombocythaemia do raise the risk of clots, and at very high counts they can cause bleeding too, which is why a persistent unexplained high count is checked.
