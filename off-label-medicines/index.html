Off-label medicines: surprising second lives

‘Off-label’ means using a medicine for a condition, dose, age group or route that isn’t in its official approved information. It’s legal, common and often sensible, but the evidence ranges from rock-solid to wishful thinking.

What off-label means

  • In Australia, each medicine’s approved uses are set by the TGA. A doctor may prescribe outside them when there’s a good reason.
  • It’s very common in children, pregnancy, palliative care and mental health, because many medicines were never formally tested in those groups. For antipsychotics, an estimated 40–75% of adult use is off-label.
  • Your doctor should tell you it’s off-label, explain the likely benefits and risks, and write down the reason.
  • Off-label medicines may not be PBS-subsidised for that use, so they can cost more.
  • Drug companies aren’t allowed to advertise off-label uses.

Surprising second lives

  • Heroin, as a hospital painkiller: in the UK, diamorphine (medical heroin) is a licensed medicine for severe pain after surgery, heart attacks and at the end of life. In Australia, heroin is a prohibited substance.
  • An angina pill that became sildenafil: developed for chest pain in the 1990s, its unexpected side effect changed its career. It’s now also used for a lung blood-pressure condition (pulmonary arterial hypertension).
  • A blood pressure tablet for hair: minoxidil was a strong blood pressure medicine that grew hair as a side effect. Low doses by mouth are now used off-label for hair loss, with improvement in most people in published studies. Watch for unwanted hair growth elsewhere, dizziness and ankle swelling.
  • A ‘water tablet’ for acne: spironolactone, a diuretic, calms hormonal acne in women. A 2023 UK trial (SAFA) found better acne quality of life at 12 weeks than placebo.
  • A heart tablet for stage fright: propranolol blunts the shaking, racing heart and dry mouth of performance nerves. Musicians have used it for decades. Not for people with asthma.
  • A bleeding drug for dark skin patches: tranexamic acid tablets, used for heavy periods, lighten melasma over about 12 weeks. Not for people with a history of blood clots.
  • An antidepressant for the gut: low-dose amitriptyline improved IBS symptoms in a large UK primary care trial (ATLANTIS, 2023).
  • Nerve and blood pressure medicines for hot flushes: gabapentin and clonidine reduce menopausal hot flushes when hormone therapy isn’t suitable.
  • Thalidomide’s comeback: infamous for birth defects in the 1960s, it is now approved for myeloma (a blood cancer) and a leprosy complication, under strict pregnancy-prevention programs.

Popular off-label uses that failed proper trials

  • Prazosin for PTSD nightmares: widely used after small positive trials, but a large 2018 trial in 304 veterans found no benefit over placebo.
  • Low-dose naltrexone for fibromyalgia: small early trials looked promising; the largest placebo-controlled trial (2024) found no difference in pain.
  • The lesson: small, open studies often look better than the truth. Bigger, placebo-controlled trials settle it.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Is this use approved, or off-label?
  • What’s the evidence: big trials, small studies, or experience?
  • What are the risks, and how will we know if it’s working?
  • How long should I try it before we stop?
  • Will it cost more without a PBS subsidy?

Stay safe

  • Don’t take someone else’s prescription, or buy prescription medicines online without a script.
  • Tell every doctor and pharmacist about everything you take, including supplements.

Common questions

What does off-label mean?

Using a medicine for a condition, dose, age group or route that isn’t listed in its approved product information. It’s legal when there’s a good clinical reason.

Is off-label prescribing legal in Australia?

Yes. Doctors can prescribe off-label with a sound reason, after explaining it and getting your consent. Companies can’t advertise off-label uses.

Is off-label use safe?

It depends on the medicine and the evidence. Some off-label uses are standard care backed by big trials; others rest on small studies. Ask what the evidence is.

Why might an off-label medicine cost more?

The PBS subsidises medicines for specific approved uses. Off-label use often isn’t covered, so you may pay the full private price.
