Fact sheet

Paronychia

A sore, inflamed nail fold. Acute: comes on over days, usually an infection after nail biting, a hangnail or a manicure. Chronic: lasts 6+ weeks, usually from wet hands, and behaves like dermatitis.

The paronychia ladder

Acute and chronic paronychia are treated differently, so follow the steps for your type. Most acute cases settle within days.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careAcute: warm soaksWarm water or diluted antiseptic, 10–15 minutes, 3–4 times a day for the first few days.
    Works
    Not tested in trials; settles many mild, early infections
    Cost
    Free, or a few dollars for antiseptic
    How long
    Expect improvement within 2 days
    Watch for
    A pus pocket, spreading redness, or no better in 2 days: see a GP.

    Sitting with your finger in a mug is legitimate medical care.

  2. Step 2 · Self-careChronic: keep hands dryThe main treatment. Cotton-lined waterproof gloves for wet work, dry hands well, moisturise often.
    Works
    No trials, but every guideline’s foundation: without it, nothing else lasts
    Cost
    About $10–20 for gloves and a good hand cream
    How long
    Months: nail folds heal slowly
    Watch for
    Pushing back or cutting cuticles undoes the good work.

    The cuticle is a seal, not a design flaw.

  3. Step 3 · PharmacyAcute: antiseptic ointment or paintFor a mild infection with no pus.
    Works
    Evidence weak (no good trials); may help mild cases
    Cost
    About $8–15
    How long
    A few days
    Watch for
    Skin irritation. It can’t fix a pus pocket.

    No ointment can drain pus, however hopefully you apply it.

  4. Step 4 · PrescriptionChronic: strong steroid ointmentApplied to the nail fold to calm the dermatitis-like inflammation.
    Works
    Improved or cured about 6 in 10, vs 2 to 3 in 10 on antifungal tablets (one small trial)
    Cost
    Under the $25 PBS co-payment, about $15–25 a tube
    How long
    2–4 weeks, then review
    Watch for
    Skin thinning with long use. Antifungals only if yeast is confirmed on testing.

    Chronic paronychia is more eczema than infection. Antifungal roulette rarely wins.

  5. Step 5 · PrescriptionAcute: antibiotic tabletsSuch as cefalexin or flucloxacillin, if redness is spreading or it isn’t settling.
    Works
    Not well tested in trials; usually settles the infection within days
    Cost
    Under the $25 PBS co-payment, about $12–20
    How long
    Usually 5–7 days
    Watch for
    Nausea, diarrhoea, rash. Tell your doctor about any penicillin allergy.

    Many mild cases don’t need them. Pus usually needs a drain, not just a tablet.

  6. Step 6 · ProcedureAcute: draining the pusA small cut under local anaesthetic, done by a doctor.
    Works
    No trials, but works for most: fast relief, often within hours; often all that’s needed
    Cost
    GP procedure, often bulk-billed or a gap of about $40–90
    How long
    One visit, then a few days of soaks and dressings
    Watch for
    The numbing needle stings. Never DIY it.

    Bathroom surgery with a sewing needle: zero stars. Ten-minute GP procedure: five.

Costs are rough Australian prices in October 2026 and vary by pharmacy. PBS co-payment: $25, or $7.70 with a concession card.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Antibiotic tablets for chronic paronychiaThe long-term type is inflammation, not infection, so antibiotics don’t fix it. Dry hands and steroid ointment do.
  • Antifungal tablets for chronic paronychiaA trial found steroid cream worked better. Yeast is usually a bystander, not the culprit.
  • DIY lancing with a needleCan push infection deeper. Doctors drain it with sterile kit and local anaesthetic for a reason.
  • Tea tree oilNo trials for paronychia, and it can irritate already inflamed skin.

What you can do

  • Don’t bite your nails, pick hangnails or cut your cuticles: the cuticle seals out germs.
  • Moisturise your hands and nail folds often.
  • Cut toenails straight across, and wear shoes with enough toe room.
  • If you have diabetes, check your feet daily.

See your GP if

  • Pain, swelling or pus not improving after 2 days of soaks
  • You have diabetes, poor circulation or a weak immune system
  • Small blisters around the nail (could be a cold-sore virus, which needs different treatment)
  • A nail fold that hasn’t healed after 6 weeks, or keeps coming back

See a doctor the same day for red streaks up the hand or foot, fever, a very swollen fingertip pad, or pain on moving the finger.

Common questions

How do I treat a mild nail fold infection at home?

Soak in warm water, or a diluted antiseptic, for 10 to 15 minutes 3 to 4 times a day for the first few days. See your GP if it isn’t improving after 2 days.

Does paronychia need antibiotics?

Many mild cases don’t. Antibiotic tablets are used if redness is spreading or it isn’t settling, and a doctor can drain a pus pocket for quick relief.

What is chronic paronychia?

Nail fold inflammation lasting 6 weeks or more, usually in people whose hands are often wet. It behaves more like dermatitis than an infection, so keeping hands dry and a strong steroid ointment are the main treatments.
