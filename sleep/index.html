Fact sheet

Trouble Sleeping

Difficulty getting to sleep, staying asleep or waking too early, with tiredness during the day. Short spells after stress are common and usually pass. Insomnia lasting 3 months or more is best treated with a specific talking therapy (CBT-i), not tablets.

Treatment options

Ratings show how strong the research is.

  • Strong evidenceCBT for insomnia (CBT-i)The first-line treatment. Usually 4 to 8 sessions with a psychologist, or a free online program such as This Way Up. Helps most people, and the benefits last.
  • Moderate evidenceOrexin-blocking tabletsNewer prescription medicines (such as suvorexant, lemborexant or daridorexant) when CBT-i isn’t enough. Can cause next-day drowsiness.
  • Limited evidenceMelatoninSlow-release tablets give a small benefit for people over 55, from a pharmacy, for a short course. Other forms need a prescription.
  • Limited evidenceSleep hygiene aloneGood habits help, but on their own rarely fix long-term insomnia.
  • Limited evidenceShort-term sleeping tabletsSuch as temazepam or zolpidem, for a few nights in a crisis. Less effective than CBT-i and they stop working quickly.
  • Not recommendedLong-term sleeping tabletsRisk of dependence, falls, memory problems and next-day drowsiness, especially in older people.
  • Not recommendedSedating antihistaminesPharmacy sleep aids such as doxylamine cause next-day grogginess and aren’t suitable for older people.

What you can do

  • Get up at the same time every day, even after a bad night and on weekends.
  • Go to bed only when sleepy. If you can’t sleep after about 20 minutes, get up, do something calm in dim light, and return when sleepy.
  • Get outdoor light in the morning. Keep screens and lights low in the hour before bed.
  • No caffeine after midday. Alcohol worsens sleep later in the night.
  • Keep naps under 30 minutes and before 3 pm.
  • If worries keep you awake, set aside a "worry time" earlier in the evening to write down plans.

See your GP if

  • Trouble sleeping most nights for 3 months or more
  • Loud snoring, gasping or pauses in breathing at night, or dozing while driving
  • Low mood, anxiety, pain or restless legs keep you awake
  • You rely on sleeping tablets or alcohol to sleep

Don’t drive if you feel drowsy. Don’t stop long-term sleeping tablets suddenly: ask your GP about reducing them slowly.

Common questions

What is the best treatment for insomnia?

CBT for insomnia (CBT-i), usually 4 to 8 sessions with a psychologist or a free online program such as This Way Up. It helps most people, and the benefits last.

Does melatonin help you sleep?

Slow-release melatonin gives a small benefit for people over 55, as a short course from a pharmacy. Other forms need a prescription.

Are sleeping tablets safe long term?

No. Long-term use risks dependence, falls, memory problems and next-day drowsiness, especially in older people. Don’t stop them suddenly: ask your GP about reducing slowly.

What should I do if I can’t fall asleep?

If you’re still awake after about 20 minutes, get up, do something calm in dim light, and return when sleepy. Get up at the same time every day, even after a bad night.
