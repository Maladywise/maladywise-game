Testicular Lumps and Self-Checks

Most lumps in the scrotum aren’t cancer. But testicular cancer is the cancer of young men, and with 98% surviving five years, finding it early is the whole game. A monthly 2-minute check is all it takes.

How to check

  • After a warm shower, when the skin is relaxed.
  • Hold the scrotum in your palm. Roll each testicle gently between your thumb and fingers.
  • A healthy testicle feels firm and smooth, like a hard-boiled egg without its shell.
  • Normal: one testicle slightly bigger, the left hanging lower, and a soft, coiled tube at the back of each (the epididymis).
  • Look for a hard lump, swelling or change from how it usually feels. Check monthly so you know your normal.

Common harmless lumps

  • Epididymal cyst: a smooth, pea-sized fluid lump, separate from the testicle. Very common and harmless.
  • Hydrocele: fluid around the testicle, making a soft, painless swelling. Can be drained or repaired if large.
  • Varicocele: enlarged veins, like a ‘bag of worms’ above the testicle.
  • Inguinal hernia: a bulge from the groin into the scrotum, often bigger when coughing or standing.

Testicular cancer

  • About 1,040 Australians are diagnosed each year, with the peak at ages 30 to 34. It’s one of the most common cancers in young men.
  • Usually a painless, hard lump or swelling in the testicle, sometimes a dull ache or heaviness.
  • Higher risk: an undescended testicle as a baby, a past testicular cancer, or a father or brother who had it.
  • Treatment usually starts with removing the testicle. 98% of men are alive five years after diagnosis.
  • One remaining testicle usually makes enough testosterone and sperm. Sperm can be banked before treatment.

When to act

  • Any hard lump or change in a testicle: see your GP soon. An ultrasound gives a quick answer.
  • Sudden, severe testicle pain, often with nausea or vomiting, especially in teens: go to hospital now. This may be testicular torsion, where the testicle twists and loses its blood supply. Surgery within about 6 hours gives the best chance of saving it.
  • A painful, red, swollen testicle with fever or burning when weeing: likely infection (epididymitis), so see a doctor the same day.

Common questions

How often should I check my testicles?

Monthly is a good habit. The point is to know what’s normal for you, so you notice any change.

Is a lump on my testicle cancer?

Most scrotal lumps aren’t cancer: cysts, fluid and veins are far more common. But a hard lump in the testicle itself needs checking with your GP, usually with an ultrasound.

What age does testicular cancer happen?

It’s most common in young men, peaking at ages 30 to 34 in Australia, but it can happen at any age.

Is sudden testicle pain an emergency?

Yes. Sudden, severe pain may be torsion, a twisted testicle. Go to hospital straight away: surgery within hours can save the testicle.
