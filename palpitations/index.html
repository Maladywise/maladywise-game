Heart Palpitations

A thud, a flutter, a skipped beat, or a heart that suddenly races. Palpitations are common and usually harmless, often set off by caffeine, stress or lack of sleep. Some need checking, and a few need urgent help.

What they feel like: racing, pounding, fluttering or irregular beats, felt in the chest, neck or throat. They can last seconds, minutes or longer.

Common triggers: caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, recreational drugs such as cocaine and amphetamines, stress and anxiety, lack of sleep, hard exercise, fever and some medicines, including asthma relievers and ADHD medicines. Pregnancy and menopause can bring them on too.

Medical causes: skipped or extra beats (ectopic beats), heart rhythm problems such as atrial fibrillation, an overactive thyroid, anaemia, low blood sugar and, less often, heart valve or heart muscle problems.

The palpitations ladder

Most palpitations need trigger-spotting, not treatment. The key test is catching the heart rhythm during symptoms.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Energy drinks and high-caffeine pre-workoutsCaffeine is a common trigger. Cutting back is cheaper than any test.
  • Cigarettes, vapes and nicotine pouchesNicotine is a known trigger.
  • Recreational stimulantsCocaine and amphetamines can cause palpitations and dangerous heart rhythms.

What you can do

  • Keep a diary of episodes: time, length, what you were doing, and any triggers.
  • Cut back on caffeine and alcohol, and quit smoking. Our quitting smoking fact sheet can help.
  • Prioritise sleep and stress management.
  • If anxiety is a big part of it, read our anxiety fact sheet.
  • Find out whether anyone in your family has a heart rhythm problem.

See your GP if

  • Palpitations keep coming back or are happening more often
  • They last more than a few minutes
  • You have a heart condition, or a family history of heart rhythm problems
  • You take a medicine, such as an asthma reliever or ADHD medicine, that might be a trigger

Call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number if palpitations come with chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness or light-headedness, or fainting or blacking out, or if they don’t go away. Don’t drive yourself to hospital.

Common questions

Are heart palpitations dangerous?

Usually not. Most are caused by triggers like caffeine, stress or lack of sleep, or harmless extra beats. See a doctor if they keep happening or last more than a few minutes, and call an ambulance if they come with chest pain, breathlessness or fainting.

What does a skipped heartbeat mean?

Usually an extra beat (an ectopic beat) followed by a pause, which makes the next beat feel stronger. They are common and usually harmless, but a GP can check with an ECG.

Can anxiety cause palpitations?

Yes. Stress, anxiety and panic attacks are common causes. A doctor may still check your heart to be sure.

What tests are done for palpitations?

An ECG and blood tests first. If episodes come and go, a heart monitor worn for 1 to 2 days or longer can catch the rhythm. Some people also have a heart ultrasound.

Why do I get palpitations at night?

When it’s quiet and you’re lying down, especially on your left side, you notice your heartbeat more. Caffeine, alcohol, a big meal, stress or reflux late in the day can trigger extra beats. If they wake you or come with other symptoms, get them checked.

Can caffeine cause palpitations?

Large amounts can, especially energy drinks. Moderate coffee (a few cups a day) doesn’t seem to cause harmful heart rhythms, though one trial found slightly more harmless extra beats on coffee days. If you notice a link, cut back and see if it helps.

Can menopause cause palpitations?

Yes. Palpitations are common in perimenopause, often alongside hot flushes and poor sleep. Your doctor may still do an ECG and blood tests (such as thyroid and iron) to rule out other causes.
