  1. #36 · Sunday 22 November 2026 Genius

    Theme: 22q and the chromosome club

    1. Today is 22q11.2 Awareness Day. Roughly how often does the deletion occur in live births?

      Answer: About 1 in 4,000

      One of the most common microdeletions in people, yet it often takes years to be recognised.

    2. The old mnemonic for 22q features borrowed the title of a Joseph Heller novel. What was it?

      Answer: CATCH-22

      Cardiac, Abnormal facies, Thymic hypoplasia, Cleft palate, Hypocalcaemia. Clinicians and families have mostly retired it, and fair enough.

    3. The thymus and inferior parathyroid glands develop from which pharyngeal pouch?

      Answer: The third

      Which explains the immune and calcium problems. Inferior glands, third pouch: embryology enjoys being confusing.

    4. Which gene in the deleted region is thought to drive many of the physical features?

      Answer: TBX1

      A transcription factor needed for pharyngeal arch development. Mice missing it show very similar heart and arch defects.

    5. Which heart lesion has an especially strong link with 22q11.2 deletion?

      Answer: Interrupted aortic arch type B

      Around half of babies with this rare lesion have the deletion, so genetic testing is routine.

    6. About what share of adults with 22q11.2 deletion develop a schizophrenia spectrum condition?

      Answer: About 1 in 4

      One of the strongest known genetic risk factors, and most people with the deletion don’t develop it.

    7. Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes involve the same part of chromosome 15. Angelman usually results when the missing copy came from the?

      Answer: Mother

      This is genomic imprinting. Prader-Willi is the one with the missing paternal copy.

    8. French scientist Marthe Gautier did key lab work showing the extra chromosome in Down syndrome. Credit went largely to?

      Answer: Jérôme Lejeune

      She spoke up about it decades later, in 2009. A very old story in science, sadly.

    9. Daraxonrasib, the pancreatic cancer pill in this week's news, blocks RAS proteins. Roughly what share of pancreatic cancers are driven by RAS changes?

      Answer: About 9 in 10

      Mostly KRAS, a gene on chromosome 12. In a phase 3 trial in previously treated metastatic disease, median survival was about 13 months versus about 7 on chemotherapy.

    10. In 1973, Janet Rowley showed the Philadelphia chromosome in chronic myeloid leukaemia comes from?

      Answer: A swap between chromosomes 9 and 22

      She reportedly worked it out with photos on her dining table, and proved chromosome swaps can cause cancer.

  2. #35 · Saturday 21 November 2026 Hard

    Theme: Legionella, and the week's health news

    1. Today is World Hello Day. Brothers Brian and Michael McCormack started it in 1973 in response to which conflict?

      Answer: The Yom Kippur War

      Their idea: greet ten people and let leaders notice that talking beats fighting. Sadly under-prescribed.

    2. Legionnaires’ disease got its name from a 1976 outbreak at a Philadelphia convention of?

      Answer: US military veterans (the American Legion)

      The convention marked the US Bicentennial. This week UKHSA reported two deaths among people with Legionnaires’ disease in Rotherham, England; the public risk there is described as low.

    3. The 1976 bug was finally identified in January 1977 by Joseph McDade, working at?

      Answer: The CDC

      He went back to old slides over the Christmas holidays and spotted rod-shaped bacteria he had dismissed months earlier.

    4. In ponds, rivers and pipes, Legionella bacteria often multiply inside?

      Answer: Free-living amoebae

      Amoebae act as Trojan horses, shielding the bacteria from chlorine. It’s also good practice for invading our lung macrophages.

    5. Legionella multiplies best in water at roughly what temperature range?

      Answer: 20–45 °C

      Lukewarm is the danger zone, which is why stored hot water is kept at 60 °C or more and cold water kept cold.

    6. In Australia, bags of which garden product carry a Legionella health warning?

      Answer: Potting mix

      Legionella longbeachae lives in potting mix and compost and causes many Australian cases. Advice: wet the mix, wear gloves and a mask, wash hands.

    7. The common urine antigen test for Legionella reliably detects only?

      Answer: L. pneumophila serogroup 1

      Serogroup 1 causes most cases in Europe and the US. A negative test doesn’t rule out other types, including longbeachae, so cultures or PCR still matter.

    8. Pontiac fever, caused by the same bacteria as Legionnaires’ disease, is?

      Answer: A mild flu-like illness without pneumonia

      Named after a 1968 outbreak in a Pontiac, Michigan health department building. Most people recover in a few days.

    9. On 3 November the TGA approved a once-daily tablet form of which weekly weight-loss injection drug?

      Answer: Semaglutide

      It’s taken on an empty stomach each morning. A different GLP-1 pill, orforglipron, was approved on 17 November.

    10. Hospitals urge soap and water rather than alcohol gel during norovirus outbreaks because norovirus?

      Answer: Has no fatty envelope, so alcohol works poorly on it

      Envelope-free viruses shrug off alcohol. Rubbing and rinsing physically removes them.

  3. #34 · Friday 20 November 2026 Hard

    Theme: TB, and the week’s health news

    1. World TB Day is 24 March because on that date in 1882 a German scientist announced he had found the germ that causes TB. Who?

      Answer: Robert Koch

      His Berlin lecture identified Mycobacterium tuberculosis. He won the Nobel Prize in 1905 for his TB work.

    2. This week about 100 young children at three Adelaide childcare centres are being offered TB skin tests after a worker was diagnosed. The purified tuberculin these tests rely on was developed in the 1930s by?

      Answer: Florence Seibert

      SA Health says the risk is low and the worker is recovering well. Seibert, an American biochemist who had polio as a child, purified the protein derivative (PPD) that became the international standard for the skin test in the 1940s.

    3. The BCG vaccine, still given to babies in many countries, is a weakened TB germ that was first taken from?

      Answer: A cow

      Mycobacterium bovis, from a cow with tuberculous mastitis. Calmette and Guérin grew it through 230 subcultures over 13 years before the first baby was vaccinated in 1921.

    4. Australia has one of the world’s lowest TB rates. Its 2026–2030 national TB plan adds which word to ‘elimination’ in its title?

      Answer: Equity

      The burden isn’t shared fairly: people born in high-TB countries and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities carry more of it.

    5. TB bacteria keep their red dye even when washed with acid-alcohol, so they’re called ‘acid-fast’. What in their cell wall does this?

      Answer: Mycolic acids

      The waxy, mycolic acid-rich wall holds the carbolfuchsin in a Ziehl–Neelsen stain. It also makes the bacteria hardy and slow-growing.

    6. Which vitamin is often given alongside the TB drug isoniazid to protect the nerves?

      Answer: Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine)

      Isoniazid can cause peripheral neuropathy, especially with diabetes, poor nutrition, heavy drinking, HIV or pregnancy.

    7. It’s World AMR Awareness Week. In TB, ‘multidrug-resistant’ means the germ resists at least which two drugs?

      Answer: Isoniazid and rifampicin

      They’re the two most powerful first-line TB drugs, so losing both means longer, different drug combinations.

    8. Before effective antibiotics, some TB patients had ‘plombage’: the lung was collapsed to rest it, and the space was packed with?

      Answer: Plastic (Lucite) balls

      Mid-20th-century collapse therapy. Fat, oil, wax and bone were also used, and the balls still turn up on some older people’s chest X-rays.

    9. An AMA report released this week found reports of critical antibiotic resistance in Australia rose by about how much in 2024?

      Answer: A quarter

      Up 25.2%, even though antibiotic use is about 21% lower than a decade ago. The AMA wants stronger stewardship wherever antibiotics are prescribed.

    10. Your smartwatch’s HRV figure, heart rate variability, measures?

      Answer: Variation in time between heartbeats

      A healthy heart doesn’t tick like a metronome. Higher resting HRV tends to go with fitness and resilience; lower HRV is linked with higher cardiovascular risk.

  4. #33 · Thursday 19 November 2026 Medium

    Theme: Plumbing, inside and out

    1. In 1854, John Snow traced a London cholera outbreak to a water pump on which street?

      Answer: Broad Street

      He had the pump handle removed. The street is now Broadwick Street in Soho, where a replica pump marks the spot.

    2. Which nurse’s ‘rose diagrams’ showed that soldiers in the Crimean War died far more often from disease and poor sanitation than from wounds?

      Answer: Florence Nightingale

      Her charts pushed the government to clean up army hospitals. In 1858 she became the first woman elected to the Royal Statistical Society.

    3. London’s ‘Great Stink’ of 1858 led to a vast new sewer network designed by which engineer?

      Answer: Joseph Bazalgette

      Bazalgette made his pipes bigger than needed, just in case. Good call: they still carry London’s sewage today.

    4. Cholera’s ‘rice-water’ stools happen because its toxin makes the gut?

      Answer: Pour out salt and water

      The toxin jams a chloride channel open and water follows. Oral rehydration solution, just sugar, salt and water, has saved millions of lives.

    5. In 2022, poliovirus turned up in samples from London’s sewers. What did health officials then offer every child aged 1 to 9 in London?

      Answer: An extra polio vaccine dose

      Testing sewage catches viruses before anyone falls ill, which is why polio still makes headlines when it shows up. It has turned up in London’s wastewater again since. Toilets: also an early-warning system.

    6. The main pancreatic duct usually joins the common bile duct to drain into the?

      Answer: Duodenum

      They meet at the ampulla of Vater, guarded by the sphincter of Oddi. Body plumbing at its finest.

    7. A tumour in the head of the pancreas can squash the bile duct. A classic early sign is?

      Answer: Painless jaundice

      Bile backs up into the blood: yellow skin and eyes, dark urine, pale stools and itch. Jaundice without pain always deserves prompt tests.

    8. Courvoisier’s law: a jaundiced patient with a palpable, non-tender gallbladder is unlikely to have?

      Answer: Gallstones

      Stones scar the gallbladder so it can’t stretch. A big, smooth one suggests a slower blockage, often a tumour. Swiss surgeon Ludwig Courvoisier described it in 1890.

    9. A ‘sponge on a string’ test, now trialled in some English pharmacies for people with long-term heartburn, looks for?

      Answer: Barrett’s oesophagus

      You swallow a capsule on a thread. It dissolves in the stomach and a small sponge collects cells as it’s pulled back up the food pipe, all in under 10 minutes. Cambridge’s Professor Rebecca Fitzgerald led its development over about 20 years.

    10. The insulin-making islets of Langerhans make up roughly what share of the pancreas?

      Answer: About 1–2%

      The rest is exocrine tissue, pumping digestive enzymes down the duct. Paul Langerhans spotted the islets as a medical student in 1869. GLP-1 medicines work partly by nudging their beta cells to release insulin after meals; in November 2026 Australia’s TGA approved the first GLP-1 pill that isn’t a peptide (orforglipron).

  5. #32 · Wednesday 18 November 2026 Einstein

    Theme: Breathless and resistant

    1. In GOLD spirometric grading, GOLD 3 (severe) COPD means a post-bronchodilator FEV1 of?

      Answer: 30–49% predicted

      GOLD 1 is 80% or more, 2 is 50–79%, 4 is under 30%. Today is World COPD Day.

    2. The BODE index predicts death in COPD. Besides BMI, FEV1 and breathlessness, it scores?

      Answer: Six-minute walk distance

      Celli and colleagues, NEJM 2004. The E is exercise capacity.

    3. Long-term oxygen improves survival in hypoxaemic COPD. The two landmark trials were NOTT and?

      Answer: The MRC trial

      Both published around 1980–81. UPLIFT and TORCH tested inhalers; ECLIPSE was an observational cohort.

    4. GOLD says inhaled corticosteroids help COPD exacerbations most when blood eosinophils are at least?

      Answer: 300 cells/µL

      Under 100 cells/µL, benefit is small and pneumonia risk still applies.

    5. The emphysema of alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency is classically?

      Answer: Panacinar, worst in the lower lobes

      Smoking-related emphysema is usually centriacinar and upper-lobe.

    6. In 1940, before purified penicillin had been given to a single patient, Abraham and Chain described a bacterial enzyme that destroys it, called?

      Answer: Penicillinase

      Resistance was spotted before the drug reached the clinic. World AMR Awareness Week starts today.

    7. A November 2026 Oxford study made ‘molecular glues’ that tag TEM β-lactamases in E. coli for destruction by which periplasmic protease?

      Answer: DegP

      Built from the β-lactamase inhibitor tazobactam, the degraders boosted piperacillin against resistant E. coli in the lab (Angewandte Chemie, 2026). Lon and ClpXP work in the cytoplasm; OmpT sits in the outer membrane.

    8. The mcr-1 gene, reported from China in 2015, spreads resistance to which last-line antibiotic?

      Answer: Colistin

      It sits on a plasmid, so it moves between bacteria. It was found in pigs, raw meat and people.

    9. The GRAM study (Lancet, 2022) estimated deaths directly attributable to bacterial AMR in 2019 at about?

      Answer: 1.27 million

      4.95 million deaths were associated with AMR. 1.27 million were directly caused by it. This week WHO warned AMR could cause 39 million deaths over the next 25 years, and that in 2023 one in six lab-confirmed bacterial infections reported to it was resistant.

    10. VanA vancomycin-resistant enterococci swap the cell-wall end D-Ala-D-Ala for?

      Answer: D-Ala-D-Lac

      Vancomycin binds D-Ala-D-Lac about 1,000 times less tightly. D-Ala-D-Ser is the low-level VanC trick.

  7. #30 · Monday 16 November 2026 Easy

    Theme: Mind games for Psychology Week

    1. It’s Psychology Week in Australia. Pavlov’s dogs learned to drool at a sound that meant food was coming. This kind of learning is called?

      Answer: Classical conditioning

      Operant conditioning, learning from rewards and punishments, was B. F. Skinner’s territory.

    2. Psychologist Elizabeth Loftus is famous for showing that memories can be?

      Answer: Changed by misleading questions

      In her 1974 car-crash study, asking how fast the cars ‘smashed’ rather than ‘hit’ raised people’s speed estimates.

    3. Henry Molaison (patient H.M.) couldn’t form new memories after 1953 epilepsy surgery removed much of both his?

      Answer: Hippocampi

      Psychologist Brenda Milner showed he could still learn a new skill, mirror drawing, without remembering ever practising it.

    4. Seeing crowds of tiny people is called a ‘Lilliputian’ hallucination. The name comes from?

      Answer: Gulliver’s Travels

      In 2026 Utah mycologists linked a cluster of them to Lanmaoa asiatica, a mushroom eaten undercooked in Yunnan and the Philippines. It has no psilocybin, so an unknown compound may be behind it.

    5. In a 2026 study, carrion crows judged moving dots about twice as fast as humans do. The bird brain area doing the deciding is thought to work like our?

      Answer: Prefrontal cortex

      It’s called the nidopallium caudolaterale. Its neurons ‘made the call’ about 70 milliseconds after the dots appeared.

    6. Tourette syndrome is named after the French neurologist who described it in 1885. Its hallmark is?

      Answer: Motor and vocal tics

      Involuntary swearing (coprolalia) gets all the attention but affects only a minority. Tics often ease by adulthood.

    7. Today is also Mediterranean Diet Day. In Australia’s SMILES trial (2017), a modified Mediterranean diet improved?

      Answer: Depression

      Led by Professor Felice Jacka. About a third of the diet group went into remission.

    8. Saying the ink colour of the word RED printed in blue is weirdly slow. This is the?

      Answer: Stroop effect

      Described by John Ridley Stroop in 1935. Reading is so automatic it trips up the colour-naming.

    9. Neuroscientist V. S. Ramachandran used a simple mirror box to ease?

      Answer: Phantom limb pain

      Watching the reflected ‘missing’ hand move seems to settle the brain’s mismatch between what it sees and what it feels.

    10. In a 2019 study, how long did university students need to pat dogs and cats to have lower levels of the stress hormone cortisol?

      Answer: About 10 minutes

      Led by Patricia Pendry at Washington State University, who measured it in saliva.

    5. Waddell’s triad in a child hit by a car is femoral fracture, chest or abdominal injury and?

    7. A lucid interval then rapid decline, with a lens-shaped bleed on CT, is classically from which vessel?

    9. Measles outbreaks are in the news again. Measles can wipe out much of the antibody memory you had to other germs. This is called?

      Answer: Immune amnesia

      A 2019 Science study found measles erased 11% to 73% of children’s existing antibodies, leaving them open to infections they’d already beaten. The MMR vaccine prevents it.

    10. The UN’s Decade of Action for Road Safety 2021–2030 aims to cut road deaths and injuries by at least?

      Answer: Half

      Speed limits, helmets, safer roads and fast trauma care all help.

  10. #27 · Friday 13 November 2026 Hard

    Theme: Symbols, signs and stimulants

    1. Medicine’s proper symbol is the rod of Asclepius: one snake, one staff. The winged, two-snake caduceus belongs to?

      Answer: Hermes, messenger of the gods

      The caduceus is Hermes’ herald’s staff, tied to trade, travel and messages rather than healing. Two snakes, zero medical credentials.

    2. A 2026 American Heart Association abstract on FDA adverse-event reports linked energy drinks and stimulant supplements to how many times more cardiac arrest reports than non-stimulant products?

      Answer: About 11 times

      Voluntary reports show an association, not proof of cause: nobody knows how many people had them without a problem. About 45% of the heart reports involved adults aged 18 to 34.

    3. The caduceus mix-up in American medicine is often traced to which body putting it on uniforms in 1902?

      Answer: The US Army Medical Corps

      Captain Frederick Reynolds lobbied for it, and the Army put the caduceus on its collars in 1902. The AMA, Britain’s RAMC and WHO stuck with Asclepius, so that guess was in good company, just wrong.

    4. Caffeine keeps you alert mainly by blocking receptors for which chemical that builds up in the brain while you’re awake?

      Answer: Adenosine

      Adenosine slowly nudges you towards sleep; caffeine sits in its receptors without switching them on. Friedrich Runge first isolated fairly pure caffeine in 1819.

    5. The six arms of the blue Star of Life on ambulances stand for?

      Answer: Six stages of emergency care

      Detection, reporting, response, on-scene care, care in transit and transfer to definitive care, a meaning added by Leo Schwartz at the US road safety agency NHTSA. Not a tribute to six heroic paramedics, sadly.

    6. Taurine, a regular on energy-drink labels, is named after the Latin for bull or ox because it was first isolated in 1827 from?

      Answer: Ox bile

      Friedrich Tiedemann and Leopold Gmelin found it in ox bile. Latin taurus means bull or ox. Your own body makes taurine too.

    7. In 2005, a third protective emblem with no religious or national meaning joined the red cross and red crescent. It’s the red?

      Answer: Crystal

      The red crystal, a red square stood on its point, came in with a third Additional Protocol to the Geneva Conventions in December 2005. The lion and sun was real, but Iran’s, and retired long before.

    8. A 35-year US study (PNAS, 2026) found a big acorn crop predicts more Lyme-carrying nymphal ticks two years later. The key middle link?

      Answer: A boom in mice

      Acorns feed mice, and mice are very good at passing Borrelia burgdorferi to young ticks. Surprisingly, deer numbers showed no clear link with nymph numbers.

    9. WHO’s emblem, chosen at the first World Health Assembly in 1948, sets the UN world map behind?

      Answer: A staff with one snake coiled around it

      It’s the rod of Asclepius, so WHO got the snake count right. If you went for the caduceus, so have plenty of logo designers.

    10. DNA of which livestock virus was recently recovered from the parchment of medieval books, including the 1,000-year-old York Gospels?

      Answer: Sheeppox virus

      Parchment is animal skin, so the pages kept the virus’s DNA. The Science Advances study traced its lineage back about 3,500 years. Sheeppox doesn’t infect humans.

  12. #25 · Wednesday 11 November 2026 Medium

    Theme: War and medicine

    1. Which New Zealand-born surgeon pioneered facial reconstruction for WWI soldiers at Sidcup?

      Answer: Harold Gillies

      He’s often called the father of modern plastic surgery. McIndoe, his cousin, led WWII burns care.

    2. The 1918 flu pandemic tore through troops in the war’s final months. What actually caused most of its deaths?

      Answer: Secondary bacterial pneumonia

      A 2008 NIH study of autopsy samples found most victims had bacterial pneumonia on top of the flu, in an era before antibiotics. World Pneumonia Day is 12 November.

    3. Which US Army doctor set up the first blood depots, storing blood ahead of battle, on the Western Front in 1917?

      Answer: Oswald Robertson

      Landsteiner found blood groups; Drew ran WWII’s Blood for Britain.

    4. Napoleon’s surgeon who introduced ‘flying ambulances’ and sorting the wounded by severity was?

      Answer: Dominique-Jean Larrey

      Triage comes from the French ‘trier’, to sort.

    5. In 1900, after the Spanish–American War, a US Army commission in Cuba led by Walter Reed proved which disease is spread by mosquitoes?

      Answer: Yellow fever

      They tested Cuban doctor Carlos Finlay’s idea: the Aedes aegypti mosquito. It’s in the news again, with WHO reporting 67 confirmed cases in nine African countries this year (to mid-October). One dose of vaccine gives lifelong protection.

    6. WWI ‘shell shock’ was eventually recognised in 1980 when DSM-III added?

      Answer: Post-traumatic stress disorder

      Many men with shell shock were treated as cowards, and some were executed.

    7. Florence Nightingale showed deaths in the Crimean War with a chart called a?

      Answer: Polar area diagram

      Also called a coxcomb. It showed that infection killed far more soldiers than wounds did.

    8. Jamaican-born nurse Mary Seacole ran which establishment for soldiers in Crimea?
