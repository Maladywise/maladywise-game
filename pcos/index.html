Fact sheet

PCOS (now called PMOS)

A common hormone condition behind irregular periods, extra hair, acne and fertility struggles. Now renamed PMOS, because it’s about hormones and metabolism, not cysts. Treatment targets what bothers you most.

How common: about 1 in 8 women, and possibly more common in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women. Often missed or diagnosed late.

Signs: irregular or absent periods, extra facial or body hair, scalp hair thinning, acne, trouble losing weight, anxiety or low mood, and difficulty getting pregnant. It raises the long-term risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease.

Diagnosis: adults need 2 of 3: signs of high male-type hormones (hair, acne or a blood test), irregular ovulation, and polycystic ovaries on ultrasound or a high AMH blood test, once thyroid and other causes are ruled out. Teenagers need both of the first two; ultrasound isn’t used.

The new name: PMOS stands for polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome. Same condition, same diagnosis.

The PCOS ladder

There’s no single cure, so treatment is matched to your goal: regular periods, less hair, fertility, or lowering long-term diabetes and heart risk.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careActivity and healthy eating150–300 minutes of moderate activity a week (or 75–150 vigorous) plus strength work; about 250 minutes for modest weight loss.
    Works
    Recommended for everyone, mainly for metabolic health. No single diet beats the others
    Cost
    Free
    How long
    Ongoing
    Watch for
    Weight stigma helps no one; focus on habits, not just the scales.

    There is no PCOS diet. There’s the one you can live with.

  2. Step 2 · PrescriptionCombined pillRegulates periods and lowers male-type hormones.
    Works
    First choice for irregular periods, excess hair and acne
    Cost
    Many under the $25 PBS co-payment
    How long
    Ongoing; hair takes 6+ months to respond
    Watch for
    Not suitable for everyone, such as some with clot risks.

    Old, cheap and still first-line for good reason.

  3. Step 3 · PrescriptionMetforminA diabetes tablet used mainly for the metabolic side of PCOS, especially with a BMI of 25 or more.
    Works
    About as effective as an active lifestyle program for metabolic features
    Cost
    Under the $25 PBS co-payment, about $22–23
    How long
    Ongoing
    Watch for
    Stomach upset and diarrhoea; start low. More in our metformin guide.

    A supporting act, not a magic fix.

  4. Step 4 · PrescriptionHair and skin treatmentsLaser for facial hair; anti-androgen tablets if other treatments fail or aren’t suitable.
    Works
    Laser works for facial hair in some people; anti-androgens have a limited role
    Cost
    Laser courses cost hundreds to thousands of dollars
    How long
    Months
    Watch for
    Anti-androgens need reliable contraception.

    Hair is slow to leave. Be patient.

  5. Step 5 · PrescriptionWeight-loss medicinesMay be considered alongside lifestyle changes, using the same rules as for anyone else.
    Works
    Help with weight and metabolic health; see our weight sheet
    Cost
    Not on the PBS for weight loss; private scripts cost several hundred dollars a month
    How long
    Long term
    Watch for
    Not for pregnancy or when trying to conceive.

    A useful tool for some, not a requirement.

  6. Step 6 · SpecialistFertility helpLetrozole tablets to trigger ovulation, then other options, then IVF if needed.
    Works
    Letrozole is the first-line tablet to help ovulation
    Cost
    GP or specialist fees; IVF costs vary widely
    How long
    Months
    Watch for
    Metformin isn’t routinely used in pregnancy.

    PCOS makes pregnancy harder, not impossible.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Inositol supplementsLimited benefit, not recommended for fertility, and no particular type or dose has good evidence. Metformin does more for hair and waist.
  • Insulin blood testsNot recommended in routine care. They don’t change treatment.
  • AMH or ultrasound alone for diagnosisAn AMH test can’t diagnose PCOS on its own, and neither test is used in teenagers.
  • Home IPL hair-removal kitsNot shown to work for PCOS hair. Clinic laser has the evidence.

What you can do

  • Track your cycles in an app or diary.
  • Move most days and add strength work twice a week.
  • Ask about regular checks of blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol.
  • Tell your GP about low mood or anxiety; screening is recommended for everyone with PCOS.

See your GP if

  • Periods are irregular, very infrequent or absent
  • Extra hair, acne or hair thinning is bothering you
  • You’re trying to get pregnant
  • Low mood, anxiety or body image worries

See your GP promptly if new hair growth is rapid, or your voice deepens. Fast-changing male-type features point to other causes that need checking.

Common questions

Why has PCOS been renamed PMOS?

The new name, polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, reflects that it affects hormones and metabolism across the body, not just the ovaries. The condition and how it is diagnosed have not changed.

Can I get pregnant with PCOS?

Many people do. If you have trouble ovulating, letrozole tablets are the first-line treatment, with other options and IVF if needed.

Is there a special diet for PCOS?

No single diet has been shown to work better than others. Any healthy eating pattern you can keep up is fine, combined with regular activity.

Does inositol work for PCOS?

The benefit is limited. Guidelines say it can be considered if you prefer, but it is not recommended for fertility and no type or dose has good evidence.
