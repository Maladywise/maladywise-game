Fact sheet

Mastocytosis

Mastocytosis is a build-up of too many mast cells, the cells that release histamine in allergic reactions. In children it usually affects only the skin and often fades. In adults it’s more often systemic, involving the bone marrow. It’s different from MCAS, where mast cell numbers are normal.

Types

  • Cutaneous mastocytosis: mostly children, with brownish spots or a single lump (mastocytoma). Considered benign; about 67 to 80% resolve by early adulthood.
  • Systemic mastocytosis: mostly adults; mast cells collect in the bone marrow and sometimes the skin, gut, liver, spleen or lymph nodes.
  • Most adults have indolent systemic mastocytosis, with no organ damage and a good outlook. Rarer advanced forms (aggressive, with another blood disorder, mast cell leukaemia) need specialist cancer care.

Symptoms

  • Itch, flushing, hives; spots that swell and redden when rubbed (Darier’s sign)
  • Tummy cramps, diarrhoea, nausea
  • Light-headedness, fast heart rate, fainting
  • Anaphylaxis, especially after insect stings
  • Bone pain, osteoporosis and fractures in systemic disease
  • Common triggers: heat, friction, exercise, alcohol, stress, some medicines (opioids, NSAIDs, some anaesthetic drugs)

Tests

  • Baseline serum tryptase: almost always raised in systemic mastocytosis; above 20 ng/mL is one of the diagnostic criteria.
  • A raised tryptase isn’t always mastocytosis: hereditary alpha-tryptasaemia, a common inherited trait, also raises it.
  • KIT D816V mutation: found in about 80 to 95% of adults with systemic mastocytosis; sensitive blood tests can detect it.
  • Bone marrow biopsy confirms systemic disease; skin biopsy confirms cutaneous disease.
  • Bone density scan in adults with systemic disease.

Treatment

  • Avoid known triggers.
  • Daily H1 antihistamines, often with an H2 blocker (famotidine); oral cromoglicate for gut symptoms; montelukast for some.
  • Adrenaline autoinjector and an ASCIA Action Plan for anyone at risk of anaphylaxis; venom immunotherapy after sting anaphylaxis.
  • Treat osteoporosis (calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonates).
  • Advanced disease: targeted drugs such as midostaurin or avapritinib, given by specialists.
  • Tell anaesthetists and dentists before procedures.

When to see a doctor

  • Unexplained flushing, hives or fainting episodes
  • Severe reaction to a sting
  • A child with brown spots that swell when rubbed
  • Any anaphylaxis: call 000 and use adrenaline

Common questions

What is the difference between mastocytosis and MCAS?

Mastocytosis means too many mast cells. In MCAS, mast cell numbers are normal but they release chemicals inappropriately.

Does cutaneous mastocytosis go away?

In children it usually does: about two-thirds to four-fifths resolve by early adulthood.

Is systemic mastocytosis cancer?

It’s a clonal mast cell disorder. Most adults have the indolent form with a normal or near-normal outlook; rare advanced forms behave like blood cancers.

What tryptase level suggests mastocytosis?

A baseline tryptase above 20 ng/mL is one criterion for systemic mastocytosis, but other causes, including hereditary alpha-tryptasaemia, need to be considered.
