The Weekly Dose, 27 Nov 2026

A woman who hibernated, a bird flu death from dairy cows and a new gonorrhoea pill.

Bugs got smarter, sepsis shortcuts didn't pay off, and one fossil had a very modern jaw.

🤯 Weird and wonderful

  • A Dutch woman went into a hibernation-like state every winter for 30 years, her core temperature dropping as low as 31°C. At autopsy, her hypothalamus had about a tenth of the normal nerve connections. One case. The ground squirrels feel seen.
  • A 2-million-year-old Australopithecus jaw has a mandibular torus, the bony ridge dentists still spot inside lower jaws today. Harmless then, harmless now, cause still unknown. Some mysteries predate the dental chair.
  • In mice, the uterus squeezes the early embryo into a slight oval, and that squeeze decides which end becomes the head. Squashing embryos in a dish did the same. Mouse study, Science Advances. Body plan by hug.
  • A pickleball to the unprotected eye left a woman with permanent glaucoma three months later. One case report, BMJ Case Reports. Modern paddles send shots past 95 km/h. The "gentle" sport, apparently.

🩺 This week in health news

  • WHO reported 3 H5N1 bird flu cases in Washington dairy workers, including the first reported death after exposure to infected dairy cattle. The worker who died had other health conditions. No human-to-human spread. Risk to the public: low. The cows, meanwhile, keep breaking records nobody wanted.
  • WHO conditionally recommended zoliflodacin, a single-dose oral antibiotic, as an extra option for uncomplicated genital gonorrhoea. Ceftriaxone resistance turned up in 4 of 10 surveillance countries. The injection stays first-line. Gonorrhoea: still out-evolving us, one dose at a time.
  • The FDA approved ribitol, a simple sugar alcohol, as the first treatment for limb-girdle muscular dystrophy R9. In a 112-person trial, walking speed improved by 0.27 m/s versus placebo at 12 months (interim results). Sometimes the fix is sweet.
  • Upadacitinib became the first tablet approved in the US for nonsegmental vitiligo, from age 12. After 48 weeks, about 1 in 4 had 75% facial repigmentation, versus 6–7% on placebo. JAK-inhibitor safety warnings apply. Progress, with fine print.

📺 In the media

  • Hepatitis B vaccination on the first day of life fell from 80.6% of US newborns in 2020 to 65.6% in early 2026. What's actually true: the records study of 1.3 million babies shows the drop began in 2023, before this year's US policy change. Babies infected at birth have about a 90% chance of chronic infection.
  • "Heart attacks can be predicted 30 years ahead," said the headlines. What's actually true: across 113,612 people, average risk factors differed decades before events (blood pressure about 4 mmHg higher). Group averages, not a crystal ball. The real message: blood pressure at 30 counts.
  • Britain's big sex survey says about 1 in 3 people aged 16–24 have never had sex. What's actually true: Natsal-4 surveyed 6,719 people. Adults 16–44 now report about twice a month, down from four in 1990. Trends, not causes. Phones remain the leading suspect, without a conviction.
  • US flu hospitalisations are the highest for this point in the season since 2010–11. What's actually true: CDC says early doesn't mean worse. Severity is "low" so far, and 93% of tested H1N1 viruses match the vaccine. Early arrival, not a bigger monster.

🔬 New research

  • Antibiotics given in the ambulance didn't reduce sepsis deaths (18.0% versus 18.4%), and nor did extra fluids. Factorial RCT, about 2,000 patients, Ontario paramedics, JAMA (two papers). Why it matters: faster isn't always better, and early antibiotics made blood cultures less useful.
  • Endometriosis genetics mapped: 58 risk regions, 27 new, pointing to immune, inflammation and hormone pathways. Genome-wide study, about 1 million women, a third of non-European ancestry, Nature Genetics. Why it matters: hard biology for a condition still too often waved away.
  • Children exposed to ADHD medication in the womb had no higher risk of autism, ADHD or other neurodevelopmental conditions. Cohort, 3.6 million children in Denmark, Norway and Sweden, BMJ. Association, not cause. Why it matters: reassurance for decisions made with a doctor.
  • Loperamide, a cheap anti-diarrhoea pill, was linked to about 60% lower death rates in Ebola patients with diarrhoea. Pooled data, 5,509 patients, West Africa 2014–16, Lancet Global Health. Association, not cause. Why it matters: a possible tool where IV fluids run short. Big studies: Research hub.

🐝 Beyond humans

  • 42% of Aspergillus fungus samples from soil, air, crops and hospitals in Cameroon resisted a common antifungal. Farm fungicides are the likely driver. The same drugs treat lung infections in people. One Health, one problem.
  • Thin air at high altitude stopped Varroa mites breeding: 13 of 15 mite-infested hives near Lhasa survived two years; none elsewhere lasted one. 60 colonies, PLOS Biology. Low-oxygen hives worked too. Altitude sickness, for parasites.
  • "Vaccinating" frogs with live chytrid fungus lowered infection and deaths on re-exposure. Meta-analysis, 36 studies, 22 species, Nature Ecology & Evolution. Chytrid has driven about 90 presumed extinctions. Immunology works on amphibians too.
  • Europe's winter bird flu wave has begun: Poland lost 587 of 18,723 turkeys on one farm, and France raised its alert to the highest level. Indoor poultry, again. Watching birds now protects people later.

💡 Useful things

  • Travelling north for the holidays? Pack your flu jab. The northern flu season has started early. A yearly vaccine is recommended for most people from 6 months of age.
  • STI tests are quick, confidential and often just a urine sample. Gonorrhoea and chlamydia often cause no symptoms. With World AIDS Day on Tuesday, it's a good week to ask about an HIV test too.
  • Know your blood pressure in your 20s and 30s, not just your 60s. High blood pressure usually has no symptoms. A check at the GP or pharmacy takes minutes.
  • Ball sports and power tools: wear eye protection. A hit to the eye that causes pain, blurred vision or blood in the eye needs same-day medical care.

🦓 Zebras

  • FKRP limb-girdle muscular dystrophy (R9) weakens hip and shoulder muscles and can affect the heart and breathing. About 1 in 11,000 to 1 in 100,000 in Europe. This week it got its first approved treatment.
  • Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency can cause emphysema before 50, especially in smokers, and liver disease. About 1 in 5,000 in Europe, and a simple blood test finds it. November is Alpha-1 Awareness Month.
  • In xeroderma pigmentosum, skin can't repair sun damage to DNA, so skin cancers can start in childhood. About 1 in 430,000 births in Europe. Strict sun protection is lifelong.
  • Acute intermittent porphyria causes attacks of severe tummy pain, dark urine and weakness, often triggered by certain medicines or fasting. About 1 in 190,000 people in Europe. Most people with the gene never have an attack.
