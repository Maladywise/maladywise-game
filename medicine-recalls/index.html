Medicine safety

My medicine was recalled: what now?

A recall headline about your tablets is unsettling. Most recalls cover a few batches, many are precautionary, and the safest first step is usually a quick chat with your pharmacist, not tipping the pills in the bin.

Quick answer: Don’t stop a regular medicine on your own, especially blood pressure, heart, epilepsy or mental health medicines. First check whether your pack is affected: recalls usually name specific batch (lot) numbers, strengths or brands. Then talk to your pharmacist or doctor. A pharmacist can often swap you to an unaffected batch or another brand the same day. Check recalls with the TGA (Australia), FDA (US) or MHRA (UK).

What a recall means

  • A recall removes a product from supply, or fixes it, because of a problem with its safety, quality, effectiveness or labelling.
  • The problem may be the medicine itself, or something like a labelling mistake, a packaging fault, or tablets that don’t dissolve as they should in lab tests.
  • Usually only certain batches are affected. Other batches and brands of the same medicine are fine.
  • Regulators can also issue an alert (keep using it, but with extra care) or a correction (fixing a label or leaflet).

Recall classes

Classes rank how serious the problem is. Australia (TGA) and the US (FDA) use similar wording:

  • Class I: a reasonable chance of serious harm or death. Example: the wrong medicine in the pack, or contamination of an injection or eye drop.
  • Class II: may cause temporary or medically reversible problems, or serious harm is unlikely. Example: a tablet that fails a strength or dissolution test, or missing safety information.
  • Class III: unlikely to cause harm. Example: a missing batch number or expiry date.
  • UK (MHRA): Class 1 (act now) to Class 4 (caution in use, usually minor packaging or leaflet faults).

Why most recalls are precautionary

  • Many recalls come from quality testing by the maker or regulator, not from reports of people being harmed.
  • In 2019–20, the TGA recorded 790 recall actions across medicines, devices and other products. About 12% were Class I.
  • Only about 1 in 25 (4%) reached consumer level, where the public is asked to act. Most stopped at hospitals, pharmacies or wholesalers.
  • A recall doesn’t automatically mean the medicine harmed anyone. It means a batch didn’t meet the standard.

Don’t just stop your blood pressure tablets

  • Missing blood pressure tablets for days can let your blood pressure climb, and stopping some (such as beta blockers or clonidine) suddenly can cause a rebound.
  • The same goes for medicines for the heart, epilepsy, thyroid, diabetes, blood clots and mental health.
  • Talk to your pharmacist or doctor before stopping. Pharmacists can often supply an unaffected batch, or a different brand of the same medicine, and your doctor can switch you to a similar medicine if needed.
  • If you’re told to stop, ask what to take instead, and when to recheck your blood pressure.

How to check recalls

  • Australia: the TGA’s Database of Recalls, Product Alerts and Product Corrections (DRAC), covering actions since 1 July 2012. The TGA can only recall products with an AUST R or AUST L number on the pack, so medicines bought from overseas websites may never get an Australian recall.
  • US: the FDA’s recalls, market withdrawals and safety alerts page and its weekly Enforcement Report, which lists Class I to III drug recalls.
  • UK: the MHRA’s drug and device alerts on GOV.UK, which you can filter to medicines recalls and sign up to by email.
  • Anywhere: ask your pharmacist. Pharmacies get recall notices directly.

What to do with an affected pack

  • Follow the recall notice. Usually you return the pack to the pharmacy.
  • Keep the box or bottle so the batch number can be checked.
  • Report side effects you think are linked: TGA (Australia), FDA MedWatch (US) or the Yellow Card scheme (UK).

The current US olmesartan recall (October 2026)

  • What: olmesartan medoxomil, amlodipine and hydrochlorothiazide 40 mg/10 mg/12.5 mg tablets (a three-in-one blood pressure tablet), made by Macleods Pharma USA.
  • Why: “failed dissolution specifications”. In lab tests the tablets didn’t dissolve as required, so they might not release the medicine as expected. It’s not an impurity recall.
  • Which packs: lot 12251163A (30-tablet bottles) and lot 12251163B (90-tablet bottles), both expiring 31 May 2027. That’s 3,744 bottles.
  • Class II: the recall began on 7 October 2026 and the FDA classified it on 26 October 2026.
  • Where: distributed across the US. It’s a US recall of US-labelled bottles. If you take olmesartan in Australia or elsewhere, check your local regulator or ask your pharmacist.
  • If you take it: check the lot number on your bottle, and talk to your pharmacist or doctor before stopping.

See your pharmacist or GP if

  • Your medicine is named in a recall and you’re not sure if your pack is affected
  • You’ve already stopped a regular medicine because of a recall
  • You notice new side effects, or your usual symptoms (such as blood pressure readings) change

Get urgent help: call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number for chest pain, trouble breathing, swelling of the face or throat, or signs of stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech). If you’ve taken too much or the wrong medicine, call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26 (Australia, 24/7) or your local poisons service.

Common questions

Should I stop taking a recalled medicine?

Not without advice. Stopping some medicines suddenly, such as blood pressure, heart, epilepsy or mental health medicines, can be riskier than the recall problem. Talk to your pharmacist or doctor first. They can usually swap you to an unaffected batch or another brand.

How do I know if my medicine is affected?

Recalls usually cover specific batches (lot numbers), strengths or brands, not every pack of a medicine. Compare the batch number and expiry date on your box or bottle with the recall notice, or ask your pharmacist to check.

What do recall classes mean?

They rank how serious the problem is. In Australia and the US, Class I is the most serious (a real chance of serious harm), Class II may cause temporary or reversible problems, and Class III is unlikely to cause harm. The UK uses Classes 1 to 4.

Where can I check for medicine recalls?

Australia: the TGA’s recall actions database. US: the FDA’s recalls page and Enforcement Report. UK: the MHRA’s drug and device alerts on GOV.UK. Your pharmacist can check too.

Can I get a refund or replacement?

Follow the recall instructions. Usually you return the affected pack to your pharmacy, which can supply a replacement from an unaffected batch or brand.

Is the US olmesartan recall in Australia?

It’s a US recall of two lots of one combination tablet sold in the US. If you take olmesartan anywhere else, check your local regulator’s recall list or ask your pharmacist, and don’t stop it without advice.
