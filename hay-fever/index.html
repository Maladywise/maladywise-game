Fact sheet

Hay Fever

Hay fever (allergic rhinitis) is an allergy that causes sneezing, a runny, itchy or blocked nose, and itchy, watery eyes. Triggers include grass and tree pollen (worst in spring and early summer), dust mites, mould and pets. It affects about 1 in 5 Australians. It can also trigger thunderstorm asthma, even in people without asthma.

Treatment options

Ratings show how strong the research is.

  • Strong evidenceSteroid nasal spraysSuch as budesonide (Rhinocort), fluticasone (Flixonase) or mometasone (Nasonex). The most effective treatment, especially for a blocked nose. Use daily; full effect takes a few days.
  • Strong evidenceCombination nasal spraysA steroid plus an antihistamine, such as azelastine with fluticasone (Dymista). Works better than either alone.
  • Strong evidenceAllergen immunotherapy (desensitisation)Tablets or drops under the tongue, or injections, for 3 to 5 years. Reduces symptoms and medicine use for years afterwards. For proven allergy not controlled by sprays. Started by an allergy specialist.
  • Moderate evidenceNon-sedating antihistaminesSuch as cetirizine, loratadine or fexofenadine. Help sneezing, itch and watery eyes, but do little for a blocked nose. Antihistamine eye drops also help.
  • Limited evidenceSalt-water (saline) sprays or rinsesSafe and cheap. They wash pollen out and may ease symptoms.
  • Limited evidenceDecongestantsNasal sprays (such as oxymetazoline) work fast, but after 3 days the nose gets more blocked. Tablets (such as pseudoephedrine) can raise blood pressure.
  • Limited evidenceMontelukastLess effective than steroid sprays. Can cause mood and sleep changes.
  • Not recommendedSedating antihistaminesSuch as promethazine or dexchlorpheniramine. Next-day drowsiness affects driving, work and learning.
  • Not recommendedSteroid injections or courses of steroid tabletsSuch as a "hay fever injection". Risks include weakened bones, raised blood sugar and mood changes, and safer treatments work.
  • Not recommendedUnproven allergy testsIgG food tests, kinesiology, hair analysis and Vega testing are unreliable.

What you can do

  • Check pollen counts and thunderstorm asthma forecasts in spring. Stay indoors with windows closed before and during storms on high-pollen days.
  • Start your steroid spray a couple of weeks before your usual season, and keep using it daily.
  • Wear wraparound sunglasses outside, and shower after time outdoors.
  • For dust mites: wash bedding weekly in hot water and use mite-proof covers.

See your GP if

  • Symptoms aren’t controlled after a few weeks of daily steroid spray
  • You have a cough, wheeze or tight chest, especially in spring: you may have asthma
  • You have a blocked nose on one side only, nosebleeds, or loss of smell
  • You’d like allergy testing or to ask about immunotherapy

Call 000 for trouble breathing, wheeze or a tight chest that isn’t quickly relieved by a reliever puffer. Thunderstorm asthma can start fast during or after a spring storm, even if you’ve never had asthma. If you have asthma, take your preventer every day through pollen season and always carry your reliever.

Common questions

What is the most effective hay fever treatment?

A steroid nasal spray used every day, especially for a blocked nose. Full effect takes a few days. A combined steroid and antihistamine spray works better than either alone.

Do antihistamines help a blocked nose?

Not much. Non-drowsy antihistamines help sneezing, itch and watery eyes, but do little for a blocked nose.

Is a hay fever injection safe?

Steroid injections aren’t recommended. Risks include weakened bones, raised blood sugar and mood changes, and safer treatments work.

What is thunderstorm asthma?

Sudden asthma that can start during or after a spring storm on high-pollen days, even in people who’ve never had asthma. Call 000 for trouble breathing that isn’t quickly relieved by a reliever puffer.
