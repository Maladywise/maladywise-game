Neck Osteoarthritis (Cervical Spondylosis)

Age-related changes in the neck’s discs and joints. Very common after 40, and many people with it have no symptoms at all. When it does cause trouble, it’s usually neck stiffness and pain that come and go. Rarely, it presses on a nerve or the spinal cord, which needs prompt care.

Symptoms. Neck pain and stiffness that come and go, often with headaches starting at the back of the head. A pinched nerve can cause pain, pins and needles, numbness or weakness down one arm. Pressure on the spinal cord (much rarer) causes clumsy hands, trouble with buttons or handwriting, and unsteady walking.

Who gets it. Mostly a normal part of ageing. Earlier in people who’ve had a neck injury or when it runs in the family.

How it’s diagnosed. From your story and an examination of your neck, arms, reflexes and walking. X-rays or an MRI are only needed for nerve or spinal cord symptoms, or pain that isn’t settling. Neck changes on scans are common in people with no pain, so a scan alone doesn’t explain symptoms.

The neck arthritis ladder

Most flares settle with movement and time. The higher steps are mainly for a pinched nerve or pressure on the spinal cord.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Scans “just to see”Neck changes are a normal part of ageing and show up in people without pain. A scan rarely changes treatment unless there are nerve or spinal cord symptoms.
  • GlucosamineNICE advises against it for osteoarthritis. No good evidence for the neck.
  • AcupunctureNICE advises against it for osteoarthritis because the benefit is too small or uncertain.

What you can do

  • Move your neck gently through its range a few times a day.
  • Break up long spells at a screen; raise the screen to eye level.
  • Use one supportive pillow that keeps your neck level with your body.
  • Follow our neck exercise handout: one printable page of movement and strengthening.

See your GP if

  • Pain, pins and needles or numbness spreads down an arm
  • Pain is getting much worse, or isn’t improving after a few weeks
  • Pain between the shoulder blades, or pain that’s worse at night
  • You also have fever, weight loss or a history of cancer

Get urgent help (call 000 in Australia, or your local emergency number) for new trouble walking, loss of bladder or bowel control, sudden loss of coordination, a drooping face, being unable to lift both arms, or difficulty speaking.

See a doctor within a day or two for new arm or leg weakness or heaviness, clumsy hands, or poor balance. These can mean pressure on a nerve or the spinal cord.

Common questions

Is cervical spondylosis serious?

Usually not. It’s a normal part of ageing and many people have no symptoms. Most flares of neck pain settle with movement and time. Only a small number of people develop nerve or spinal cord pressure.

Do I need an X-ray or MRI?

Not usually. Scans are for nerve or spinal cord symptoms or pain that isn’t settling, because neck changes on scans are common in people with no pain.

Can it cause arm pain?

Yes. Narrowed spaces can pinch a nerve, causing pain, tingling, numbness or weakness down one arm. See your GP, and get urgent help for weakness in both arms or legs, or clumsy hands.

Should I wear a neck collar?

Not routinely. Keeping the neck moving is the main treatment, and NHS advice focuses on exercise and staying active.

What causes cervical spondylosis?

Age-related changes in the discs and joints of the neck, sometimes sped up by past injury or heavy work. It’s very common: most people over 60 show changes on X-ray, but many have no pain.

What exercises help neck arthritis?

Gentle range-of-motion exercises (turning, tilting and tucking the chin) and strengthening of the neck and upper back muscles ease pain and stiffness. A physiotherapist can tailor a routine; aim for a little each day.

What are the warning signs of spinal cord pressure in the neck?

Clumsy hands, trouble with buttons or handwriting, unsteady walking, weakness or numbness in both arms or legs, or bladder or bowel changes. These need urgent medical review, as early surgery can stop it getting worse.
