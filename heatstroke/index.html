Heatstroke and heat exhaustion

Heat exhaustion is the body struggling to cool down. Heatstroke is when cooling fails and body temperature climbs past about 40 °C: a medical emergency that can damage the brain, kidneys and heart.

Tell them apart

  • Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, pale clammy skin, thirst, headache, dizziness, nausea, muscle cramps, feeling weak. Still alert.
  • Heatstroke: confusion, slurred speech, strange behaviour, fainting or a seizure. Very hot skin, which may be dry or still sweaty. This is the dangerous one.

Call 000 for heatstroke. Confusion in the heat is heatstroke until proven otherwise. Start cooling while you wait: cooling fast saves lives.

First aid

  • Move to shade or air conditioning and lie down with legs raised.
  • Remove extra clothing. Wet the skin with cool water and fan hard.
  • Ice packs or cold wet towels on the neck, armpits and groin. For heatstroke in a fit person (e.g. after sport), immersing the body in cold water is the fastest way to cool.
  • Small sips of cool water if they’re alert and not vomiting. Nothing by mouth if they’re confused or drowsy.
  • Heat exhaustion that isn’t better within an hour, or gets worse, needs medical help.

Who’s most at risk

  • Babies, young children, and people over 65 (especially living alone)
  • People with heart, kidney or lung disease, diabetes, or dementia
  • Some medicines: fluid tablets, some blood pressure tablets, antipsychotics, some antidepressants and medicines with anticholinergic effects. Don’t stop them, but ask your GP or pharmacist about hot weather.
  • Outdoor workers, athletes and anyone exercising in the heat; people who are pregnant

Staying cool

  • Drink water regularly, before you feel thirsty; go easy on alcohol.
  • Plan activity for early morning or evening. Shade, hats and light, loose clothes.
  • Cool showers, damp cloths and fans. Spend the hottest hours somewhere air-conditioned (a library, shopping centre or cinema) if home is hot.
  • Never leave children or pets in a parked car, even for a minute: the inside heats far faster than you’d expect.
  • Check on older neighbours and relatives during a heatwave.

Common questions

What’s the difference between heat exhaustion and heatstroke?

Heat exhaustion causes heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness and nausea, but the person stays alert. Heatstroke is more severe: body temperature rises above about 40 °C and the brain is affected, causing confusion, collapse or seizures. Heatstroke is an emergency: call 000.

What should I do for someone with heatstroke?

Call 000, move them somewhere cool, remove extra clothing, and cool them fast with water, fanning and ice packs on the neck, armpits and groin. Cold-water immersion is fastest in young, fit people.

Who is most at risk in a heatwave?

Older people, babies and young children, people with heart, lung or kidney disease or diabetes, people on some medicines such as fluid tablets, and people working or exercising outdoors.

How much water should I drink in hot weather?

Drink regularly through the day so your urine stays pale. People with heart or kidney failure who have a fluid limit should ask their doctor what’s right for them.
