Drowning: staying safe in the water

Hundreds of people drown in Australia every year, with a spike over summer and the holidays. Drowning is fast and quiet: it rarely looks like the splashing and shouting in films.

What drowning looks like

  • Silent: no breath to spare for calling out
  • Head low in the water, mouth at water level, tilted back
  • Upright, with arms pressing down on the water rather than waving
  • Glassy or empty eyes; not moving forward
  • A child who suddenly goes quiet in the water needs checking now

Children

  • Active supervision: stay within arm’s reach of young children around any water, including baths, buckets and paddling pools. Put the phone away.
  • Pool fences and gates must self-close and self-latch. Never prop the gate open. Empty paddling pools after use.
  • Swimming lessons help, but they don’t make a child drown-proof.

Adults

  • Swim at patrolled beaches, between the red and yellow flags. Rips are the biggest beach hazard: if caught, float, raise an arm and wait for help, or swim parallel to the shore.
  • Rivers, lakes and dams cause many drownings: cold water, hidden snags, strong currents and changing depths.
  • Avoid alcohol and drugs around water, and never swim alone.
  • Wear a lifejacket when boating, kayaking or rock fishing.
  • Older adults, especially men, are a growing group: health conditions, medicines and overconfidence all add risk.

If someone is in trouble

  • Call 000 (or alert a lifeguard). Throw something that floats rather than going in yourself.
  • Once they’re out: if they’re not breathing normally, start CPR. Learn CPR: a refresher course takes a few hours and is worth it.
  • Anyone pulled from the water who coughed, vomited or was confused should be checked by a doctor.

Common questions

What does drowning look like?

Usually quiet: the head is low in the water, the mouth at water level, the body upright, and the arms push down instead of waving. There’s often no shouting at all.

How do I keep children safe around water?

Stay within arm’s reach of young children around any water, use compliant self-closing pool gates, empty paddling pools after use, and don’t rely on swimming lessons alone.

What should I do if caught in a rip?

Stay calm and float, raise an arm to signal for help, or swim parallel to the shore until you’re out of the current, then swim back in.

Who is most at risk of drowning?

Young children around pools and home water, men of all ages, older adults, and people swimming in rivers, lakes, dams or at unpatrolled beaches, especially after alcohol.
