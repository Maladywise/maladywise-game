Fact sheet

Middle Ear Infection in Children

A painful infection behind the eardrum, usually after a cold. Very common in young children: most have at least one before school. Most get better within about 3 days without antibiotics, so pain relief is the main treatment.

The ear infection ladder

Pain relief for everyone. Antibiotics for children who are very unwell or more likely to benefit. Grommets for frequent infections.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careRegular pain relief (paracetamol or ibuprofen)Dosed for your child’s age or weight, given regularly for the first day or two.
    Works
    The main treatment: it eases pain while the infection settles. Most children improve within 3 days and nearly all within a week
    Cost
    About $5–15
    How long
    A few days
    Watch for
    Check the dose on the pack. No aspirin for children.

    Treat the pain, not the panic. Most ears sort themselves out.

  2. Step 2 · GPGP check and “wait and see”Your GP looks in the ear and checks your child over. Some families get a back-up script to use only if things aren’t improving.
    Works
    Without antibiotics, about 84 in 100 children no longer have ear pain by day 2–3
    Cost
    GP visit often bulk billed for children, or about $40–80 gap
    How long
    Fill the back-up script if not starting to improve after 2–3 days, or sooner if worse
    Watch for
    Return sooner if your child becomes more unwell, has ear discharge, or isn’t drinking.

    Waiting isn’t doing nothing. It’s doing the right amount.

  3. Step 3 · PrescriptionAntibiotics (usually amoxicillin)For children who are very unwell, under 2 with both ears infected, with fluid draining from the ear, or at higher risk of complications, including many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children.
    Works
    For every 20 children treated, about 1 has less pain at day 2–3 because of the antibiotic, and about 1 in 14 gets vomiting, diarrhoea or a rash. The benefit is bigger in the higher-risk groups
    Cost
    Under the $25 PBS co-payment ($7.70 concession)
    How long
    Usually 5–7 days, as prescribed
    Watch for
    Rash, diarrhoea, thrush. Tell your GP about any penicillin allergy.

    Great when needed. Tummy chaos when not.

  4. Step 4 · SpecialistEar, nose and throat surgeon (grommets)Tiny tubes in the eardrum for frequent infections, or for glue ear that’s affecting hearing.
    Works
    In small, older studies, children with grommets had about 1–1.5 fewer ear infections over the next 6 months. Low-quality evidence
    Cost
    Free in public hospitals (waits apply); private costs vary
    How long
    A short day procedure under general anaesthetic. Grommets usually fall out by themselves
    Watch for
    Needs an anaesthetic. Ask about hearing tests first if fluid lingers.

    Small tubes, big difference for some ears. Not a first move.

Costs are rough Australian prices in November 2026 and vary by provider. PBS co-payment: $25, or $7.70 with a concession card. Results are typical figures from trials, not promises.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Decongestants and antihistaminesTrials show they don’t help ear infections, and cough and cold medicines shouldn’t be given to children under 6.
  • Ear candlingDoesn’t remove anything and can burn the ear canal or damage the eardrum.
  • Oil, garlic or homeopathic ear dropsNo good evidence for middle ear infections. The infection is behind the eardrum, out of reach.

What you can do

  • Give regular pain relief at the right dose, and offer plenty of fluids.
  • Don’t put anything in the ear, including cotton buds or drops, unless a doctor suggests it.
  • Keep your child away from cigarette smoke, at home and in the car.
  • Keep vaccinations up to date, including flu and pneumococcal vaccines.
  • Breastfeed if you can, feed babies upright, avoid bottles in bed and limit dummy use.
  • Worried about hearing or speech after repeated infections? Ask for a hearing check.

See your GP if

  • Ear pain with fever, or your child seems unwell
  • Not starting to improve after 2–3 days, or getting worse
  • Fluid or pus draining from the ear
  • Babies under 6 months with a suspected ear infection
  • Frequent ear infections, or worries about hearing or speech
  • Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children with ear pain or discharge: see a doctor early

Go to emergency or call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number for redness, swelling or pain behind the ear or an ear that sticks out, a stiff neck, a child who is floppy, drowsy or hard to wake, weakness on one side of the face, or a baby under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C or more.

Common questions

What are the symptoms of a middle ear infection?

Older children usually say their ear hurts. Babies and toddlers may cry more at night, pull or rub their ear, be irritable, go off their food or vomit, and often have a fever. Hearing may be muffled for a while.

What causes ear infections in children?

They usually follow a cold. Children’s tubes between the middle ear and the back of the nose are short and floppy, so fluid gets trapped and germs grow. Smoke exposure, childcare and bottle-feeding lying down raise the chances.

How is it diagnosed?

Your GP looks at the eardrum with a light (otoscope). A bulging, red or cloudy eardrum with fluid behind it points to infection.

Why didn’t my child get antibiotics?

Most children get better on their own within a few days, and antibiotics help only about 1 in 20 with pain by day 2–3, while causing side effects in about 1 in 14. Your GP may give them if your child is very unwell or in a higher-risk group.

Do Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children need different care?

Yes. They have higher rates of ear infections and complications, so doctors are more likely to treat with antibiotics and to arrange follow-up and hearing checks.
