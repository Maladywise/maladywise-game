Haemochromatosis Quiz

Twelve questions, easy to hard, on haemochromatosis, one of the most common genetic conditions in Australia: how to find it, treat it and prevent damage.

  1. Question 1 of 12

    What happens in haemochromatosis?

    1. The body can't absorb any iron
    2. Iron leaks out in the urine
    3. The body absorbs too much iron from food
    4. Too much copper builds up
    Show answer

    Answer: The body absorbs too much iron from food. Iron slowly builds up in the liver, heart, pancreas, joints and skin.

  2. Question 2 of 12

    Which gene causes most cases?

    1. HFE (two copies of C282Y)
    2. ATP7B (two copies)
    3. BRCA1 (two copies)
    4. CFTR (two copies)
    Show answer

    Answer: HFE (two copies of C282Y). Inherited from both parents. Not everyone with the gene change gets iron overload.

  3. Question 3 of 12

    How common is the high-risk gene pattern in Australians of northern European descent?

    1. About 1 in 2
    2. About 1 in 200
    3. It doesn’t occur in Australia
    4. About 1 in a million
    Show answer

    Answer: About 1 in 200. One of the most common genetic conditions in Australia, yet often missed.

  4. Question 4 of 12

    Which blood tests screen for it?

    1. Vitamin B12 and folate
    2. Thyroid function tests
    3. Haemoglobin only
    4. Ferritin and transferrin saturation
    Show answer

    Answer: Ferritin and transferrin saturation. A high transferrin saturation is the earliest sign. Then confirm with the HFE gene test.

  5. Question 5 of 12

    What is the main treatment?

    1. Chemotherapy
    2. Iron tablets
    3. Antibiotics
    4. Venesection (regularly removing blood)
    Show answer

    Answer: Venesection (regularly removing blood). Each 500 mL removes about 250 mg of iron. Simple, cheap and very effective.

  6. Question 6 of 12

    Can people with haemochromatosis donate blood at Lifeblood?

    1. Yes, it can count as their treatment
    2. Only if their ferritin is normal
    3. Only once in their life
    4. No, never
    Show answer

    Answer: Yes, it can count as their treatment. Lifeblood accepts donations from many people with haemochromatosis, so the blood helps someone else.

  7. Question 7 of 12

    Which organ damage is the most serious risk?

    1. Hearing loss in both ears
    2. Cataracts in both eyes
    3. Liver cirrhosis and liver cancer
    4. Hair loss and thinning
    Show answer

    Answer: Liver cirrhosis and liver cancer. Diabetes, heart failure and arthritis can also occur. Early treatment prevents almost all of it.

  8. Question 8 of 12

    Which joints are classically affected?

    1. The jaw joint
    2. The 2nd and 3rd knuckles of the hand
    3. The spine only
    4. The big toe joint
    Show answer

    Answer: The 2nd and 3rd knuckles of the hand. Painful knuckles with a weak grip (‘iron fist’) is a clue.

  9. Question 9 of 12

    Why do women often show iron overload later than men?

    1. Hormones destroy iron
    2. Women absorb less iron
    3. Women don’t carry the gene
    4. Periods and pregnancy remove iron
    Show answer

    Answer: Periods and pregnancy remove iron. Many women aren’t diagnosed until after menopause.

  10. Question 10 of 12

    What should people with haemochromatosis avoid?

    1. Drinking too much water
    2. Iron and vitamin C supplements, raw shellfish
    3. All green leafy vegetables
    4. Dairy foods and calcium
    Show answer

    Answer: Iron and vitamin C supplements, raw shellfish. Vitamin C boosts iron absorption. Raw oysters can carry Vibrio vulnificus, which is dangerous with iron overload.

  11. Question 11 of 12

    What ferritin level is usually aimed for during maintenance?

    1. About 50 to 100 µg/L
    2. Over 1000 µg/L
    3. Zero
    4. As high as possible
    Show answer

    Answer: About 50 to 100 µg/L. Venesection is then done every few months to keep it there.

  12. Question 12 of 12

    What is the outlook if treated early?

    1. Lifelong dialysis
    2. Cirrhosis is inevitable
    3. A normal life expectancy
    4. Always shortened by 20 years
    Show answer

    Answer: A normal life expectancy. Finding it before organ damage is the key. Test first-degree relatives.

Common questions

Should my family be tested?

Yes. Parents, siblings and children of someone with haemochromatosis should be offered testing.

Do I need a special diet?

Not usually. Avoid iron and vitamin C supplements and raw shellfish, and limit alcohol, which adds to liver damage.

Is this quiz medical advice?

No. It’s general education. See your GP about testing.
