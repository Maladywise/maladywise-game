Fact sheet

Lower back pain

What it is

About 8 in 10 adults have lower back pain at some point. Most back pain comes from muscles, ligaments and joints rather than serious damage, and it usually improves within a few weeks. Scans aren't needed for most people.

What helps

  • Stay active. Gentle walking and normal activity help you recover faster than bed rest.
  • Heat packs can ease pain and muscle spasm.
  • Pain relief such as paracetamol or anti-inflammatories from the pharmacy can help you keep moving. Follow the packet directions and check with a pharmacist if you have other health conditions.
  • Gentle stretches and exercises as the pain settles. A physiotherapist can guide you.
  • Return to work and usual activities as soon as you can, even if you're not 100%.

Things that make it worse

  • Long periods of bed rest
  • Sitting or standing still for hours
  • Smoking, poor sleep and stress
  • Fear of movement. Hurting doesn't always mean harm.

Preventing it coming back

  • Regular exercise: walking, swimming, pilates or strength training
  • A healthy weight
  • Taking breaks from sitting

See your GP if

  • The pain isn't improving after 4 to 6 weeks
  • Pain or numbness spreads below the knee
  • You're over 50 with new back pain
  • You have a history of cancer, weight loss you can't explain, fever, or pain that is worse at night

Go to the emergency department or call 000 if you have numbness around the bottom or genitals, new trouble passing urine or controlling your bladder or bowels, or weakness in your legs. These can be signs of nerve compression that needs urgent treatment.
