Autism in Adults

Many autistic adults only find out in their 20s, 40s or 70s, often after years of feeling different and working hard to fit in. A diagnosis, or recognising yourself, can explain a lot, unlock support, and connect you with a community that gets it.

The autism support ladder for adults

Not about becoming less autistic: about less exhaustion, better health and a life that fits. Pick what helps. For the overview, see our autism fact sheet.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careSensory kit, energy budget and recovery timeHeadphones or earplugs, sunglasses, comfortable clothes, scripts for tricky conversations, and planned downtime after busy or social days.
    Works
    Not tested well in trials, but low risk; many autistic adults say it’s what keeps them going
    Cost
    Free to a few hundred dollars
    How long
    Ongoing
    Watch for
    Running on empty for weeks. That’s the road to burnout

    Leaving the party early is a strategy, not a failure.

  2. Step 2 · CommunityAutistic-led peer supportOnline and local groups, books and podcasts by autistic people, and post-diagnosis programs.
    Works
    Little formal research, but many newly diagnosed adults describe it as the most helpful step
    Cost
    Mostly free
    How long
    As long as you like
    Watch for
    Groups that feel judgemental. Try another; there are many

    Finding your people: no small talk required.

  3. Step 3 · Work and studyReasonable adjustmentsClear written instructions, predictable schedules, a quieter workspace, flexible hours, interview questions in advance, and a named contact person.
    Works
    Little formal research. In Australia, about 1 in 5 autistic people looking for work were unemployed in 2022, versus 1 in 30 people without disability: a gap adjustments are meant to close
    Cost
    Free to you. JobAccess gives free advice and may fund equipment; unis have disability services
    How long
    Reviewed as roles change
    Watch for
    You don’t have to disclose, but sharing what you need usually makes adjustments easier to get

    The talent is there. Fluorescent lights and vague emails are the barrier.

  4. Step 4 · Allied healthPsychology and occupational therapyTherapy adapted for autistic people for anxiety, depression or trauma; OT for sensory needs, routines and daily-living skills.
    Works
    Adapted CBT reduces anxiety in trials, and guidelines recommend adapting talking therapies for autistic adults
    Cost
    About $180–280 a session, with Medicare rebates on a GP mental health plan (and autism items for under-25s)
    How long
    Often 8–20 sessions
    Watch for
    Find someone experienced with autistic adults and neuro-affirming practice

    Treat the anxiety, not the autism.

  5. Step 5 · PrescriptionMedicines for co-occurring conditionsFor anxiety, depression, ADHD or sleep problems when they’re present. There’s no medicine for autism itself.
    Works
    Same evidence as for these conditions in general, though autistic people are under-represented in trials
    Cost
    Mostly under the $25 PBS co-payment
    How long
    Reviewed regularly
    Watch for
    Some autistic people notice side effects more, so tell your doctor early. See ADHD in adults

    Medicine for the extras, not the identity.

  6. Step 6 · SpecialistAdult autism assessmentA psychiatrist or psychologist experienced with adults, especially women and people who mask.
    Works
    Not a treatment as such: many adults describe relief and self-understanding, and it can open doors to supports
    Cost
    Mostly private in Australia; fees vary widely, and Medicare autism items stop at 25
    How long
    Waits can be months
    Watch for
    Choose a clinician experienced with adults. Self-identifying is valid too; a formal diagnosis matters mainly for some supports and legal protections

    Late diagnosis isn’t late. It’s right on time for the rest of your life.

Costs are rough Australian prices in November 2026 and vary by pharmacy and provider. PBS co-payment: $25, or $7.70 with a concession card. Experiences are individual, and every autistic adult is different.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Online quizzes as a diagnosisScreening questionnaires can start a conversation with your GP, but they can’t diagnose, and anxiety, trauma and ADHD can score high too.
  • Biological or “brain scan” autism testsThere’s no biological test for autism. Save your money for an experienced assessor.
  • Supplements or diets to “treat” autismNo evidence they change autism, and some interact with medicines.
  • Courses that train you to seem less autisticConstant masking is linked to exhaustion and burnout. Skills you want are great; pretending to be someone else isn’t.

What you can do

  • Notice your sensory triggers and recovery needs, and plan around them where you can.
  • Before an assessment, jot down examples from childhood and now, and ask family what you were like as a kid.
  • Learn about autism from autistic people: books, podcasts and peer groups.
  • Ask for adjustments at work, uni or medical appointments, such as the first or last appointment of the day, written information, or dimmed lights.
  • Protect sleep and recovery time, especially during busy or stressful periods.
  • For free, independent advice in Australia, call Autism Connect on 1300 308 699.

See your GP if

  • You’ve always felt different, find social situations exhausting, and wonder whether you’re autistic
  • You feel burnt out: deep exhaustion, loss of skills you usually have, and more sensitivity to noise or light
  • You have anxiety, low mood, an eating disorder, sleep problems or gut issues
  • Your child has been diagnosed and you recognise yourself

Get help now if you have thoughts of suicide: they are more common in autistic people. Call Lifeline on 13 11 14 (24/7, Australia), or 000 (or your local emergency number) if you are in danger.

Common questions

What are the signs of autism in adults?

Finding social situations confusing or tiring, taking things literally, preferring routine and being thrown by sudden change, intense focused interests, stimming (such as rocking or fidgeting), and being over- or under-sensitive to sound, light, smell, taste or textures. Many adults have learnt to hide these, so they may not be obvious to others.

How is autism diagnosed in adults?

A psychiatrist or psychologist experienced with adults takes a detailed history of your development and current life, often with input from someone who knew you as a child, and may use structured interviews or observation. There’s no blood test or scan. Anxiety, ADHD, trauma and other conditions are considered alongside.

What is masking?

Hiding or covering autistic traits to fit in, such as copying others, forcing eye contact or rehearsing conversations. It can make people seem to cope well, but it’s often exhausting and is linked to anxiety, depression and burnout.

Why are women often diagnosed later?

Autistic women and girls more often mask, may have interests that seem typical, and are more often diagnosed first with anxiety, depression or eating disorders. Men can mask too.

Is it worth getting diagnosed as an adult?

It’s a personal choice. Many people find a diagnosis brings relief and self-understanding. In Australia, a formal diagnosis may be needed for some supports such as the NDIS, and helps with workplace or study adjustments.

Should I say “autistic” or “has autism”?

Views differ, so ask. Many autistic people prefer identity-first language (“autistic person”), because autism shapes how they experience everything. Others prefer “person on the autism spectrum” or “person with autism”. In a 2020 Australian survey of autistic adults, “autistic”, “autistic person” and “person on the autism spectrum” were all well liked. We mostly say “autistic”, and respect whatever words a person uses for themselves.
