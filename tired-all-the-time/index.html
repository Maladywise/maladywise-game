Tired All the Time

Running on empty, even after a full night in bed? Tiredness is one of the most common reasons people see a GP. The cause is usually sleep, stress, mood or lifestyle, and sometimes a medical condition. Often it’s a mix.

How common: fatigue comes up in about 1 in 70 GP visits in Australia, and is the most common unexplained symptom GPs see.

Common causes: poor or short sleep, a busy life, stress, anxiety, depression and grief, too much caffeine or alcohol, too little exercise, and a new baby. Hormone changes in pregnancy and menopause play a part too.

Medical causes: iron deficiency and anaemia, an underactive or overactive thyroid, diabetes, sleep apnoea, infections such as glandular fever and COVID-19, some medicines, and less often heart, kidney or autoimmune disease or cancer.

When no cause is found: that is common. In one Dutch study, over half of people had no diagnosis a year later and only about 8 in 100 had a physical disease. ME/CFS is diagnosed when extreme fatigue lasts at least 3 months with other features.

The fatigue ladder

Sleep and lifestyle fixes come first. A short list of blood tests finds most treatable causes; a long list rarely adds much.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careSleep, caffeine and movementKeep regular sleep times and aim for 6 to 9 hours. Avoid daytime naps and screens in the hour before bed. Caffeine can last up to 7 hours, so stop by early afternoon. Go easy on alcohol, which makes sleep lighter.

    Even a single 15-minute walk can lift energy. Building up to 150 minutes of moderate activity a week helps over time

    How long
    A few weeks
    Watch for
    Cut caffeine down gradually; stopping suddenly can cause headaches.

    How long
    2 to 4 weeks
    Watch for

    The history finds more than the blood tests. Really.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • A huge panel of blood testsA small set of tests is almost as good at finding serious disease. Big panels mostly find borderline results that lead to more tests.
  • Extra caffeine to push throughIt can disrupt sleep for up to 7 hours, making tomorrow worse.
  • A nightcap to help you sleepAlcohol makes sleep lighter and can leave you more tired the next day.
  • Diagnosing yourself onlineTiredness has many causes. Guessing can lead you down the wrong path. Talk to a GP.

What you can do

  • Keep a 2-week diary of sleep, caffeine, alcohol, exercise and energy levels.
  • Ask someone if you snore, gasp or stop breathing in your sleep.
  • Bring a list of your medicines to your GP.
  • If you have heavy periods, read our heavy periods fact sheet; they are a common cause of low iron.
  • Be honest with your GP about mood and stress. They are the most common causes.
  • Take care with driving if you are very tired.

See your GP if

  • You’ve been tired for more than 2 weeks and it isn’t improving
  • Tiredness is stopping you doing normal things
  • You’ve lost weight without trying, or your mood has changed
  • Someone has noticed you snore, gasp or choke in your sleep
  • You’re older and the tiredness is new
  • You also have fevers, night sweats, swollen glands, unusual bleeding or shortness of breath

Tiredness isn’t laziness. It’s a symptom worth talking about. If you feel so low that life doesn’t feel worth living, call 000 or Lifeline on 13 11 14 (Australia), or your local crisis line.

Support lines

If you or someone else is in danger right now, call your emergency number. To talk to someone, any time:

  • Australia: Lifeline 13 11 14 (24/7, or text 0477 13 11 14), Suicide Call Back Service 1300 659 467, Beyond Blue 1300 22 4636, 13YARN 13 92 76 for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. Emergency: 000.
  • Young people in Australia: Kids Helpline 1800 55 1800 (24/7, ages 5 to 25) or headspace (ages 12 to 25).
  • New Zealand: call or text 1737. Emergency: 111.
  • UK and Ireland: Samaritans 116 123. Emergency: 999 (or 112).
  • US and Canada: call or text 988. Emergency: 911.
  • Anywhere else: findahelpline.com lists free helplines in more than 100 countries.

Common questions

Why am I tired all the time?

Most often from poor sleep, stress, low mood or lifestyle. Medical causes include low iron, thyroid problems, diabetes, sleep apnoea and recent infections. Often it is a mix of things.

What blood tests check for tiredness?

A GP usually checks a blood count, iron, glucose, thyroid, kidney and liver function and an inflammation marker. A short list like this finds most treatable causes.

When should I see a doctor about tiredness?

If it lasts more than 2 weeks without improving, affects your daily life, or comes with weight loss, mood changes or other symptoms.

Can tiredness have no cause?

Yes. Many people never get a specific diagnosis, and their tiredness often improves with time and lifestyle changes. If extreme fatigue lasts more than 3 months, your GP may consider ME/CFS.

What vitamins help with tiredness?

Supplements only help if you’re actually low in something, such as iron, vitamin B12 or vitamin D. A blood test can show this. For most tired people with normal results, supplements don’t improve energy.

Is tiredness a sign of depression?

It can be. Tiredness, low energy, poor sleep and loss of interest are common in depression and anxiety. Your GP may ask about mood, as treating depression often improves energy.

How much sleep do adults need?

Most adults need 7 to 9 hours a night, and older adults 7 to 8. Regularly getting less, or broken sleep from snoring, shift work or a young baby, is one of the most common reasons for daytime tiredness.
