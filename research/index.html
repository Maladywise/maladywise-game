This week in research

17–23 November 2026

Two labour drugs didn't help first-time mums avoid interventions

Strong evidencelarge double-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial; small benefits can't be ruled out

What they found. SAINT: 2,963 first-time mothers having an induced labour at 11 Norwegian hospitals were randomised to sodium bicarbonate, hyoscine (Buscopan) given into a vein, both, or placebo once active labour started. Births without forceps, vacuum or caesarean were much the same in every group: 57% with both drugs, 61% with each drug alone and 56% with placebo. None of the differences were statistically significant, and serious side effects were similar.

So what. If you're being induced, these add-ons aren't a proven way to make a natural birth more likely, so don't feel you're missing out if they aren't offered. Your midwife and obstetrician can talk you through what actually helps.

Source: BMJ Group summary of the BMJ paper (19 Nov 2026)

Your waist may say more than your BMI

Moderate evidencehuge observational study; shows a link, not cause and effect; one Korean population, measured once

What they found. Health check data from more than 17 million South Korean adults, followed for nearly 14 years. Risk of death was U-shaped: lowest with a waist of 80–94 cm in men and 75–89 cm in women. Among people with a bigger-than-average waist for their BMI, every extra 5 cm was linked to a 16% higher risk of death in men and 14% in women. Very small waists were linked to higher risk too.

So what. Where you carry weight matters, not just how much. A tape measure around your middle is a cheap, useful extra to the bathroom scales, but it's one clue, not a verdict. Ask your GP if you're unsure what your numbers mean.

Source: BMJ Group summary of the Journal of Epidemiology & Community Health paper (18 Nov 2026)

Many health tests sold straight to consumers overpromise

Moderate evidencecross-sectional review of websites, UK only; not a study of patient outcomes

What they found. Researchers checked 50 UK websites selling health tests to healthy people. 36 claimed earlier diagnosis and better outcomes and 16 claimed a longer life, without evidence. 70% didn't explain the limits or possible harms. Offers included mammograms for average-risk women under 50 and bowel tests for under-50s, priced from about £7 to £14,000.

So what. A test you buy online can mean false alarms, more tests and worry, or false reassurance. Before paying for a check-up package, ask your GP whether it's actually recommended for someone your age and risk. In Australia, free national screening programs cover bowel, breast and cervical cancer.

Source: BMJ Group summary of the BMJ Open paper (18 Nov 2026)

Light-and-sound headset for Alzheimer's missed its main goal

Early evidencerandomised sham-controlled trial missed its primary endpoint; positive findings are subgroup only; conference presentation, not yet peer-reviewed

What they found. HOPE: 663 people with mild to moderate Alzheimer's used an at-home device that flickers light and sound (or a sham version) for an hour a day for 12 months. It didn't beat the sham on the main measure of memory and daily function. In a pre-planned subgroup with milder disease (395 people) decline looked about 47–56% slower on some scales, but those results weren't adjusted for multiple testing. Headache, tinnitus and seizures were slightly more common with the real device.

So what. You may see headlines calling this a breakthrough. It isn't proven: the main result was negative and the device isn't approved anywhere. The early-disease signal needs a new trial to confirm it. Don't buy flicker gadgets online hoping for the same effect.

Source: NeurologyLive report from CTAD 2026 (17 Nov 2026)

Earlier weeks

10–16 November 2026

A weight-loss injection worked for teenagers too

Strong evidencerandomised trial, but fairly small and 72 weeks only

What they found. SURMOUNT-ADOLESCENTS: 160 teens aged 12–17 with obesity, plus healthy-lifestyle support. After 72 weeks, weekly tirzepatide cut BMI by 18.5%, while placebo nudged it up 0.5% (about 18 kg lost vs 2 kg gained). Nearly half lost 20% or more of their BMI. Gut side effects were common (vomiting in about half), and 9% stopped because of side effects.

So what. These medicines can work for teenagers, not just adults, but they come with real side effects and weight tends to return when they stop. It's a decision for a young person, their family and their doctor together, alongside food, sleep and movement, not instead of them.

Source: JAMA (15 Nov 2026)

Skipping your blood pressure tablet on surgery day helped

Strong evidencelarge randomised placebo-controlled trial (China)

What they found. A double-blind trial of 2,012 adults aged 60–80 on ACE inhibitors or ARBs having non-heart surgery. Skipping the morning dose meant fewer dangerous blood pressure drops under anaesthetic (31% vs 46%) and less need for drugs to raise it (29% vs 41%), with no difference in complications or deaths at 30 days.

So what. If you take one of these tablets and have an operation coming up, ask your surgeon or anaesthetist whether to skip that morning's dose. Don't stop it on your own at any other time.

Source: BMJ Group summary of the BMJ paper (12 Nov 2026)

Which antidepressants keep working long term?

Moderate evidencenetwork meta-analysis of randomised trials; many at high risk of bias

What they found. A review of 115 trials with more than 30,000 people compared 20-odd antidepressants over the longer haul. People who stayed on an antidepressant kept their improvement more often than those switched to placebo (about 62% vs 45%). Escitalopram, duloxetine and agomelatine came out ahead of fluoxetine, but over half the key trials had a high risk of bias and most lasted about six months.

So what. Staying on treatment that's working helps prevent relapse. If your current medicine suits you, this isn't a reason to switch. If you're starting or changing, it's one more thing to talk through with your doctor.

Source: Radboudumc news (11 Nov 2026), published in The Lancet Psychiatry

Australia cut pregabalin misuse without taking it off the shelf

Moderate evidencelarge observational study; can't prove which change did the work

What they found. Australian dispensing and Poisons Information Centre data from 2016 to 2024 (3.4 million prescriptions). After media coverage, tighter prescribing rules, boxed warnings and real-time prescription monitoring, dispensing was about 25% below the expected trend and intentional overdose calls about 47% lower.

So what. Pregabalin (Lyrica) still has a place, but it carries real risks, especially with opioids or alcohol. If you take it, keep to the prescribed dose and talk to your doctor before stopping, as coming off suddenly can be rough.

Source: BMJ Group summary of the BMJ paper (12 Nov 2026)

3–10 November 2026

Lowering Lp(a) didn’t prevent heart attacks

Strong evidencelarge randomised trial

What they found. Lp(a)HORIZON: 8,322 people with heart or vessel disease and very high lipoprotein(a). Pelacarsen injections cut Lp(a) by 78%, but heart attacks, strokes and urgent procedures happened just as often as on placebo over about 4.7 years (12.3% vs 12.1%).

So what. Lp(a) is still a useful risk marker, but this drug didn’t turn a lower number into fewer events. Treat the other risk factors you can change, and be wary of anyone selling Lp(a) fixes.

Source: HCPLive summary of the NEJM paper (7 Nov 2026)

A weight-loss pill eased sleep apnoea

Strong evidencetwo randomised trials, 52 weeks

What they found. ATTAIN-OSA: two trials, 712 adults with overweight or obesity and moderate to severe sleep apnoea. A year of daily oral orforglipron (a GLP-1 tablet) cut breathing interruptions by about 13 more an hour than placebo, with about 10% weight loss. Gut side effects affected about 7 in 10.

So what. Tablet versions of weight-loss medicines are coming, and sleep apnoea is one of the clearest benefits. CPAP still works best for most people while they lose weight.

Source: JAMA (9 Nov 2026)

Stents for stable angina: still no longer life, 13 years on

Strong evidencelong-term follow-up of a large randomised trial

What they found. ISCHEMIA-EXTEND followed people with stable coronary disease for up to 13 years. Death rates were the same whether they had early stents or bypass surgery or started with medicines alone (26% vs 27%).

So what. For stable chest pain, procedures help symptoms but don’t add years. Good medicines and lifestyle do the heavy lifting. Unstable or worsening pain is different: get it checked urgently.

Source: NYU Langone news (7 Nov 2026), published in JAMA

How to read the evidence labels

  • StrongLarge randomised trials or good reviews of them. Likely to hold up.
  • ModerateSmaller trials, or large observational studies. Promising, not proven.
  • EarlyLab, animal or small early studies. Interesting, often doesn’t pan out in people.
