Fact sheet

Mast Cell Activation Syndrome (MCAS)

Mast cells overreacting: flushing, hives, swelling, wheeze, faintness or tummy cramps, in two or more body systems at once. Real but uncommon, and easy to over-diagnose. Here’s what helps.

The MCAS ladder

Most of the evidence is small studies, so treatment is try, review and adjust, ideally with an immunologist. Give each step 2 to 4 weeks before judging it.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careTrigger diary and a tryptase planSpot the patterns, and get the one blood test that actually confirms MCAS.
    Works
    Not a treatment, so no effect to measure; it’s how the diagnosis gets made (or ruled out)
    Cost
    Diary: free. Tryptase blood test: usually little or no cost with a GP request form
    How long
    Blood 30 minutes to 2 hours after an episode starts, plus a baseline 24+ hours after it settles
    Watch for
    Common triggers: heat, exercise, alcohol, stress, infections, stings, anti-inflammatories, opioids.

    Keep the request form in your bag. Episodes never book ahead.

  2. Step 2 · PharmacyDaily non-sedating antihistamineCetirizine, fexofenadine, loratadine or desloratadine. Blocks histamine, the main chemical behind flushing, itch and hives. The first thing to try.
    Works
    Evidence weak (small studies), but helps many people, especially skin symptoms
    Cost
    About $10–25 for 70 tablets (generic)
    How long
    Every day, not just on bad days. Judge after 2–4 weeks
    Watch for
    Mild drowsiness in some. A specialist may advise up to 4 times the usual dose: don’t freelance that.

    Daily means daily. Antihistamines work better as a fence than an ambulance.

  3. Step 3 · PharmacyFamotidineAn acid-lowering tablet that blocks a second type of histamine receptor. Added for flushing or gut symptoms.
    Works
    Evidence weak (small studies); helps some people, mainly gut symptoms
    Cost
    About $10–20 a month
    How long
    2–4 weeks to judge
    Watch for
    Usually well tolerated. Occasional headache, constipation or diarrhoea.

    Two antihistamines, two different jobs. Teamwork, not doubling up.

  4. Step 4 · PrescriptionAdrenaline autoinjector, if you’ve had anaphylaxisComes with an ASCIA Action Plan and training. If it’s prescribed, it’s not optional.
    Works
    No dummy treatment trials (they’d be unethical), but the only treatment that reverses anaphylaxis; first-line in every guideline
    Cost
    $25 per script on the PBS ($7.70 concession) for people at risk of anaphylaxis
    How long
    Carry it always. Check the expiry date
    Watch for
    Shaky, pale and racing heart afterwards is normal. Call 000 every time you use it.

    The best autoinjector is the one in your bag, not the one in the kitchen drawer.

  5. Step 5 · PrescriptionMontelukastA tablet that blocks leukotrienes, another mast cell chemical. May help wheeze or flushing.
    Works
    Evidence weak (case reports, small studies); helps some people
    Cost
    About $20–40 a month on a private script
    How long
    About 4 weeks to judge
    Watch for
    Mood or sleep changes, vivid dreams. Stop and tell your doctor if you notice them.

    Worth a fair trial. Not worth the nightmares.

  6. Step 6 · SpecialistMast cell stabilisers or omalizumabSodium cromoglicate by mouth before meals for gut symptoms, or omalizumab injections for severe, repeated anaphylaxis despite other treatment.
    Works
    Small trials, mostly in related mast cell disorders; helps some people
    Cost
    Specialist fees. Oral cromoglicate isn’t routinely available in Australia and can be costly; omalizumab is PBS-subsidised only for certain conditions
    How long
    Cromoglicate: 4–8 weeks to judge. Omalizumab: months
    Watch for
    Cromoglicate: mild tummy upset. Omalizumab: injection-site reactions, rarely allergic reactions.

    Specialist territory. Online forums are many things, but not specialists.

Costs are rough Australian prices in October 2026 and vary by pharmacy. PBS co-payment: $25, or $7.70 with a concession card. Evidence for MCAS treatments is mostly small studies, so expect some trial and adjustment.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Strict low-histamine dietsNo proven benefit in MCAS, and a real risk of a shrinking menu and poor nutrition.
  • Histamine blood or urine tests and IgG food testsNot reliable for diagnosing MCAS or food allergy, and ASCIA advises against them.
  • Kinesiology, hair analysis and Vega testingOn ASCIA’s list of unproven allergy “tests”. Impressive machines, imaginary results.
  • DAO enzyme supplementsSmall, low-quality studies in “histamine intolerance”, none in proper MCAS.
  • Quercetin and “mast cell stabilising” supplement stacksMostly lab-dish studies. Promising in a test tube is not the same as helping a person.

What you can do

  • Keep a diary of episodes and possible triggers.
  • Ask your GP for a tryptase request form to keep at home, so blood can be taken 30 minutes to 2 hours after an episode starts.
  • Wear medical ID, and carry your adrenaline autoinjector everywhere if prescribed.
  • Tell doctors, dentists and anaesthetists before procedures.

See your GP if

  • You have repeated episodes of flushing, hives or swelling with faintness, wheeze or tummy cramps
  • You’ve been told you have MCAS without a tryptase test during an episode
  • Treatment isn’t working, or you’re cutting out more and more foods
  • Your symptoms are ongoing rather than in episodes: other causes are more likely and need checking

Call 000 and use your adrenaline autoinjector for any sign of anaphylaxis: trouble breathing, wheeze or persistent cough, swollen tongue or tight throat, hoarse voice, dizziness or collapse. Lie flat (or sit with legs out if breathing is hard), and don’t stand or walk.

Common questions

How is MCAS diagnosed?

All three are needed: typical symptoms in at least 2 body systems at the same time; blood tryptase rising during an episode to more than 20% above your usual level plus 2 (for example, from 5 to 8 or more); and improvement with mast cell medicines. An immunologist or allergist usually makes the diagnosis.

Is MCAS common?

True MCAS is uncommon. Most people with long-term, varied symptoms don’t meet the criteria, so other causes need checking.

Does a low-histamine diet help MCAS?

There’s no proven benefit, and strict diets risk poor nutrition. If you change your diet, see a dietitian with allergy experience.

Are histamine blood or urine tests useful?

No. Histamine blood or urine tests and IgG food tests aren’t reliable for diagnosing MCAS, and ASCIA advises against them.
