Fact sheet

Vertigo (BPPV)

Short, violent spins when you roll over in bed, look up or bend down. Loose crystals in the inner ear are to blame. A head-turning manoeuvre fixes most cases in minutes, no tablets needed.

Typical signs: spinning that lasts under a minute, set off by changing head position: rolling over, lying down, looking up or bending. Nausea is common. Up to half of people feel a bit unsteady between spells.

Why it happens: tiny calcium crystals drift from their usual spot into the inner ear’s balance canals, sending confusing signals as you move. Usually there’s no clear cause. Head injury is the commonest known trigger. It’s more common in women and usually starts in your 50s to 70s.

Diagnosis: the Dix-Hallpike test: a doctor or physio lies you back quickly with your head turned and watches your eyes flick. No scan is needed for typical BPPV.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Dizziness tabletsProchlorperazine, antihistamines and similar don’t treat BPPV and may slow your brain’s recovery. Anti-nausea tablets help the nausea, not the spinning.
  • Routine scansCT and MRI aren’t recommended for typical BPPV. They’re for when other nerve symptoms are present.
  • Days of sleeping propped upTrials suggest restrictions after the manoeuvre aren’t needed, though some clinics still advise 48 hours of caution. Optional, not essential.

What you can do

  • If spells are brief and set off by head movement, ask your GP or a physio for the Dix-Hallpike test and an Epley.
  • Ask which ear is affected and get taught a home manoeuvre in case it returns.
  • Get up in stages, use a night light and keep a hand free for rails.
  • If you’re unsteady, try our falls prevention exercise plan.
  • If your vitamin D is low, correcting it may lower the chance of BPPV coming back.

See your GP if

  • It’s your first bout of vertigo
  • Spinning lasts hours or days, or comes on without head movement
  • Vertigo comes with hearing loss, ringing or fullness in one ear
  • It doesn’t settle after an Epley, or keeps coming back
  • You’ve fallen or are scared of falling

Get emergency help (call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number. From a mobile, 112 works in most countries.) if dizziness comes with new weakness or numbness, a drooping face, slurred speech, double vision, trouble walking, a sudden severe headache, or constant vertigo that stops you standing. These can mean a stroke.

Common questions

Will BPPV come back?

It often does. About 1 in 3 people have it return after treatment, and some studies report about 15 in 100 a year. It usually responds to the same manoeuvre again.

Can I do the Epley manoeuvre myself?

Yes, once a doctor or physio has confirmed it’s BPPV and which ear is affected, and taught you how. Done on the wrong side, it won’t help.

Is vertigo a sign of a stroke?

Usually not. BPPV spells are brief and set off by head movement. Constant vertigo, or dizziness with weakness, slurred speech, double vision or trouble walking, needs emergency care.

Do tablets help BPPV?

Not really. Dizziness tablets don’t fix the loose crystals and may slow recovery. A repositioning manoeuvre is the treatment.
