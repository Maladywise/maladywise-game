Fact sheet

D-MER (“sad nipple syndrome”)

A sudden wave of dread or sadness every time your milk lets down, gone a few minutes later. If that sounds familiar, you’re not imagining it, it isn’t your fault, and it doesn’t mean anything about how much you love your baby.

Quick answer: D-MER (dysphoric milk ejection reflex) is a brief wave of negative feelings, such as dread, sadness, anxiety or a hollow feeling in the stomach, that starts just before milk lets down and lasts from seconds to a few minutes. The leading theory is a sudden dip in dopamine at let-down. It’s different from postnatal depression and from breastfeeding aversion. Knowing what it is, tracking it and distracting yourself often help. Evidence is limited, mostly case reports and small studies.

What it feels like

  • A sudden drop in mood just before let-down: dread, sadness, anxiety, irritability, hopelessness, or a hollow or sinking feeling in the stomach.
  • It fades once the milk is flowing, usually within a few minutes.
  • It can happen with every let-down or only the first one of a feed, and with pumping or a let-down out of the blue.
  • It ranges from a mild “sigh” to intense, frightening feelings.
  • “Sad nipple syndrome” is a nickname. Nothing is wrong with the nipple: it’s linked to the let-down reflex.

How common is it?

  • In one 2019 study, about 9% of breastfeeding mothers (about 1 in 11) reported D-MER.
  • The Australian Breastfeeding Association estimates 5% to 15%.
  • It may be more likely if you’ve had depression or anxiety before, and lack of sleep can make it worse.

Why it happens

  • Dopamine, a brain chemical linked to mood and reward, normally keeps prolactin (the milk-making hormone) in check. It falls when breastfeeding starts so prolactin can rise.
  • The leading theory: in D-MER, dopamine drops too abruptly, causing a brief dip in mood. Oxytocin may play a part too.
  • This is a hypothesis, not proven. It’s a physical reflex, not a sign you’re doing anything wrong.

The evidence is limited: mostly case reports, small case series (one described just 3 women) and one descriptive study. It isn’t yet a formally recognised diagnosis, and many health professionals haven’t heard of it.

How it differs from other things

  • Postnatal depression: low mood or loss of interest most of the day, nearly every day, for 2 weeks or more, whether or not you’re feeding. D-MER comes with let-down and lifts within minutes. You can have both, so mention both if you’re unsure. See depression.
  • Breastfeeding aversion: anger, agitation or a skin-crawling feeling that lasts the whole time the baby is latched, with a strong urge to unlatch. It’s often described during pregnancy, tandem feeding or with an older baby. D-MER is more dread or sadness, and it passes soon after let-down.
  • Postnatal anxiety: worry that’s hard to switch off and isn’t tied to feeds. See anxiety.

What helps

  • Knowing it has a name. Many people say this alone makes it easier.
  • Tracking it for a week or two: when it happens, how long it lasts, how strong it is, and what seems to make it worse. Patterns help you and your health professional.
  • Distraction at let-down: a snack or drink, a show, a podcast, your phone, or talking to someone.
  • Look after the basics: sleep when you can, drink water, and notice whether stress or caffeine make it worse.
  • Ask for support from your partner, family or friends, and drop less important jobs.
  • For severe D-MER, a doctor may talk through treatment options with you.
  • It often eases over the first few months. For some it lasts while breastfeeding continues and stops when it ends.

Whatever you decide about feeding is valid. Many people keep breastfeeding once they understand D-MER; others choose to wean. Your GP, midwife, child health nurse or a lactation consultant can help you weigh it up, without judgement.

Talk to your GP, midwife or a lactation consultant if

  • The feelings are strong, frightening or getting worse
  • They last longer than a few minutes, or happen outside let-down
  • You feel low, anxious or flat most of the day, for 2 weeks or more
  • You’re dreading feeds or thinking about stopping because of it, and want help deciding
  • You’ve had depression or anxiety before, or it’s affecting sleep, eating or bonding

Get help the same day if you have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, feel unable to keep yourself or your baby safe, feel confused, or hear or see things others don’t. Call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number, or go to the nearest emergency department.

Common questions

What is sad nipple syndrome?

A nickname for D-MER (dysphoric milk ejection reflex): a sudden wave of negative feelings, such as dread, sadness or anxiety, that starts just before milk lets down and fades within a few minutes. It isn’t really about the nipple. It’s linked to the let-down reflex.

How long does a D-MER episode last?

Usually from a few seconds to a few minutes. It starts just before let-down and eases as the milk flows. It can happen with every let-down or only the first one of a feed.

Is D-MER the same as postnatal depression?

No. D-MER is tied to let-down and lifts within minutes. Postnatal depression is low mood or loss of interest most of the day, for 2 weeks or more. They can happen together, so mention both to your GP if you’re unsure.

What causes D-MER?

The leading theory is a brief, abrupt drop in dopamine, a brain chemical linked to mood, when let-down is triggered. It’s a hypothesis based on limited evidence, mostly case reports.

Does D-MER go away?

Often it eases over the first few months, and most people find it easier to manage as their baby gets older. For some it lasts as long as they breastfeed, and it stops when breastfeeding ends.

Do I have to stop breastfeeding if I have D-MER?

No. Many people keep breastfeeding once they know what it is and have a few coping strategies. Whatever you decide about feeding is valid, and your GP, midwife or a lactation consultant can help you weigh it up.
