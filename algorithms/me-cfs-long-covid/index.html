ME/CFS and Long COVID: Treatment Algorithm

For health professionals General practice

A practical, evidence-graded path for adults with ME/CFS or long COVID: confirm and exclude, protect the energy envelope, then treat the dominant symptom clusters. Options run from guideline-backed to anecdotal but worth a careful trial in selected patients. No treatment is TGA-approved for either condition.

At a glance

  • First, do no harm: screen every patient for post-exertional malaise (PEM). If present, avoid fixed graded exercise; pace instead.
  • Exclude the treatables: anaemia, iron deficiency, thyroid, coeliac, sleep apnoea, depression, medicines, POTS. One afternoon of bloods and a 10-minute stand test.
  • Treat by cluster: orthostatic intolerance, sleep, pain, cognition, breathing, smell, mast-cell-type symptoms, gut.
  • One change at a time, start low, review at 4–8 weeks with the same outcome measures, and stop what doesn’t help.
  • Severe and very severe ME/CFS (housebound or bedbound) need home visits, sensory reduction and careful nutrition, not clinic-style plans.

Most of what helps is unglamorous: pacing, salt and fluids, sleep, and stopping the things that make it worse.

Evidence grades used here. A strong: guidelines plus consistent randomised trials. B moderate: at least one decent RCT or strong evidence in a closely related condition. C weak or mixed: small or conflicting trials. D anecdotal or mechanistic: case series or expert experience; a reasonable trial in selected patients with clear stop rules. X avoid: harmful or shown not to work.

Step 0 · Confirm and exclude

  • ME/CFS (NICE 2021; IOM 2015): substantially reduced activity with debilitating fatigue for at least 3 months (NICE suspects it at 6 weeks and diagnoses at 3 months; IOM uses 6 months), PEM, unrefreshing sleep, plus cognitive problems and/or orthostatic intolerance.
  • Long COVID (WHO): symptoms 3 months after probable or confirmed COVID-19, lasting at least 2 months, not explained by another diagnosis. Many meet ME/CFS criteria; many don’t.
  • Screen for PEM: “What happens the day after you do more than usual?” Delayed worsening (12–72 hours) lasting days is the key feature. The DePaul Symptom Questionnaire PEM items help.
  • Baseline tests: FBC, iron studies (ferritin), B12, folate, UEC, LFT, calcium, glucose or HbA1c, TSH, CRP or ESR, CK, coeliac serology, vitamin D, urinalysis. Consider morning cortisol, HIV, hepatitis and syphilis by risk, pregnancy test, and ECG.
  • Bedside: 10-minute active stand test (lying then standing heart rate and BP), STOP-BANG for sleep apnoea, PHQ-9 and GAD-7, medication review (sedatives, beta blockers, antihistamines, opioids).
  • Red flags needing other workup: weight loss, fevers or night sweats, focal neurology, chest pain, haemoptysis, persistent lymphadenopathy, desaturation on exertion, abnormal bloods.
  • Long COVID specifics: exertional breathlessness or desaturation needs a chest X-ray and lung function; chest pain or palpitations need ECG ± troponin and echo; consider pulmonary embolism, myocarditis, new diabetes and thyroiditis.

Step 1 · Core management for everyone

These apply whatever the cluster. They carry the most consensus and the least risk.

  1. B · ModerateEducation, validation and a diagnosisName it, explain PEM and the energy envelope, and set realistic goals. Being believed is itself therapeutic.
    Evidence
    Guideline consensus (NICE 2021, CDC, WHO); strong patient-reported benefit
    How
    Plain-language handout (/me/, /long-covid/); a named GP; a written plan
    Watch for
    Language that implies laziness or “just anxiety”.
  2. B · ModeratePacing within the energy envelopePlan, prioritise and break up activity; rest before crashing; stay within what’s sustainable on a bad day.
    Evidence
    Recommended by NICE 2021 and most patient bodies; trial evidence is limited, but harm from boom-bust cycles is well described
    How
    Activity diary, heart rate monitor (some aim to stay under about 55–60% of age-predicted maximum), step counts, scheduled rests
    Watch for
    Pacing that becomes avoidance of all activity in people without PEM.
  3. B · ModerateSymptom-titrated activity (only if no PEM)Long COVID without PEM: gentle, gradual rehab, adjusted to symptoms rather than fixed increments.
    Evidence
    Rehabilitation trials in long COVID show benefit in people without PEM
    How
    Physio or exercise physiologist; recumbent options first if orthostatic
    Watch for
    Any delayed crash: drop back and reassess for PEM.
  4. X · AvoidGraded exercise therapy with fixed increments (in ME/CFS)Pushing through symptoms on a fixed schedule.
    Evidence
    NICE 2021 advises against it; surveys report harm in many patients with PEM
    How
    Don’t
    Watch for
    If used historically, ask about relapse.
  5. B · ModerateCBT as support, not cureFor coping, distress, sleep and mood. Not to “change illness beliefs” or as a cure.
    Evidence
    Helps distress and coping (NICE 2021 supports this framing)
    How
    Psychologist via a GP Mental Health Treatment Plan
    Watch for
    Programs that promise recovery through thinking differently.
  6. A · StrongTreat what’s treatableIron if ferritin is low, B12 or vitamin D if deficient, thyroid, CPAP for sleep apnoea, depression, anaemia.
    Evidence
    Strong evidence for each underlying condition
    How
    Usual treatment; recheck in 3 months
    Watch for
    Over-replacement (iron overload, high-dose vitamin D).
  7. B · ModeratePractical supportsWorkplace or study adjustments, carers, mobility aids, disability and income support.
    Evidence
    Consensus; large real-world impact
    How
    Letters for work or uni, Centrelink, NDIS for severe long-term disability, aids such as shower chairs and wheelchairs for longer trips
    Watch for
    Under-recognising how disabling severe ME/CFS is.

Step 2a · Orthostatic intolerance and POTS

Common in both. Check a 10-minute stand test. See the POTS deep dive for detail.

  1. B · ModerateFluids, salt and compression2–3 L of fluid a day, extra salt where safe, waist-high or abdominal compression.
    Evidence
    Physiological rationale and small trials; guideline-recommended for POTS
    How
    Salt tablets or electrolyte drinks; bolus 500 mL water before getting up
    Watch for
    Hypertension, kidney or heart disease.
  2. C · Weak / mixedLow-dose propranololBlunts the standing heart rate.
    Evidence
    Small RCT: low dose (10 mg) improved symptoms more than higher dose (Raj 2009)
    How
    10 mg once or twice daily
    Watch for
    Asthma, low BP, fatigue, low mood.
  3. C · Weak / mixedIvabradineSlows the sinus node without lowering BP.
    Evidence
    Small trials lowered heart rate; a larger RCT lowered heart rate but didn’t improve symptoms
    How
    2.5–5 mg twice daily (off label)
    Watch for
    Visual phosphenes, bradycardia; contraception needed.
  4. C · Weak / mixedFludrocortisone or midodrineExpand volume or tighten vessels for low-BP-type symptoms.
    Evidence
    Small studies, mostly in orthostatic hypotension or POTS
    How
    Fludrocortisone 50–100 µg; midodrine 2.5–10 mg three times daily, not within 4 hours of bed
    Watch for
    Low potassium, oedema, supine hypertension.
  5. D · Anecdotal or mechanisticPyridostigmineBoosts autonomic signalling.
    Evidence
    Case series and small studies
    How
    30 mg twice to three times daily, titrate
    Watch for
    Cramps, diarrhoea, increased secretions.

Step 2b · Sleep

Unrefreshing sleep is near-universal. Treat sleep disorders first.

  1. A · StrongExclude and treat sleep apnoea and restless legsSTOP-BANG, sleep study if indicated; ferritin for restless legs.
    Evidence
    Strong evidence for CPAP and iron in RLS
    How
    Home sleep study; iron if ferritin under about 75
    Watch for
    Sedatives worsening untreated apnoea.
  2. B · ModerateRegular sleep window and adapted CBT-IFixed wake time, wind-down routine, limit long daytime naps but allow planned rest.
    Evidence
    CBT-I is first line for insomnia; strict sleep restriction may need adapting in ME/CFS
    How
    Psychologist or online CBT-I programs
    Watch for
    Over-strict sleep restriction triggering crashes.
  3. C · Weak / mixedMelatoninFor delayed sleep onset.
    Evidence
    Helps sleep onset in general populations; small studies in ME/CFS
    How
    1–2 mg 1–2 hours before bed
    Watch for
    Morning grogginess.
  4. C · Weak / mixedLow-dose amitriptylineWhen sleep and pain coexist.
    Evidence
    Good evidence in fibromyalgia for sleep and pain; small effects
    How
    5–10 mg at night, up to 25–30 mg
    Watch for
    Morning hangover, dry mouth, constipation, orthostatic worsening.

Step 2c · Pain

Myalgia, headache and neuropathic-type pain overlap with fibromyalgia. Borrow fibromyalgia evidence.

  1. B · ModerateDuloxetine or amitriptylineCentral pain modulation.
    Evidence
    Moderate evidence in fibromyalgia (Cochrane); benefit for a minority
    How
    Duloxetine 30 mg, then 60 mg; amitriptyline as above
    Watch for
    Nausea, sweating, orthostatic effects.
  2. C · Weak / mixedPregabalinFor neuropathic-type pain.
    Evidence
    Modest benefit in fibromyalgia; frequent side effects
    How
    Start 25–75 mg at night
    Watch for
    Dizziness, sedation, weight gain, cognitive dulling; misuse.
  3. C · Weak / mixedLow-dose naltrexone (LDN)Thought to modulate microglia and pain signalling.
    Evidence
    Small RCTs in fibromyalgia (mixed, mostly small benefit); open-label long COVID studies positive; larger RCTs under way
    How
    Compounded 1.5 mg at night, rising to 3–4.5 mg over weeks
    Watch for
    Vivid dreams, insomnia; not with opioids.
  4. X · AvoidOpioids for chronic symptomsLong-term opioids for widespread pain.
    Evidence
    No benefit for central pain; risks of dependence and worse pain
    How
    Avoid
    Watch for

Step 2d · Cognition (“brain fog”)

Pace cognitive load like physical load.

  1. B · ModerateCognitive pacing and aidsShort tasks, breaks, lists, alarms, reduced multitasking.
    Evidence
    Consensus and occupational therapy experience
    How
    OT; written plans; quiet workspace
    Watch for
  2. D · Anecdotal or mechanisticGuanfacine plus N-acetylcysteinePrefrontal noradrenergic support plus antioxidant.
    Evidence
    Case series in long COVID brain fog (Yale, 2023)
    How
    Guanfacine 1 mg at night, rising to 2 mg; NAC 600 mg daily
    Watch for
    Low BP, sedation.
  3. D · Anecdotal or mechanisticStimulants or modafinilFor marked fatigue or cognitive slowing.
    Evidence
    Small, mixed trials in ME/CFS; may cause crashes by masking limits
    How
    Low dose, cautious trial with clear goals
    Watch for
    Overdoing it then crashing; insomnia; misuse.
  4. D · Anecdotal or mechanisticLow-dose aripiprazoleProposed to modulate neuroinflammation.
    Evidence
    Retrospective series only (Crosby 2021)
    How
    0.25–2 mg daily
    Watch for
    Akathisia, insomnia.

Step 2e · Mast-cell-type symptoms

Flushing, itch, hives, food reactions, gut upset, palpitations. Don’t label MCAS without evidence; treat symptoms.

  1. D · Anecdotal or mechanisticH1 plus H2 antihistaminese.g. fexofenadine or cetirizine plus famotidine.
    Evidence
    Observational long COVID reports of improvement (Glynne 2022); no RCTs
    How
    Fexofenadine 180 mg daily (up to twice daily) plus famotidine 20 mg twice daily; trial 4–6 weeks
    Watch for
    Sedation (cetirizine), headache.
  2. D · Anecdotal or mechanisticKetotifen or sodium cromoglycateMast-cell stabilisers.
    Evidence
    Small studies in mastocytosis; anecdotal in long COVID
    How
    Specialist or compounded
    Watch for
    Weight gain, sedation (ketotifen).
  3. D · Anecdotal or mechanisticQuercetinFlavonoid with mast-cell-stabilising effects in the lab.
    Evidence
    Mechanistic and small studies only
    How
    500 mg daily or twice daily
    Watch for
    Interactions; liver.

Step 2f · Breathing and smell (long COVID)

  1. B · ModerateBreathing retrainingFor dysfunctional breathing: nasal, slow, diaphragmatic.
    Evidence
    Physiotherapy evidence in dysfunctional breathing; long COVID pilot trials
    How
    Respiratory physio
    Watch for
    Exclude PE, lung and heart causes first.
  2. B · ModerateOlfactory trainingSniff 4 strong scents twice a day for 3–6 months.
    Evidence
    Supported by meta-analyses in post-viral smell loss
    How
    Rose, lemon, clove, eucalyptus
    Watch for
    Parosmia can be distressing; persist.
  3. C · Weak / mixedSteroid nasal spray or rinseFor smell loss with nasal inflammation.
    Evidence
    Mixed evidence
    How
    Mometasone daily; budesonide rinse via specialist
    Watch for

Step 2g · Gut

Many have IBS-type symptoms, reflux or nausea.

  1. B · ModerateTreat as for IBS or functional dyspepsiaSoluble fibre, low-FODMAP with a dietitian, antispasmodics, gut-brain modulators.
    Evidence
    Strong evidence in IBS; extrapolated
    How
    See /ibs/
    Watch for
    Weight loss or restrictive eating in severe illness.

Step 3 · Specialist, experimental or emerging

For selected patients, ideally in trials or with specialist input.

  1. C · Weak / mixedHyperbaric oxygen (long COVID)High-pressure oxygen sessions.
    Evidence
    One sham-controlled RCT (Israel, 2022) improved cognition and energy; needs replication
    How
    40 sessions in specialist centres; costly
    Watch for
    Ear barotrauma; cost; access.
  2. C · Weak / mixedCoenzyme Q10 with NADHMitochondrial support.
    Evidence
    One small RCT in ME/CFS showed modest fatigue benefit; a long COVID CoQ10 RCT was negative
    How
    CoQ10 200 mg + NADH 20 mg daily
    Watch for
    Cost; interactions with warfarin.
  3. D · Anecdotal or mechanisticStellate ganglion blockProposed to reset autonomic signalling.
    Evidence
    Case series; controlled data lacking
    How
    Interventional pain specialist
    Watch for
    Procedural risks, cost.
  4. D · Anecdotal or mechanisticImmunoadsorption, IVIG, other immunotherapiesFor suspected autoimmunity.
    Evidence
    Small or negative trials; experimental
    How
    Trials only
    Watch for
    Cost, risk.
  5. B · ModerateMetformin during acute COVID (prevention)Taken for 2 weeks during acute infection.
    Evidence
    COVID-OUT RCT: about 41% fewer long COVID diagnoses in overweight or obese adults
    How
    500 mg, titrated to 1,500 mg daily for 14 days (off label)
    Watch for
    Gut upset; not for long COVID once established.
  6. X · AvoidRituximabB-cell depletion.
    Evidence
    Large RCT (RituxME, 2019) showed no benefit in ME/CFS
    How
    Avoid
    Watch for
  7. X · AvoidExtended Paxlovid for established long COVID15–25-day courses.
    Evidence
    RCTs (STOP-PASC, others) showed no benefit
    How
    Avoid outside trials
    Watch for
  8. X · AvoidUnproven clinicsStem cells, IV vitamin infusions, chelation, ozone, “detox”.
    Evidence
    No evidence; cost and harm
    How
    Avoid
    Watch for

Severe and very severe ME/CFS

  • Home visits or telehealth; reduce light, noise and touch; short, planned contacts.
  • Watch nutrition and hydration, pressure care, bone health and orthostatic tolerance.
  • Admissions need a plan: quiet single room, minimal handling, a support person.
  • Coordinate care and disability support (NDIS, carers, equipment).

Measuring progress

  • Pick 2–3 measures and repeat them at each review: Bell CFS disability scale, daily steps or upright time, DePaul PEM frequency, a single 0–10 fatigue score, sleep quality.
  • Change one thing at a time; trial 4–8 weeks; stop what doesn’t help.
  • Expect fluctuation. Many people improve slowly; some recover, especially in long COVID.

Common questions

Is graded exercise therapy recommended for ME/CFS?

No. NICE (2021) advises against graded exercise therapy with fixed increments for ME/CFS. People without post-exertional malaise, including many with long COVID, may benefit from symptom-titrated rehabilitation.

What is the first step in managing ME/CFS or long COVID?

Confirm the diagnosis, exclude treatable causes, screen for post-exertional malaise and teach pacing within the energy envelope. Then treat the dominant symptom clusters such as orthostatic intolerance, sleep and pain.

Is there a cure for long COVID?

Not yet. Many people improve over time. Treatments target symptoms; several promising ideas are still in trials, and some (like extended Paxlovid and rituximab in ME/CFS) have not worked in RCTs.
