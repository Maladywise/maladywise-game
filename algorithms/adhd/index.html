ADHD: Treatment Algorithm

For health professionals General practice

An evidence-graded path for ADHD in children and adults, built on the AADPA 2022 Australian guideline and NICE: assess well, treat co-occurring conditions, then titrate medication with clear monitoring. Prescribing rules for stimulants vary by state: check yours.

Evidence grades: A strong · B moderate · C weak or mixed · D anecdotal · X avoid.

Step 0 · Assess

  • DSM-5 criteria: symptoms before 12, in 2 or more settings, with impairment. Collateral history (family, school reports) matters.
  • Screen: ASRS v1.1 (adults), Vanderbilt or Conners (children). Screens don’t diagnose.
  • Look for co-occurring and mimicking conditions: autism, anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, substance use, sleep apnoea and poor sleep, learning disorders, thyroid disease, iron deficiency, hearing or vision problems, trauma.
  • Cardiac screen before stimulants: personal or family history of sudden death, syncope on exertion, structural heart disease; BP and heart rate. ECG only if indicated.
  • Baseline height, weight, BP, heart rate, sleep and appetite.

Step 2 · Medication

Titrate weekly to effect or side effects; review at each step.

  1. A · StrongStimulants: methylphenidate (children first line) or lisdexamfetamine / dexamfetamine (adults first line)Short- or long-acting; long-acting preferred for adherence.
    Evidence
    Large effect sizes in a network meta-analysis (Cortese 2018): roughly 0.8–1.0 in children and 0.5–0.8 in adults
    How
    Start low; titrate weekly; S8 rules apply
    Watch for
    Appetite, sleep, heart rate and BP, mood, tics, growth in children.
  2. A · StrongAtomoxetineWhen stimulants fail, aren’t tolerated or misuse is a concern.
    Evidence
    Moderate effect size (~0.5); 4–12 weeks to work
    How
    0.5 then 1.2 mg/kg in children; 40 then 80 mg in adults
    Watch for
    Nausea, sexual side effects, suicidal thoughts in youth, liver.
  3. B · ModerateGuanfacine extended releaseNon-stimulant; helpful with tics, sleep, emotional dysregulation, or as an add-on.
    Evidence
    Moderate evidence in children and adolescents; adult evidence growing
    How
    1 mg, titrate weekly to 0.05–0.12 mg/kg; PBS for 6–17 with criteria
    Watch for
    Sedation, low BP, rebound hypertension if stopped suddenly.
  4. C · Weak / mixedClonidineSleep onset, tics, aggression; add-on.
    Evidence
    Small trials
    How
    50 µg at night, titrate
    Watch for
    Sedation, low BP; taper to stop.
  5. C · Weak / mixedBupropion (off label)Adults with co-occurring depression or substance use concerns.
    Evidence
    Small to moderate benefit, less than stimulants
    How
    150 mg, then 150 mg twice daily
    Watch for
    Seizure risk, insomnia; CYP2D6 interactions.
  6. D · Anecdotal or mechanisticModafinil (off label)Occasionally tried in adults.
    Evidence
    Trials in adults negative or mixed
    How
    Watch for
    Contraception interaction; rash.
  7. X · AvoidNeurofeedback, elimination diets, supplement stacks
    Evidence
    Blinded trials show little or no benefit
    How

Special situations

  • ADHD and autism: stimulants still help, though response is lower and side effects (irritability) more common: start lower, go slower.
  • Anxiety: treat the most impairing first; stimulants often reduce anxiety caused by ADHD chaos. Atomoxetine or guanfacine if anxiety worsens.
  • Substance use: long-acting formulations (lisdexamfetamine) have lower misuse potential; consider atomoxetine.
  • Bipolar disorder: stabilise mood first.
  • Pregnancy: individualise; recent data are reassuring for stimulants, but discuss risks and benefits.
  • Monitoring: BP, heart rate, weight (height in children) every 6 months; yearly review of need and dose.

Common questions

What is first-line medication for ADHD?

Stimulants: methylphenidate first in children, and lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate in adults, titrated weekly. Atomoxetine and guanfacine are alternatives.

Do children need an ECG before stimulants?

Not routinely. Take a cardiac history and measure BP and heart rate; do an ECG if there’s personal or family cardiac history or abnormal findings.
