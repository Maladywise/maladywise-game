Fact sheet

COPD

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: long-term lung damage, mostly from smoking, that narrows the airways and makes breathing harder over years. The damage can’t be undone, but quitting, lung rehab and the right inhalers help people breathe easier and do more.

Symptoms

  • Breathlessness, first with effort (stairs, hills), later with everyday tasks.
  • A long-term cough, often with phlegm.
  • Chest infections that are more frequent or slow to clear, and tiredness.
  • Flare-ups (exacerbations): a sudden worsening, often set off by a cold or flu.
  • Early on there may be no symptoms, or they’re put down to ageing or being unfit.

Causes and risk factors

  • Smoking is the main cause. About 1 in 4 or 5 people who smoke develop COPD, and past smoking still counts.
  • Second-hand smoke, including before birth and in childhood.
  • Dust, fumes and chemicals at work (such as mining, construction, farming and manufacturing) and air pollution.
  • Childhood lung problems, tuberculosis, long-standing asthma, and a rare inherited condition (alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency).
  • Rates are higher among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people.

How it’s diagnosed

  • A breathing test (spirometry) measures how fast you can blow air out, before and after an inhaled medicine. It’s the test that confirms COPD.
  • A chest X-ray or CT scan and blood tests may look for other causes.
  • Ask for a test if you’re over 35, have smoked or worked with dust or fumes, and are breathless or coughing.

The COPD ladder

Quitting smoking, lung rehab and vaccines are the base everyone stands on. Inhalers are added in steps. Most people need several rungs at once.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careStop smoking, completelyCounselling plus medicine gives you the best chance.
    Works
    The most important thing you can do: it stops adding new damage and slows the decline
    Cost
    Quitline free; nicotine replacement and quit medicines on the PBS
    How long
    For good; most people need several attempts
    Watch for
    A slip isn’t failure. Get back on.

    Every cigarette not smoked is a breath you get to keep.

  2. Step 2 · Self-careVaccinesYearly flu vaccine, pneumococcal vaccine, and keep COVID-19 and other recommended vaccines up to date.
    Works
    Fewer chest infections means fewer flare-ups and hospital stays
    Cost
    Often free for people with chronic lung disease; ask your GP or pharmacist
    How long
    Flu yearly; others as advised
    Watch for
    Mild sore arm and achiness for a day or two.

    Five minutes in a chair that may save you a week in a hospital bed.

  3. Step 3 · ProgramPulmonary (lung) rehabilitationA supervised exercise and education program, usually over 6–8 weeks, with breathing and airway-clearance techniques.
    Works
    Improves quality of life, breathlessness and walking distance (about 40 metres more in 6 minutes). After a flare-up, it lowers the chance of going back to hospital
    Cost
    Varies; many hospital and community programs cost little or nothing. Ask your GP for a referral
    How long
    6–8 weeks, then keep exercising
    Watch for
    Start within a few weeks of a flare-up if you can.

    Yes, exercise makes you breathless. That’s the point: your muscles learn to need less oxygen.

  4. Step 4 · PrescriptionInhalers (relievers and long-acting inhalers)A reliever for breathlessness, then one or two long-acting openers once a day. An inhaled steroid is added for people with frequent flare-ups.
    Works
    Better breathing and fewer flare-ups. With tiotropium, a common long-acting inhaler, the share of people with a flare-up in a year fell from 44% to 38%
    Cost
    Under the $25 PBS co-payment per inhaler; $7.70 concession
    How long
    Daily, long term
    Watch for
    Dry mouth, mouth thrush with steroid inhalers (rinse after). Technique matters as much as the medicine.

    Good inhalers can’t help if they end up on your tongue. Get your technique checked.

  5. Step 5 · PrescriptionA flare-up planA written COPD action plan, with steroid tablets and sometimes antibiotics to start early when symptoms worsen.
    Works
    Treating flare-ups early can shorten them and help keep you out of hospital
    Cost
    Under the $25 PBS co-payment
    How long
    Each flare-up, about 5 days of tablets
    Watch for
    Steroid tablets raise blood sugar and can affect mood and sleep.

    Plan for the storm before it arrives.

  6. Step 6 · SpecialistHome oxygenFor people whose blood oxygen stays low at rest. Assessed by a respiratory specialist with blood tests.
    Works
    Used at least 15 hours a day, it helps people with very low oxygen live longer: in one trial, deaths over 3 years were 45% vs 67%
    Cost
    Funding varies by state and country
    How long
    Long term, ideally 15+ hours a day
    Watch for
    Never smoke or use open flames near oxygen: it’s a serious fire risk.

    Oxygen isn’t for breathlessness alone; it’s for low oxygen levels.

Costs are rough Australian prices in November 2026 and vary by pharmacy and provider. PBS co-payment: $25, or $7.70 with a concession card. Results are typical figures from trials, not promises.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • “Lung detox” teas and supplementsNo good evidence they repair lung damage or improve breathing. Quitting and rehab do.
  • Stem cell treatments for COPDSold privately at high prices, but not proven to help in good trials. Ask your specialist before spending.
  • Oxygen for breathlessness aloneHome oxygen helps people whose blood oxygen is low. It isn’t a treatment for breathlessness when oxygen levels are normal.

What you can do

  • Quit smoking: call Quitline (13 78 48) and ask your GP about quit medicines.
  • Ask your GP for a referral to pulmonary rehab, then keep moving with a community class afterwards.
  • Get a written COPD action plan so you know what to do when symptoms worsen.
  • Ask your pharmacist to check your inhaler technique at least once a year.
  • Keep up your flu and pneumococcal vaccines.
  • Stay active every day; rehab teaches you how hard to push.

See your GP if

  • You’re over 35, have smoked or worked with dust or fumes, and are breathless or coughing
  • You’re more breathless than usual, coughing up more phlegm, or the colour has changed
  • You’ve had a flare-up and haven’t done lung rehab
  • Your ankles are swelling or you’re unusually tired

Call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number for severe breathlessness that stops you talking, walking or sleeping, blue lips or fingertips, confusion or drowsiness, chest pain, or a fast or irregular heartbeat. From a mobile, 112 works in most countries.

Common questions

Is COPD the same as asthma?

No. Asthma airways narrow and widen again; in COPD the narrowing is mostly permanent. Some people have features of both, and treatment is tailored.

Can my lungs heal if I stop smoking?

The damage already done doesn’t reverse, but quitting slows further loss of lung function at any age, and many people cough less within months.

Is it safe to exercise when I’m breathless?

Yes, in stable COPD. Pace yourself and use your reliever as your plan says. Lung rehab teaches you how far to push.

Will I need oxygen?

Most people with COPD don’t. Oxygen helps when blood oxygen is low at rest, which is measured with a blood test.

What are the first signs of COPD?

Getting out of breath with activity you used to manage, a daily cough with phlegm, wheeze and chest infections that take longer to clear. People often put it down to age or fitness, so a breathing test (spirometry) is worth asking about if you smoke or used to.

What are the stages of COPD?

Doctors grade COPD by how much your breathing test is reduced (mild to very severe) and by your symptoms and flare-ups. The grade guides which inhalers and supports you need, but how you feel and how often you flare matter as much as the number.

Is COPD hereditary?

Mostly it’s caused by smoking or other smoke and dust exposure. About 1 to 2 in 100 people with COPD have alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, an inherited condition. A blood test is worth doing if COPD starts young or runs in the family.
