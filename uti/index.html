Urinary tract infection (UTI)

What it is

A UTI is an infection in the bladder or urinary tract, usually caused by bacteria from the bowel. It's very common in women because the urethra (the tube from the bladder) is short. About half of all women will have at least one.

Common symptoms

  • Burning or stinging when you pass urine
  • Needing to go often or urgently
  • Cloudy, smelly or bloody urine
  • Lower tummy pain
  • In older people, confusion or new incontinence may be the only sign

Things that raise the risk

  • Recent sex
  • Spermicides or a contraceptive diaphragm
  • Menopause
  • Diabetes
  • Kidney stones or a catheter
  • Having had a UTI before

Treatment

  • Your GP may test your urine and prescribe a short course of antibiotics. Finish the course.
  • Pain relief and plenty of water can ease symptoms.
  • Urine alkalisers from the pharmacy may reduce stinging, but they don't treat the infection.
  • Symptoms usually improve within 1 to 2 days of starting antibiotics.

Prevention

  • Drink enough water so your urine is pale.
  • Pass urine soon after sex.
  • Wipe front to back.
  • Don't hold on for long periods.
  • If UTIs keep coming back, talk to your GP about other options, including vaginal oestrogen after menopause.

See your GP if

  • You're pregnant, male or it's a child with symptoms
  • Symptoms aren't better after 2 days of antibiotics
  • You have 2 or more UTIs in 6 months
  • There's blood in your urine without other symptoms

See a doctor the same day if you have fever, shivers, back or side pain, or vomiting. These can mean a kidney infection.
