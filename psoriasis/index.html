Fact sheet

Psoriasis

An immune condition that speeds up skin turnover: raised red patches with silvery scale on elbows, knees, scalp and back. Not contagious. It comes and goes, and treatment now works remarkably well.

The psoriasis ladder

Creams first, then light, tablets or injections for widespread or stubborn psoriasis. Give creams 4 weeks and bigger treatments about 3 months.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careMoisturise, quit smoking, less alcohol, healthy weightThe unglamorous basics that make every other treatment work better.
    Works
    Small trials: weight loss gives a modest extra improvement; moisturiser softens scale
    Cost
    About $10–25 a tub of moisturiser
    How long
    Daily, long term
    Watch for
    Long hot showers and harsh soaps dry skin out.

    Moisturiser is boring. Boring works.

  2. Step 2 · PharmacyTar or salicylic acid shampoos and creamsSoften and lift scale, especially on the scalp.
    Works
    Small benefit; weaker than vitamin D or steroid creams in trials
    Cost
    About $15–30
    How long
    A few times a week; leave shampoo on 5–10 minutes
    Watch for
    Tar smells and stains. Salicylic acid can irritate.

    Your bathroom may smell like fresh roadworks. Your scalp won’t mind.

  3. Step 3 · PrescriptionSteroid ointment or creamThe first treatment for most patches. Ointments suit thick scale; milder strengths on the face and skin folds.
    Works
    Clearly beats base cream in trials (stronger ones more so); most patches improve within 2–4 weeks
    Cost
    Under the $25 PBS co-payment, about $15–25 a tube
    How long
    Courses of up to 4 weeks, then breaks
    Watch for
    Skin thinning with long or strong use, especially on the face and folds.

    Courses, not a lifestyle.

  4. Step 4 · PrescriptionCalcipotriol + betamethasone gel, foam or ointmentVitamin D and a steroid in one, once a day. Great for plaques and the scalp.
    Works
    About half are clear or almost clear after 4–8 weeks; beats either ingredient alone
    Cost
    $25 on the PBS ($7.70 concession)
    How long
    Daily for up to 4 weeks, then as needed or twice a week
    Watch for
    Irritation. Not for the face.

    Once a day. A medicine that respects your schedule.

  5. Step 5 · ProcedureNarrowband UVB light therapyClinic sessions 2–3 times a week, through a dermatologist.
    Works
    About 6 to 7 in 10 clear or nearly clear
    Cost
    Often Medicare-covered, sometimes with a small gap per session
    How long
    Usually 15–25 sessions over 6–10 weeks
    Watch for
    Sunburn-like redness. Skin cancer risk rises with many courses.

    Not a solarium. Those are banned for a reason.

  6. Step 6 · SpecialistTablets that calm the immune systemSuch as methotrexate, acitretin, ciclosporin, apremilast or deucravacitinib, for moderate to severe psoriasis.
    Works
    About 3 to 7 in 10 get a big improvement, depending on the medicine
    Cost
    Usually $25 or less per script on the PBS ($7.70 concession); some only via a dermatologist
    How long
    2–4 months to judge; long term with check-ups
    Watch for
    Regular blood tests. Methotrexate and acitretin cause birth defects (acitretin: no pregnancy for 3 years after stopping).

    The blood tests are the price of admission. Pay it.

  7. Step 7 · SpecialistBiologic injectionsFor severe psoriasis when other treatments haven’t worked.
    Works
    Newer ones get about 7 to 9 in 10 people mostly or completely clear
    Cost
    $25 per script on the PBS ($7.70 concession) if you meet the criteria, via a dermatologist
    How long
    Injections every 2–12 weeks; reviewed at about 3–4 months
    Watch for
    Infection risk. Screening for TB and hepatitis first.

    The results are stunning. The PBS paperwork is the only sticky bit.

Costs are rough Australian prices in October 2026 and vary by pharmacy. PBS co-payment: $25, or $7.70 with a concession card. Results are typical figures from trials.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Vitamin D tabletsA 2023 trial found they didn’t improve psoriasis. The vitamin D that works comes in a tube (calcipotriol), not a capsule.
  • Fish oil capsulesTrials are small and mixed, with no reliable benefit for skin. Expensive burps.
  • Gluten-free dietOnly helps if you have coeliac disease or gluten antibodies. For everyone else it’s just pricier bread.
  • Turmeric and curcuminSmall, low-quality studies. Great in a curry, unproven on your elbows.
  • Aloe vera gelTrials are mixed, and one found it no better than a dummy treatment. Soothing, maybe; treatment, no.

What you can do

  • Moisturise every day, ideally just after a short, warm shower. Use soap-free washes.
  • For the scalp, leave medicated shampoo on for 5 to 10 minutes before rinsing.
  • Quit smoking and cut back on alcohol: both make psoriasis worse.
  • Avoid scratching and skin injuries, as new patches can form there.
  • Tell your doctor about all your medicines, as some (such as lithium or beta blockers) can trigger flares.

See your GP if

  • Psoriasis isn’t improving after 4 weeks of treatment
  • Patches cover large areas, or affect the face, genitals, hands or feet
  • Swollen, painful or stiff joints, or sore heels
  • It’s affecting your sleep, mood or relationships

Go to the emergency department if most of your skin becomes red, hot and painful, or you get widespread pus spots with fever or feel unwell.

Some psoriasis tablets, including methotrexate and acitretin, cause birth defects, and steroid tablets can cause a severe flare when stopped. Tell your doctor if you’re pregnant or planning to be.

Common questions

Is psoriasis contagious?

No. It is an immune condition that speeds up skin cell growth, and it comes and goes over a lifetime.

What is the best cream for psoriasis?

Steroid ointments are the first treatment for most patches. A gel, foam or ointment combining calcipotriol and betamethasone, once a day for up to 4 weeks, is very effective for plaques and the scalp.

Can I get biologics for psoriasis on the PBS?

Yes, for severe psoriasis when other treatments haven’t worked, through a dermatologist if you meet the criteria. The cost is the usual PBS co-payment.

Is psoriasis linked to joint pain?

About 1 in 4 people with psoriasis also get psoriatic arthritis. See your GP for swollen, painful or stiff joints, or sore heels.
