Fact sheet

Congenital Ptosis

A droopy upper eyelid that a baby is born with. It’s usually from a weak lifting muscle and is often harmless to look at, but it matters for one big reason: a growing eye that can’t see properly can lose vision for life. Most children do very well with checks and, when needed, surgery.

What it is

  • The muscle that lifts the upper lid (the levator) didn’t develop fully, so the lid sits lower than normal. One or both eyes can be affected.
  • It’s present from birth and usually stays about the same. It doesn’t normally get worse.
  • Sometimes it comes with other eye differences, such as a turned eye (squint) or a need for glasses.

Why it matters: vision

  • Babies’ eyes learn to see in the first years of life. If the lid blocks the pupil, or causes blurry vision, that eye can become lazy (amblyopia).
  • In one study of children with congenital ptosis, about half of the eyes that could be tested had amblyopia. Most of it was from blurry focus (astigmatism) or a squint, not just the lid itself.
  • Lazy eye is very treatable when found early, usually with glasses and patching. Found late, the vision loss can be permanent.

Signs to look for

  • One lid sits lower than the other, or both look heavy.
  • Your child tips their chin up or raises their eyebrows to see under the lid.
  • The lid covers part or all of the pupil (the black centre of the eye).
  • The eye seems to turn in or out, or your child shuts or covers one eye.

When to see a doctor

  • Any droopy eyelid in a baby should be checked by your GP, who will look at the pupils, eye movements and red reflex and refer to an eye specialist.
  • See a doctor soon if the lid covers the pupil, blocks vision, or still droops after 2 months of age.
  • See a doctor the same day if a droopy eyelid appears suddenly, especially with a large or small pupil, a turned eye or other new symptoms. That’s not congenital ptosis and needs urgent checks.

Treatment

  • Mild ptosis that doesn’t block vision often just needs regular eye checks, plus glasses or patching if a lazy eye develops.
  • Surgery lifts the lid. Most often the lifting muscle is shortened. If it’s very weak, the lid is attached to the forehead muscle with a small sling, so raising the eyebrows lifts the lid.
  • Surgery is done early if the lid blocks vision. Otherwise it’s often planned for preschool age, when the face is bigger and measurements are more reliable.
  • Lids may not end up perfectly even, and some children need more than one operation. Eye checks continue afterwards.

Common questions

Will my baby’s droopy eyelid get better on its own?

Congenital ptosis usually stays about the same. It doesn’t normally improve, but it doesn’t usually get worse either. What matters most is whether vision develops normally, so regular eye checks are important.

Can a droopy eyelid cause a lazy eye?

Yes. If the lid covers the pupil, or the eye is out of focus because of it, that eye may not learn to see properly. This is called amblyopia. It’s very treatable if found early, usually with glasses and patching.

What age is ptosis surgery done?

Early, if the lid blocks vision. If vision is developing well, surgery is often planned around preschool age. Your eye specialist will advise based on how much the lid droops and how well the muscle works.

Is a droopy eyelid in a baby ever serious?

A droopy lid present since birth is usually congenital ptosis. A droopy lid that appears suddenly, or comes with a different-sized pupil or a turned eye, needs a doctor the same day.
