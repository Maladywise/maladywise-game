Fact sheet

Trigger Finger

A finger or thumb that clicks, catches or locks bent, then snaps straight. A tendon is catching at the mouth of its tunnel. Very fixable: an injection works for most, and a small operation for the rest.

Typical signs: a painful click or catch when you bend or straighten a finger or thumb, often worst in the morning. There may be a tender lump in the palm at the base of the finger. In bad cases the finger gets stuck bent and needs help to straighten.

Why it happens: the tendon or its tunnel thickens so the tendon can’t glide smoothly. It’s more common over 40, in women, and in people with diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis or gout. Often there’s no clear cause.

Diagnosis: from what your GP sees and feels. Tests and scans are rarely needed.

The trigger finger ladder

Mild cases can settle on their own. If it’s painful or getting in the way, an injection or splint is first, surgery if it keeps coming back.

    Works
    Relieves pain and triggering in about 7 in 10; less likely to work, and more likely to return, with diabetes
    Cost
    About $0–250 depending on the clinic; sometimes bulk-billed
    How long
    Often helps within days, sometimes a few weeks
    Watch for
    Skin thinning or lightening; brief pain flare. Diabetes: blood sugar can rise for a few days.

  4. Step 4 · SpecialistSurgery (trigger finger release)A small cut in the palm under local anaesthetic to open the tunnel. Offered if triggering persists or returns after injections, if the finger is locked, or often earlier with diabetes.
    Works
    Usually a permanent cure, with better results at a year than a single injection; complications under 3 in 100
    Cost
    Free in the public system (expect a wait); private costs vary
    How long
    2–4 weeks to recover; light use the same day
    Watch for
    Infection, tenderness of the scar, stiffness. Rarely, a nerve injury.

    A small operation with a big success rate.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Massage or stretching to “break up” the noduleNo evidence it fixes the catching.
  • Laser or therapeutic ultrasoundNo good evidence for trigger finger.
  • ScansNot needed to diagnose a typical trigger finger.
  • Repeated injections into the same fingerIf two haven’t worked, surgery usually beats a third.

What you can do

  • Use padded gloves or thicker handles for gripping tools.
  • Gently bend and straighten the finger in warm water in the morning if it’s stiff.
  • Keep blood sugar well managed if you have diabetes.
  • Try a night splint if the finger is catching but not locking.

See your GP if

  • The finger is painful or catching often enough to bother you
  • It locks and you can’t straighten it
  • More than one finger is affected, or you have diabetes
  • You have numbness or tingling in the fingers (it could be carpal tunnel too)

See a doctor the same day for a red, hot, swollen finger, pain when you try to straighten it, or swelling along the finger after a cut, splinter or bite. Tendon sheath infection is an emergency.

Common questions

Will trigger finger go away on its own?

Mild cases sometimes do within weeks. If it’s painful, locking or affecting your hand use, treatment works well.

How well does a steroid injection work?

About 7 in 10 people get relief, often within days. It’s less effective in people with diabetes, and triggering can return.

Is surgery a big deal?

Usually not. It’s a short procedure under local anaesthetic, you can use the hand lightly the same day, and recovery takes about 2 to 4 weeks.

Can it happen in children?

Yes, most often in the thumb of babies and toddlers. It’s managed differently, so see your GP.

What causes trigger finger?

The tendon that bends the finger thickens or its tunnel narrows, so it catches as it slides. It’s more common in people aged 40 to 60, in women, and in people with diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis or underactive thyroid.

What does trigger finger feel like?

Clicking, catching or locking of a finger or thumb, often worse in the morning, with a tender lump in the palm at the base of the finger. A locked finger may need the other hand to straighten it.

Do splints help trigger finger?

Yes, for milder cases. Wearing a splint that keeps the finger straight, often at night, for 6 to 10 weeks settles symptoms for around half to two-thirds of people.
