Muscle Cramps

A sudden, painful knot in a muscle, most often the calf, often at night. Usually harmless, very common, and annoyingly hard to prevent. Stretching is the best-tested fix; most tablets disappoint.

Typical signs: a muscle suddenly tightens and hardens, usually the calf or foot, for seconds to minutes. The muscle can feel sore for hours afterwards. About 3 in 4 happen at night.

Why it happens: often no clear cause. Cramps are more common with age (about 1 in 3 people over 60), in pregnancy (about 1 in 3, mostly late), after hard exercise, with dehydration, and with some medicines such as statins and water tablets (diuretics), or with liver, kidney, thyroid or nerve problems.

Diagnosis: from your story and examination. Your GP may review your medicines and order blood tests (such as kidney function, salts, sugar and thyroid) if cramps are frequent or unusual.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Magnesium tabletsFor older adults with ordinary night cramps, a Cochrane review found magnesium unlikely to help, and it often causes diarrhoea. Evidence in pregnancy is mixed and uncertain.
  • Quinine and tonic waterPrescription quinine is no longer used for cramps because of serious side effects, and tonic water is not a treatment.
  • Sports drinks and electrolyte tablets for everyday crampsNo good evidence they prevent night cramps in people who aren’t dehydrated.
  • Pickle juice, mustard and soap under the sheetsFun folklore, little or no evidence. The soap does nothing, but it does make the bed smell nice.

What you can do

  • Stretch your calves and hamstrings every night before bed for at least 6 weeks.
  • Keep active through the day; a daily walk helps.
  • Loosen the bedding at your feet.
  • Ask your GP or pharmacist whether any of your medicines can cause cramps.
  • In pregnancy, cramps usually ease after the birth. Ask your midwife or GP before taking supplements.

See your GP if

  • Cramps are frequent, disturb your sleep or affect your quality of life
  • Cramps started after a new medicine
  • You also have muscle weakness, wasting, twitching, numbness, or cramps in many muscles
  • Cramps come with leg pain on walking that eases with rest (possible poor circulation)

See a doctor the same day for a cramp lasting more than 10 minutes that doesn’t ease with stretching, a swollen, red, warm or tender calf (possible blood clot), cramps or stiffness after a dirty wound (tetanus risk), or dark urine with severe muscle pain and weakness after hard exercise or a new medicine.

Common questions

What causes night leg cramps?

Often there’s no clear cause. They’re more common with age, in pregnancy, after hard exercise and with some medicines such as statins and diuretics.

Does magnesium help leg cramps?

For older adults with ordinary night cramps, probably not: a Cochrane review found no meaningful benefit. Evidence in pregnancy is unclear.

Why don’t doctors prescribe quinine for cramps anymore?

It only helps a little and can cause serious side effects, including dangerously low platelets. Regulators in Australia, New Zealand, the UK and the US removed it for cramps.

How do I stop a cramp quickly?

Stretch the muscle: pull your toes up towards your knee or stand with your weight on the cramped leg and heel down, then walk around and massage it.

What deficiency causes leg cramps?

Usually none. Most night cramps happen in people with normal blood tests. Low magnesium, potassium or calcium can rarely cause cramps, usually with other symptoms, and water tablets (diuretics) can play a part.

Are leg cramps a sign of something serious?

Rarely. See a GP if cramps are frequent and severe, come with muscle weakness, wasting or numbness, or if your calf aches every time you walk and eases with rest, which can be a sign of narrowed leg arteries.

Why do I get leg cramps in pregnancy?

Leg cramps are common in the second and third trimesters, possibly from changes in circulation and pressure on nerves. Calf stretches before bed help. Evidence for magnesium in pregnancy is mixed, so check with your midwife, GP or pharmacist before taking supplements.
