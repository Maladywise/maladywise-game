Fact sheet

Pneumonia

An infection deep in the lungs, where the tiny air sacs fill with fluid and pus. Bacteria and viruses both cause it. Most healthy adults recover at home, but it can be serious for babies, older people and anyone with heart or lung disease. William Osler called it “the friend of the aged” for a reason.

What it feels like. A cough (dry or with thick phlegm), fever and shivers, breathing faster or getting puffed easily, and sharp chest pain when you breathe in. It often starts as a cold or flu that gets worse instead of better. Older people may have no fever at all: new confusion, a fall or going off their food can be the only clue. Babies and young children may just breathe fast, grunt, or have the skin between their ribs suck in with each breath.

What causes it. The most common bacterial cause is pneumococcus (Streptococcus pneumoniae). Viruses such as flu, RSV and COVID-19 cause about half of cases. “Atypical” germs such as mycoplasma cause milder “walking pneumonia”, and Legionella causes Legionnaires’ disease. Inhaling food or drink can cause aspiration pneumonia.

Diagnosis. Usually by your GP listening to your chest and checking your temperature, breathing rate, pulse and oxygen level. A chest X-ray, blood tests or a phlegm test are used when the picture isn’t clear or you’re quite unwell. In Australia, babies, people over 70 and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people are at higher risk, and babies, young children and people over 70 are the most likely to need hospital care.

The pneumonia ladder

Mild and bacterial: antibiotics at home with a check-up in 2 days. Struggling to breathe or confused: hospital. Recovery takes weeks, not days.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Cough mixturesNo good evidence they help pneumonia. Coughing is how your lungs clear the gunk.
  • Vitamin C and other immune boostersTrials are small and low quality. They don’t replace antibiotics or vaccines.
  • Leftover antibiotics from last timeOften the wrong drug, the wrong dose and too few tablets. See your GP instead.

What you can do

  • Pneumococcal vaccine: from 1 July 2026 it’s free for adults 65 and over, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adults from 25, and adults with certain medical conditions. Babies get it in their routine vaccines.
  • Flu vaccine every year, and stay up to date with COVID-19 vaccines.
  • RSV vaccine: free for adults 75 and over and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adults 60 and over.
  • Quit smoking and vaping. Smoking damages the lung’s self-cleaning system. Quit smoking help.
  • Keep asthma, COPD, heart disease and diabetes well controlled.
  • Wash hands, cough into your elbow, and stay home while you have a fever.

See your GP if

  • A cough comes with fever, fast breathing or chest pain when you breathe
  • A cold or flu is getting worse after a few days instead of better
  • You’re not clearly improving 2–3 days after starting antibiotics
  • A cough or breathlessness hasn’t settled after 6 weeks

Call 000 for severe trouble breathing, blue or grey lips, new confusion or drowsiness, or a baby or child who is working hard to breathe, grunting, or too breathless to feed or talk.

Common questions

Is pneumonia contagious?

The germs that cause it are. Flu, COVID-19, RSV and pneumococcus spread by coughs, sneezes and hands, but most people who catch them don't get pneumonia. Stay home while you have a fever and cover your cough.

What is walking pneumonia?

A casual name for mild pneumonia you can stay on your feet with, often caused by mycoplasma. It still deserves a GP visit: it may need a different antibiotic from the usual one.

Can you have pneumonia without a fever?

Yes. Older people, babies and people with weak immune systems may have no fever. In older people, new confusion, falls or just going off their food can be the first sign.

How long should I take off work?

Usually at least until the fever has gone and you can manage a day without being wiped out, often 1 to 2 weeks. Heavy physical jobs may need longer. Tiredness can linger for months.

Do I need a chest X-ray after pneumonia?

Not routinely. Your GP may suggest a repeat X-ray if symptoms haven't settled after about 6 weeks, or if there's a reason to check the lung underneath, such as heavy smoking.

Do I need antibiotics for pneumonia?

For pneumonia that's likely bacterial, yes, and they work well. About half of cases are thought to be viral, where antibiotics don't help, but doctors often can't tell at first, so they treat to be safe and review in 2 days.

Who can get the pneumococcal vaccine for free?

From 1 July 2026 it's free on the National Immunisation Program for adults 65 and over, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adults from 25, and adults with certain medical conditions, plus babies in the childhood schedule.
