Fact sheet

Type 2 Diabetes

The body stops responding well to insulin, so sugar builds up in the blood. It’s often silent for years but can damage the eyes, kidneys, nerves, feet and heart. More than 800 million adults worldwide have diabetes. Treated well, those risks fall, and some people put it into remission.

Symptoms

  • Often none at all, which is why testing matters.
  • Feeling thirsty, passing more urine and feeling tired.
  • Wounds that heal slowly and infections that keep coming back, such as thrush or skin infections.
  • Tingling or numb feet can be a sign that sugar has been high for a while.

Causes and risk factors

  • The body becomes resistant to insulin and the pancreas can’t keep up.
  • Family history and getting older.
  • Carrying extra weight, especially around the waist, and not moving much.
  • Prediabetes, diabetes in a past pregnancy, or polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).
  • Higher rates in some communities, including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander, Pacific Islander and South Asian people.

How it’s diagnosed

  • A blood test: HbA1c (your 3-month sugar average) of 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or more, a fasting glucose of 7.0 mmol/L or more, or a random glucose of 11.1 mmol/L or more.
  • Without symptoms, two abnormal results on different days are needed.
  • Borderline results may need a glucose tolerance test (a sugary drink, then blood tests over 2 hours).
  • Once diagnosed, you’ll also have checks of kidneys, eyes, feet, blood pressure and cholesterol.

The type 2 diabetes ladder

Lifestyle is the foundation at every step, not a step you graduate from. Medicines are added when sugar stays high or to protect the heart and kidneys. Needing more of them over time is common and isn’t a failure.

  1. Step 1 · Self-careLose weight, eat well, move moreRegular activity, fewer sugary drinks and refined carbs, and weight loss if you’re above a healthy weight. A structured, supervised low-calorie program works best.
    Works
    In the DiRECT trial, 46% were in remission at 1 year on a supervised soups-and-shakes program (vs 4% with usual care), and 86% of those who lost 15 kg or more
    Cost
    Free to a program fee; dietitian visits rebated with a GP care plan
    How long
    Ongoing. Remission lasts as long as the weight stays off
    Watch for
    Very low-calorie diets need medical supervision if you take diabetes or blood pressure medicines; medicines often need reducing or stopping when the diet starts.

    Remission isn’t a cure. It’s a lease you renew by keeping the weight off.

  2. Step 2 · PrescriptionMetforminThe usual first tablet, taken with meals.
    Works
    Lowers HbA1c by about 1%. In overweight people in the UKPDS trial, it cut deaths by about a third over 10 years
    Cost
    Under the $25 PBS co-payment, about $22–23 a script; $7.70 concession
    How long
    Usually long term
    Watch for
    Nausea and diarrhoea (start low, take with food, or try slow-release). Low vitamin B12 over years. Pause it on sick days when you can’t keep fluids down.

    Cheap, old and still first choice. The reliable used car of diabetes medicines.

  3. Step 3 · PrescriptionSGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin)Tablets that make the kidneys flush extra sugar out in the urine. Often chosen early for people with heart disease, heart failure or kidney disease.
    Works
    Modest sugar lowering. In big trials, about a fifth to a quarter fewer heart failure hospital stays or heart deaths, and kidney decline cut by about 30–40%
    Cost
    On the PBS for type 2 diabetes (criteria apply)
    How long
    Long term
    Watch for
    Genital thrush, urine infections, dehydration. Rarely ketoacidosis, even with normal sugar levels: pause on sick days and before surgery as advised.

    Weeing out sugar sounds like a cheat code. The fine print is good hygiene and a sick-day plan.

  4. Step 4 · PrescriptionGLP-1 medicines (semaglutide, dulaglutide and others)Mostly weekly injections that copy a gut hormone, lowering sugar and appetite.
    Works
    HbA1c down by roughly 1 to 2 points, usually with weight loss. Some also lower the chance of heart attack and stroke
    Cost
    Some are on the PBS for type 2 diabetes (criteria apply)
    How long
    Long term; benefits fade after stopping
    Watch for
    Nausea, vomiting, constipation, reflux, gallstones. Doses are stepped up slowly. More in our GLP-1 medicines guide.

    Not cheating. A hormone that turns down the food noise.

  5. Step 5 · PrescriptionInsulinInjections when other medicines aren’t enough, during illness or pregnancy, or when sugars are very high.
    Works
    Lowers blood sugar as far as needed; there’s no ceiling on the dose
    Cost
    $25 per script on the PBS ($7.70 concession); pen needles and supplies subsidised through the NDSS
    How long
    Often long term
    Watch for
    Hypos (low sugar): shaky, sweaty, confused. Carry fast sugar. Some weight gain.

    Type 2 diabetes often progresses. Needing insulin is biology, not a report card.

  6. Step 6 · SpecialistEndocrinologist and the diabetes teamDiabetes educator, dietitian, podiatrist and eye checks are part of routine care; an endocrinologist helps when targets are hard to reach.
    Works
    No trials of referral itself; team care helps get hard-to-control sugar to target and spots complications early
    Cost
    Medicare rebates for some allied health visits with a GP care plan; specialist fees vary
    How long
    As needed
    Watch for
    Bring your sugar readings, medicines and recent blood tests.

    It takes a village to raise a normal HbA1c.

Costs are rough Australian prices in November 2026 and vary by pharmacy and provider. PBS co-payment: $25, or $7.70 with a concession card. Results are typical figures from trials, not promises.

Save your money

Popular, heavily marketed or often asked about, but the evidence is weak or says no.

  • Cinnamon capsulesA Cochrane review found cinnamon didn’t lower HbA1c more than a dummy treatment. Lovely on porridge, useless as medicine.
  • Supplements or programs promising to “reverse” diabetesNo supplement has been shown to put diabetes into remission. Weight loss can; capsules can’t.
  • Cutting out all fruitWhole fruit in normal amounts fits a diabetes diet. Juice, soft drink and sweets are the bigger problem.

What you can do

  • Register with the NDSS: it’s free and gives subsidised supplies, education and support.
  • Keep up the yearly checks: HbA1c every 6–12 months, kidneys and cholesterol yearly, blood pressure every 6 months, eyes at least every 2 years, feet at least yearly.
  • Look at your feet every day for cuts, blisters or colour changes.
  • Have a written sick-day plan, especially if you take metformin, an SGLT2 inhibitor or insulin.
  • Know your cholesterol and blood pressure: treating them protects your heart as much as sugar control does.
  • If you smoke, quitting is one of the biggest wins for your heart and feet.

See your GP if

  • You have thirst, frequent urination, tiredness or repeated infections
  • Your sugar readings or HbA1c are creeping up
  • New tingling, numbness or pain in your feet, or a change in vision
  • Side effects from your medicines, or you’re having hypos

Get urgent help (call 000 (Australia) or your local emergency number. From a mobile, 112 works in most countries) if you’re very drowsy or confused, breathing fast, or vomiting and can’t keep fluids down, especially with high sugar readings. On an SGLT2 inhibitor, nausea, tummy pain and feeling very unwell can mean ketoacidosis even when sugar looks normal. Call 000 for a hypo that isn’t improving with fast sugar, or anyone who is unconscious.

See a doctor the same day for a foot wound that’s red, hot, swollen, smelly or turning dark.

Common questions

Can type 2 diabetes go away?

It can go into remission, meaning normal sugar levels without medicine, usually after significant weight loss. It can come back if weight returns, so checks continue.

Will I end up on insulin?

Not necessarily. Many people manage for years with lifestyle and tablets or weekly injections. If you do need insulin, it’s because the pancreas tires over time, not because you failed.

What does HbA1c mean?

It shows your average blood sugar over the past 2 to 3 months. Your GP will agree a personal target with you.

Is diabetes my fault?

No. Genes, age and family background play a big part. Lifestyle helps, but blame doesn’t.

What are the early signs of type 2 diabetes?

Often none. When symptoms appear they include thirst, passing more urine, tiredness, blurred vision, slow-healing sores and repeated infections such as thrush. A diabetes risk check is recommended from age 40, or from 18 for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people.

What foods should I avoid with type 2 diabetes?

Sugary drinks and juice, sweets and refined carbs such as white bread raise blood sugar quickly. You don’t need to cut out all carbs: wholegrains, legumes, vegetables and fruit in sensible portions are part of a healthy diabetes diet.

Is type 2 diabetes genetic?

Genes play a big part: having a parent or sibling with type 2 diabetes raises your risk a lot. Weight, activity, diet, age and background (it’s more common in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander, Pacific Islander and South Asian people) matter too.
