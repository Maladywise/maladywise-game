Tiredwise: the tiredness map

What actually causes tiredness in general practice, ranked from common to rare with the data behind each rank. Then the next layer: when the standard tests are normal, which features and mechanisms are worth chasing, and what the treatment trials have shown so far.

energy: low

Bottom line

  • Mood, sleep and life load dominate. Depression alone is found in about 18.5% of tired GP patients (pooled, 26 studies).
  • Blood tests find little. Only 8% of tired patients had a somatic illness detectable by bloods. A 17-test panel added almost nothing over 4 tests but more than doubled the false positives (38 → 96).
  • Serious disease is rare but real. Pooled serious somatic disease is about 4.3%; cancer about 0.6%. In a UK cohort of 285,382, 9-month cancer risk after a fatigue visit was 2.2% in men and 1.1% in women aged 30+, rising above 3% in older men with weight loss or abdominal pain.
  • Large unexplained group. In one Dutch study about 43% ended with no diagnosis beyond "tiredness". That's a lot of people with real symptoms and no answer yet.
  • For the unexplained group, the best-supported leads are features you can measure: post-exertional malaise, orthostatic intolerance, small fibre neuropathy, iron deficiency without anaemia, sleep disorders.
  • The immune/autoantibody story is promising but repeatedly fails in proper trials (rituximab, IVIG, efgartigimod, BC007). Open-label wins keep shrinking under placebo control. Subgrouping by biomarker looks like the way forward.

Treatment trials for the unexplained group

Green = positive randomised result · amber = mixed, small or uncontrolled · red = negative randomised trial.

The pattern: small open-label studies look good, then larger placebo-controlled trials don't. Placebo and volume effects are big here (in the IVIG trial, both arms improved). The next generation of trials targets biomarker-defined subgroups, such as people with high NK cells for daratumumab, or confirmed antibody positivity.
