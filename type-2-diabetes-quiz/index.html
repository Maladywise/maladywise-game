Type 2 Diabetes Quiz

Twelve questions, easy to hard, on what type 2 diabetes is, how it’s diagnosed and treated, the newer medicines, and the checks that prevent complications.

  1. Question 1 of 12

    What is the main problem in type 2 diabetes?

    1. The immune system destroys insulin cells
    2. Eating too much sugar on a single day
    3. The kidneys make too much glucose
    4. The body resists insulin and can’t make enough extra
    Show answer

    Answer: The body resists insulin and can’t make enough extra. Insulin resistance plus a slowly failing insulin supply. Type 1 is the autoimmune one.

  2. Question 2 of 12

    Which HbA1c level is used to diagnose diabetes?

    1. 10% or higher
    2. Any level over 6.0% once
    3. 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or higher
    4. 5.0% or higher
    Show answer

    Answer: 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or higher. In Australia, HbA1c 6.5% or more (or a high fasting or tolerance test) confirms diabetes.

  3. Question 3 of 12

    What is usually the first diabetes medicine?

    1. Metformin
    2. Gliclazide
    3. Prednisolone
    4. Insulin
    Show answer

    Answer: Metformin. Cheap, doesn’t cause hypos on its own, and has decades of safety data.

  4. Question 4 of 12

    What is a common HbA1c target for most adults with diabetes?

    1. Under 10%
    2. 7% (53 mmol/mol) or less
    3. Under 4%
    4. There is no target
    Show answer

    Answer: 7% (53 mmol/mol) or less. Targets are personalised: tighter for some younger people, looser for frail older people where hypos are the bigger risk.

  5. Question 5 of 12

    Which medicine class also protects the heart and kidneys?

    1. DPP-4 inhibitors, such as sitagliptin
    2. Sulfonylureas, such as gliclazide
    3. SGLT2 inhibitors, such as empagliflozin
    4. Alpha-glucosidase inhibitors, such as acarbose
    Show answer

    Answer: SGLT2 inhibitors, such as empagliflozin. They make you pass glucose in your urine, and cut heart failure and kidney decline even beyond their sugar-lowering effect.

  6. Question 6 of 12

    Why should SGLT2 inhibitors be paused when you’re sick or not eating?

    1. They can cause ketoacidosis, even with normal sugars
    2. They can drop sodium to dangerously low levels
    3. They raise blood pressure when you're dehydrated
    4. They stop working permanently after an illness
    Show answer

    Answer: They can cause ketoacidosis, even with normal sugars. ‘Euglycaemic DKA’ is easy to miss. Sick day rules: pause when vomiting, fasting or before surgery.

  7. Question 7 of 12

    Which medicines are most likely to cause a hypo?

    1. SGLT2 inhibitors on their own
    2. Insulin and sulfonylureas (e.g. gliclazide)
    3. Statins and blood pressure tablets
    4. Metformin on its own
    Show answer

    Answer: Insulin and sulfonylureas (e.g. gliclazide). Know the signs (shaky, sweaty, confused) and carry fast sugar such as jelly beans or juice.

  8. Question 8 of 12

    Long-term metformin can lower which vitamin?

    1. Vitamin K
    2. Vitamin C
    3. Vitamin A
    4. Vitamin B12
    Show answer

    Answer: Vitamin B12. Check B12 every year or two, especially with numb feet, which can be mistaken for diabetic nerve damage.

  9. Question 9 of 12

    Which urine test screens for diabetic kidney damage?

    1. Albumin–creatinine ratio (ACR)
    2. Urine glucose dipstick
    3. Urine culture
    4. Urine drug screen
    Show answer

    Answer: Albumin–creatinine ratio (ACR). Protein in the urine is an early sign. Check ACR and kidney blood tests at least yearly.

  10. Question 10 of 12

    Can type 2 diabetes go into remission?

    1. Only once insulin has been started
    2. Yes, often with weight loss of around 10 to 15 kg
    3. No, it always gets worse over time
    4. Only in children and teenagers
    Show answer

    Answer: Yes, often with weight loss of around 10 to 15 kg. In the DiRECT trial, almost half of people who lost weight with a structured diet were in remission at one year.

  11. Question 11 of 12

    How do GLP-1 medicines like semaglutide help?

    1. Make the kidneys leak sugar into the urine
    2. Block sugar from being absorbed in the gut
    3. Replace the body's own insulin completely
    4. Boost insulin after meals, slow the stomach, curb appetite
    Show answer

    Answer: Boost insulin after meals, slow the stomach, curb appetite. Weekly injections. Nausea is common at first, so doses start low and go up slowly.

  12. Question 12 of 12

    Gestational diabetes in pregnancy means what for later life?

    1. No future risk once the baby is born
    2. It always turns into type 1 diabetes
    3. A much higher risk of type 2 diabetes: test regularly
    4. Only the baby is at risk, not the mother
    Show answer

    Answer: A much higher risk of type 2 diabetes: test regularly. Check for diabetes after the birth, then every 1 to 3 years. A healthy weight and activity cut the risk.

Common questions

Is type 2 diabetes caused by eating sugar?

Not directly. Genes, weight, activity, age and ethnicity all matter. Sugary drinks do raise the risk.

What checks do people with diabetes need?

HbA1c, blood pressure, cholesterol, kidney tests, a foot check and an eye check every 1 to 2 years.

Is this quiz medical advice?

No. It’s general education. Talk to your GP or diabetes educator about your own plan.
