  1. #12 · Friday 13 November 2026 Hard

    1. Capillary refill time for dehydration in a child

      A toddler with gastro. The doctor presses a fingertip until it goes pale and counts how long the pink takes to come back. Slow is positive.

      Sensitivity 60% (range 29%–91%); specificity 85% (range 72%–98%).

      One of the most useful single signs, and it still misses about 4 in 10. Doctors combine several signs rather than trust one.

      Steiner 2004 JAMA review, for 5% dehydration or more. Few good studies, hence the wide ranges.

      Source: Steiner 2004 JAMA review, table reproduced in Evidencia (2008); Steiner 2004 review, summarised in Evidence-Based Medicine (BMJ)

    2. Faecal calprotectin (children and teens) for inflammatory bowel disease

      A teenager with tummy pain, loose poo and weight loss. Same poo test.

      Sensitivity 92% (range 84%–96%); specificity 76% (range 62%–86%).

      Just as good at catching it in kids, but more false alarms: about 1 in 4 without bowel disease test positive.

      Meta-analysis of 7 studies in children and teenagers (2010).

      Source: van Rheenen 2010 meta-analysis (BMJ)

    3. Troponin, one test on arrival for a heart attack

      Chest pain in the emergency department. One high-sensitivity troponin on arrival, usual cut-off.

      Sensitivity 90% (range 85%–94%); specificity 78% (range 72%–83%).

      One test too early misses about 1 in 10 heart attacks, because troponin takes time to rise. That’s why you get a second test.

      Roche high-sensitivity troponin T at the 99th percentile. Another brand (Abbott troponin I) was the other way round: 75% sensitive, 94% specific.

      Source: NICE DG40: high-sensitivity troponin tests, evidence

    4. Skin turgor for dehydration in a child

      Same toddler. The doctor gently pinches the skin on the tummy and watches how fast it flattens back. Slow is positive.

      Sensitivity 58% (range 40%–75%); specificity 76% (range 59%–93%).

      A pinch that’s right more often than wrong, but not by much.

      Steiner 2004 JAMA review, for 5% dehydration or more.

      Source: Steiner 2004 JAMA review, table reproduced in Evidencia (2008); Steiner 2004 review, summarised in Evidence-Based Medicine (BMJ)

    5. ANA blood test (titre 1:80 or above counts as positive) for Lupus (SLE)

      Joint pains, a facial rash and tiredness. Your GP orders an antibody screen.

      Sensitivity 98% (range 95%–100%); specificity 75% (range 67%–81%).

      Nearly everyone with lupus is positive, so a negative result makes lupus very unlikely. But a quarter of people without lupus are positive too, so a positive result is just the start of the story.

      Pooled from about 64 studies including over 13,000 people with lupus, compared with control groups.

      Source: Leuchten et al, Arthritis Care Res 2018: ANA for classifying SLE

  2. #11 · Thursday 12 November 2026 Medium

    1. Urea breath test for H. pylori (the stomach ulcer bug)

      Burning tummy. You drink a special drink, then blow into a bag.

      Sensitivity 94% (range 89%–97%); specificity 90% (range 89%–91%).

      The best non-invasive test for H. pylori. Stop acid tablets and antibiotics beforehand or you’ll fool it.

      The Cochrane review compared tests at a fixed specificity of about 90%, so that figure is set, not measured.

      Source: Cochrane review: non-invasive tests for H. pylori (2018)

    2. Neer’s test for shoulder impingement

      Shoulder pain when you reach overhead. The doctor steadies your shoulder blade and lifts your straight arm up in front of you. Pain is positive.

      Sensitivity 79% (range 79%–79%); specificity 53% (range 53%–53%).

      Good at catching it, bad at clearing it: about half of people without impingement say ouch anyway. Shoulders are complicated.

      Pooled figures, Hegedus 2008 meta-analysis.

      Source: Hegedus 2008 meta-analysis (Br J Sports Med abstract)

    3. Coeliac antibody blood test (tTG-IgA) for Coeliac disease in adults

      Bloating, loose bowels and low iron for a year. Your GP orders a coeliac blood test.

      Sensitivity 91% (range 87%–94%); specificity 87% (range 84%–90%).

      It catches about nine in ten cases and is mostly right when positive. In these hospital-referred patients, about one in eight without coeliac disease still came back positive.

      Pooled from 5 studies of adults referred to specialist (secondary) care, checked against gut biopsy.

      Source: Sheppard et al, Aliment Pharmacol Ther 2022: serological tests for coeliac disease (abstract)

    4. Hawkins–Kennedy test for shoulder impingement

      Same sore shoulder. The doctor lifts your arm to shoulder height, bends the elbow and swings the forearm down. Pain is positive.

      Sensitivity 79% (range 79%–79%); specificity 59% (range 59%–59%).

      Neer’s near-twin, with the same problem: lots of false alarms.

      Pooled figures, Hegedus 2008 meta-analysis.

      Source: Hegedus 2008 meta-analysis (Br J Sports Med abstract)

    5. PSA blood test (above 4 counts as high) for prostate cancer

      In a big US study, men had a prostate biopsy whatever their PSA was. A PSA above 4 counted as positive.

      Sensitivity 21% (range 19%–23%); specificity 94% (range 92%–95%).

      Ouch. At this cut-off, PSA missed about 4 in 5 of the cancers found on biopsy. No PSA level is both sensitive and specific.

      From the Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial placebo group (Thompson 2005). Lower cut-offs catch more cancers but cause more false alarms.

      Source: Thompson 2005 (JAMA), abstract via US National Cancer Institute EDRN

  3. #10 · Wednesday 11 November 2026 Medium

    1. D-dimer blood test for a clot in the leg (DVT)

      Swollen calf after a long flight. Your risk looks low, so the doctor orders a D-dimer to rule out a clot.

      Sensitivity 97% (range 95%–98%); specificity 40% (range 35%–54%).

      The classic rule-out test. Negative? A clot is very unlikely. Positive? Means little on its own: pregnancy, cancer, surgery, injury and inflammation all raise it.

      Older pooled data for lab (ELISA) assays; specificity was about 35 to 54% depending on the assay. Only useful when your risk is low to moderate.

      Source: Bounameaux 1997 review (QJM): pooled D-dimer accuracy by assay; StatPearls: D-dimer test

    2. Shifting dullness for fluid in the belly (ascites)

      A swollen tummy. The doctor taps it like a drum, then rolls you onto your side and taps again to see whether the dull sound moves.

      Sensitivity 77% (range 64%–90%); specificity 72% (range 63%–81%).

      Decent, not decisive. Ultrasound is the usual tiebreaker.

      Pooled figures from Williams and Simel 1992 (JAMA).

      Source: University of Washington Advanced Physical Diagnosis: ascites evidence; Veysey 2016, Detecting ascites (Med J Aust), from Williams and Simel 1992

    3. Jolt accentuation for meningitis

      Fever and headache. You shake your head side to side, two or three times a second. If the headache gets worse, that’s positive.

      Sensitivity 65% (range 37%–86%); specificity 70% (range 48%–86%).

      Once hyped as a near-perfect rule-out. A bigger review says it misses about 1 in 3. Head-shaking is not a substitute for a lumbar puncture.

      Cochrane 2020: 9 studies, 1,161 people, very low certainty (hence the wide ranges). In people who were fully alert it caught about 75 in 100 and cleared about 61.

      Source: Cochrane review: jolt accentuation for meningitis (2020)

    4. Rheumatoid factor (IgM) blood test for Rheumatoid arthritis

      Achy, puffy knuckles and wrists. Your GP checks the classic arthritis blood test.

      Sensitivity 69% (range 65%–73%); specificity 85% (range 82%–88%).

      It picks up about the same share of cases as anti-CCP, but it turns up positive more often in people without RA. The famous name outlasts its precision.

      Pooled from 50 studies.

      Source: Nishimura et al, Ann Intern Med 2007: anti-CCP and rheumatoid factor meta-analysis

    5. Fluid wave for fluid in the belly (ascites)

      Another swollen tummy. Someone presses a hand down the middle while the doctor flicks one side and feels for a ripple on the other.

      Sensitivity 62% (range 47%–77%); specificity 90% (range 84%–96%).

      Misses plenty, but when the ripple is there, it means business. Better for ruling in than out.

      Pooled figures from Williams and Simel 1992 (JAMA).

      Source: University of Washington Advanced Physical Diagnosis: ascites evidence; Veysey 2016, Detecting ascites (Med J Aust), from Williams and Simel 1992

  4. #9 · Tuesday 10 November 2026 Easy

    1. Screening mammogram for breast cancer

      A routine screening mammogram, no lumps, no symptoms. Just your two-yearly squish.

      Sensitivity 87% (range 85%–89%); specificity 89% (range 87%–91%).

      Catches most cancers, but because so few people being screened have cancer, most call-backs turn out fine. A call-back is not a diagnosis.

      US data, 2007 to 2013, 1.6 million screens. Programs and countries differ.

      Source: Breast Cancer Surveillance Consortium (US) screening benchmarks

    2. Anti-CCP antibody blood test for Rheumatoid arthritis

      Stiff, swollen finger joints every morning for months. Your GP orders an antibody test.

      Sensitivity 67% (range 62%–72%); specificity 95% (range 92%–98%).

      A positive result is a strong pointer to rheumatoid arthritis. A negative one doesn't rule it out, since about a third of people with RA test negative.

      Pooled from 37 studies.

      Source: Nishimura et al, Ann Intern Med 2007: anti-CCP and rheumatoid factor meta-analysis

    3. Canadian C-spine rule for a serious neck injury

      Rear-ended at the lights, neck sore. The doctor asks your age, how the crash happened and about any tingling, then checks whether you can turn your head. It decides who needs a scan.

      Sensitivity 99% (range 99%–100%); specificity 44% (range 42%–45%).

      Built to miss nothing, and it very nearly doesn’t. The price: more than half of people with no injury still get imaged.

      Michaleff 2012 review (CMAJ), the 4 prospective studies that used the full rule. Across all 9 studies it caught 90 to 100 and cleared anywhere from 1 to 77.

      Source: Michaleff 2012 systematic review (CMAJ)

    4. COVID rapid antigen test (RAT) for COVID

      Sore throat, runny nose, feeling rubbish. You swab yourself at home.

      Sensitivity 73% (range 69%–76%); specificity 99% (range 99%–100%).

      Great at false alarms (it barely makes any). Less great at catching COVID: about 1 in 4 people with symptoms test negative. A negative RAT with symptoms is worth repeating.

      Pooled across many brands and 109 evaluations in people with symptoms.

      Source: Cochrane review (Dinnes 2022), summarised in American Family Physician 2023

    5. Pronator drift for a brain lesion weakening one side

      Arms straight out in front, palms up, eyes closed. If one arm slowly sinks and turns palm-in, that’s positive.

      Sensitivity 92% (range 92%–92%); specificity 90% (range 90%–90%).

      A simple test that punches well above its weight: about 9 in 10 both ways.

      One study of 170 people with suspected mild one-sided brain lesions, checked against CT (Teitelbaum 2002).

      Source: Teitelbaum 2002 (Can J Neurol Sci)

  5. #8 · Monday 9 November 2026 Easy

    1. Lachman’s test for a torn ACL (a key knee ligament)

      You twisted your knee at netball and felt a pop. The doctor bends your knee slightly and tugs your shin forward to feel how far it slides.

      Sensitivity 85% (range 83%–87%); specificity 94% (range 92%–95%).

      One of the better bedside tests in all of medicine. It catches most torn ACLs and rarely cries wolf.

      Pooled figures from the Benjaminse 2006 meta-analysis.

      Source: Benjaminse 2006 meta-analysis (PubMed abstract)

    2. Bowel screening poo test (FIT) for bowel cancer

      The bowel screening kit arrives in the mail. You do the poo stick thing and post it back.

      Sensitivity 82% (range 73%–89%); specificity 94% (range 92%–95%).

      Catches about 4 in 5 bowel cancers from one simple sample, and most people without cancer get a clear result. One reason the kit comes back every two years.

      Figures are for cancer, from a 2014 meta-analysis. They vary with the brand and the cut-off used.

      Source: Lee 2014 meta-analysis (Ann Intern Med), summarised in American Family Physician

    3. HPV test (cervical screening) for cervical pre-cancer

      Routine cervical screening. The sample is tested for HPV, the virus behind almost all cervical cancer.

      Sensitivity 90% (range 89%–93%); specificity 90% (range 89%–91%).

      Much better at catching pre-cancer than the old Pap smear, which is why Australia switched to it in 2017.

      Cochrane 2017, for moderate pre-cancer (CIN2+) using one HPV test (HC2). Figures differ slightly between tables in the review.

      Source: Cochrane review: cytology versus HPV testing (2017)

    4. HINTS exam for a stroke causing vertigo

      Sudden nonstop spinning, vomiting, can’t walk straight. A doctor checks three things: a quick head turn, the pattern of eye flicker, and whether one eye sits higher.

      Sensitivity 94% (range 82%–98%); specificity 87% (range 75%–94%).

      A few minutes of eye-watching catches almost all of these strokes. The catch is it needs trained eyes.

      Cochrane 2023, bedside HINTS in people with acute vertigo. About half the exams were done by neurologists. Low certainty.

      Source: Cochrane review: HINTS for acute vestibular syndrome (2023), abstract

    5. Procalcitonin blood test for Sepsis (infection) rather than non-infectious inflammation in very unwell patients

      Feverish and very unwell in intensive care. The team wants to know if infection is to blame.

      Sensitivity 77% (range 72%–81%); specificity 79% (range 74%–84%).

      It gets it right about three-quarters of the time either way. Useful, but not good enough to settle the question on its own.

      Pooled from 30 studies, about 3,240 critically ill patients.

      Source: Wacker et al, Lancet Infect Dis 2013: procalcitonin for sepsis (abstract)

  6. #7 · Sunday 8 November 2026 Genius

    1. CRP blood test for pneumonia

      Chesty cough and fever. Could one CRP tell pneumonia from a plain chest infection?

      Sensitivity 54% (range 10%–98%); specificity 72% (range 44%–99%).

      Studies were all over the place: sensitivity anywhere from 10 to 98%. So almost any guess scores today. That’s not a compliment to CRP.

      A 2005 systematic review found study quality generally poor and said CRP alone can’t rule pneumonia in or out. The grid shows the middle of the range.

      Source: van der Meer 2005 systematic review (BMJ)

    2. Empty can test for a full tear of a shoulder tendon (rotator cuff)

      A weak, painful shoulder. Arms out in front, thumbs down as if emptying a can, you push up while the doctor pushes down. Weakness or pain is positive.

      Sensitivity 66% (range 32%–99%); specificity 66% (range 40%–91%).

      Studies can’t agree whether it’s brilliant or useless. Range scoring means almost anything wins today. Don’t get cocky.

      Hughes 2008 review: 32 to 99 caught, 40 to 91 cleared across studies. Single studies found 31/52 and 50/84. The grid shows the middle of the range.

      Source: Hughes 2008 systematic review, summarised by BestBETs

    3. Amylase blood test (more than 3 times the upper normal limit counts as high) for Acute pancreatitis

      Sudden belly pain and vomiting after a big weekend. The hospital checks an older pancreas blood test.

      Sensitivity 72% (range 59%–82%); specificity 93% (range 66%–99%).

      When it's sky-high it usually means pancreatitis, but it misses more than a quarter of cases. It's the older sibling that lipase tends to outshine.

      Cochrane review summary: 3 studies, 605 people with acute abdominal pain.

      Source: Cochrane 2017: serum amylase and lipase for diagnosis of acute pancreatitis

    4. Drop arm test for a full tear of a shoulder tendon (rotator cuff)

      The doctor lifts your arm out to the side, then asks you to lower it slowly. If it drops or you can’t control it, that’s positive.

      Sensitivity 20% (range 4%–35%); specificity 94% (range 88%–100%).

      Rarely positive, but when it is, take notice. It misses most tears.

      Hughes 2008 review: 4 to 35 caught, 88 to 100 cleared across studies. The grid shows the middle of the range.

      Source: Hughes 2008 systematic review, summarised by BestBETs

    5. Ultrasound for appendicitis (children) for appendicitis

      A child with pain low on the right of the tummy. An ultrasound first, no radiation.

      Sensitivity 88% (range 86%–90%); specificity 94% (range 92%–96%).

      Very good in skilled hands, and no radiation. It misses about 1 in 8, so a doubtful scan gets a rethink.

      Doria 2006 meta-analysis, children.

      Source: Doria 2006 meta-analysis (Radiology), summarised by ALiEM

  7. #6 · Saturday 7 November 2026 Hard

    1. Ferritin (under 15 counts as low) for iron deficiency

      Same blood test, stricter cut-off: only a ferritin under 15 counts as low.

      Sensitivity 65% (range 57%–75%); specificity 98% (range 96%–100%).

      Under 15, you can be pretty sure it’s iron deficiency. But this cut-off misses about a third of people who have it.

      Small, older studies with wide variation (one in pregnant women found much lower sensitivity).

      Source: Daru 2017 review: Serum ferritin as an indicator of iron status (Am J Clin Nutr)

    2. Raised neck vein (JVP) for heart failure

      Breathless in the emergency department. The doctor looks at a vein in your neck while you lie back at an angle, to see how high it fills.

      Sensitivity 39% (range 39%–39%); specificity 92% (range 92%–92%).

      If it’s up, heart failure gets much more likely. If it’s not, it tells you little: it missed 6 in 10.

      Twist: Imagine an emergency department where 4 in 10 breathless adults have heart failure.

      Wang 2005 JAMA review of breathless adults in emergency departments.

      Source: Wang 2005 JAMA review, teaching summary (University of Florida)

    3. Screening mammogram for breast cancer

      A routine screening mammogram, no lumps, no symptoms. Just your two-yearly squish.

      Sensitivity 87% (range 85%–89%); specificity 89% (range 87%–91%).

      Catches most cancers, but because so few people being screened have cancer, most call-backs turn out fine. A call-back is not a diagnosis.

      US data, 2007 to 2013, 1.6 million screens. Programs and countries differ.

      Source: Breast Cancer Surveillance Consortium (US) screening benchmarks

    4. Lipase blood test (more than 3 times the upper normal limit counts as high) for Acute pancreatitis

      Severe upper tummy pain going through to your back. The emergency team takes bloods.

      Sensitivity 79% (range 54%–92%); specificity 89% (range 46%–99%).

      It catches about four in five cases and is usually right when it's high. The wide ranges show the studies didn't entirely agree with each other.

      Cochrane review summary: 4 studies, 678 people with acute abdominal pain.

      Source: Cochrane 2017: serum amylase and lipase for diagnosis of acute pancreatitis

    5. Third heart sound (S3) for heart failure

      Same breathless patient. The doctor listens for an extra, low ‘gallop’ beat after the usual lub-dub.

      Sensitivity 13% (range 13%–13%); specificity 99% (range 99%–99%).

      Rare, but rarely wrong. It caught about 1 in 8, yet a real S3 all but shouts the diagnosis. Hearing one takes a good ear and a quiet room.

      Wang 2005 JAMA review of breathless adults in emergency departments.

      Source: Wang 2005 JAMA review, teaching summary (University of Florida)

  8. #5 · Friday 6 November 2026 Hard

    1. Rapid flu test (children) for the flu

      A feverish, miserable kid. Quick nose swab for influenza.

      Sensitivity 67% (range 62%–72%); specificity 98% (range 97%–99%).

      Better in kids than in adults, but it still misses about 1 in 3.

      Meta-analysis 2012. Specificity was reported for all ages combined.

      Source: Chartrand 2012 meta-analysis (Ann Intern Med), summarised by ACP

    2. NT-proBNP blood test (above 300 ng/L counts as high) for Heart failure as the cause of sudden breathlessness

      Breathless and can't lie flat tonight. The hospital checks a heart strain blood test.

      Sensitivity 99% (range 96%–100%); specificity 43% (range 26%–62%).

      It almost never misses heart failure, so a normal result is very reassuring. But it flags more than half of people who don't have it, so a high result is mostly an invitation to look further.

      Twist: The lowest-prevalence emergency group in the review, where most breathless patients had something other than heart failure.

      Pooled from 10 study groups, about 3,350 adults in acute care settings.

      Source: Roberts et al, BMJ 2015: natriuretic peptides in acute heart failure (open-access PDF)

    3. Brudzinski’s sign for meningitis

      Fever and a pounding headache. You lie flat and the doctor lifts your head towards your chest. If your hips and knees bend up by themselves, that’s positive.

      Sensitivity 5% (range 5%–5%); specificity 95% (range 95%–95%).

      Kernig’s famous sibling, and just as useless for ruling out: it caught 1 in 20 cases. A negative Brudzinski’s should reassure nobody.

      One study of 297 adults with suspected meningitis (Thomas 2002). Kernig’s sign scored exactly the same.

      Source: Thomas 2002 (Clin Infect Dis), summarised in American Family Physician

    4. CT scan for appendicitis (children) for appendicitis

      Same child, same pain, but this time a CT scan.

      Sensitivity 94% (range 92%–96%); specificity 95% (range 93%–97%).

      A bit better than ultrasound, at the cost of radiation. One reason kids usually get ultrasound first.

      Doria 2006 meta-analysis, children.

      Source: Doria 2006 meta-analysis (Radiology), summarised by ALiEM

    5. Anterior drawer test for a torn ACL

      An old knee injury that keeps giving way. You lie with your knee bent to a right angle and the doctor pulls your shin forward, like opening a drawer.

      Sensitivity 92% (range 88%–95%); specificity 91% (range 87%–94%).

      Excellent for an old tear like this one. The same review found it far less reliable straight after an injury, when pain and swelling get in the way.

      Benjaminse 2006 meta-analysis. These figures are for long-standing (chronic) tears only; it gave no good figures for fresh injuries.

      Source: Benjaminse 2006 meta-analysis (PubMed abstract)

  9. #4 · Thursday 5 November 2026 Medium

    1. Phalen’s test for carpal tunnel syndrome

      Numb, tingly fingers that wake you at night. You press the backs of your hands together, wrists bent, for a minute. Tingling in the fingers is positive.

      Sensitivity 68% (range 57%–69%); specificity 73% (range 67%–73%).

      Middling both ways. A third of people with carpal tunnel pass it, and plenty without it fail. Nerve conduction studies are the usual tiebreaker.

      A meta-analysis cited in J Fam Pract 2012 found 68 and 73; later meta-analyses landed nearby (57 to 69 caught, 67 to 71 cleared).

      Source: Wipperman and Potter 2012, Carpal tunnel syndrome: try these diagnostic maneuvers (J Fam Pract); LITFL: Phalen test (summary of reviews)

    2. Rapid flu test (adults) for the flu

      Fever, aches, the works. A quick nose swab for influenza at the clinic.

      Sensitivity 54% (range 48%–60%); specificity 98% (range 97%–99%).

      A coin toss for catching flu in adults, but a positive is very believable. Good for ruling in, bad for ruling out.

      Rapid antigen tests, meta-analysis 2012. Specificity was reported for all ages combined.

      Source: Chartrand 2012 meta-analysis (Ann Intern Med), summarised by ACP

    3. BNP blood test (above 100 ng/L counts as high) for Heart failure as the cause of sudden breathlessness

      Suddenly short of breath and puffy ankles. The emergency doctor orders a blood test.

      Sensitivity 95% (range 92%–98%); specificity 63% (range 52%–73%).

      A normal result makes heart failure quite unlikely. A high one is more of a shrug, because plenty of other things can push BNP up too.

      Pooled from 19 study groups, about 6,950 adults, mainly in emergency departments.

      Source: Roberts et al, BMJ 2015: natriuretic peptides in acute heart failure (open-access PDF)

    4. Tinel’s sign for carpal tunnel syndrome

      Same tingly fingers. The doctor taps over the front of your wrist. A zing shooting into the fingers is positive.

      Sensitivity 50% (range 50%–50%); specificity 77% (range 77%–77%).

      Tap, tap, coin toss. It catches half of cases. A JAMA review lumped Tinel’s and Phalen’s in with the least useful signs.

      Figures from the table in a 2012 J Fam Pract review. The JAMA Rational Clinical Examination review (D’Arcy and McGee 2000) rated both as having little or no diagnostic value.

      Source: Wipperman and Potter 2012, Carpal tunnel syndrome: try these diagnostic maneuvers (J Fam Pract); Epistemonikos: D’Arcy and McGee 2000 (JAMA) abstract

    5. Faecal calprotectin (adults) for inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn’s or colitis)

      Months of tummy pain and loose poo. IBS, or inflamed bowel? A poo test for calprotectin.

      Sensitivity 93% (range 85%–97%); specificity 96% (range 79%–99%).

      One of the good ones in adults. Negative? Inflamed bowel is unlikely, and you may skip a colonoscopy.

      Twist: In the BMJ review’s example, about 1 in 3 adults sent for this test had bowel disease.

      Meta-analysis of 6 adult studies (2010). Specificity varied a lot between studies.

      Source: van Rheenen 2010 meta-analysis (BMJ)

  10. #3 · Wednesday 4 November 2026 Medium

    1. Ferritin (under 30 counts as low) for iron deficiency

      Tired all the time. Your GP checks ferritin, your iron stores. Under 30 counts as positive.

      Sensitivity 92% (range 82%–100%); specificity 85% (range 75%–92%).

      A cut-off of 30 catches most iron deficiency. The catch: inflammation pushes ferritin up, so it can look normal when it isn’t.

      Small studies that varied a lot. Experts say better studies are urgently needed.

      Source: Daru 2017 review: Serum ferritin as an indicator of iron status (Am J Clin Nutr)

    2. Murphy’s sign for an inflamed gallbladder (cholecystitis)

      Pain under the right ribs after a fatty meal. The doctor presses there and asks you to breathe in. If you stop mid-breath in pain, that’s positive.

      Sensitivity 65% (range 58%–71%); specificity 87% (range 85%–89%).

      Better at ruling in than out: it misses about 1 in 3. Hence the ultrasound.

      Pooled figures from the Trowbridge 2003 JAMA review. Single studies varied a lot: 44 to 97 caught, 48 to 96 cleared.

      Source: Trowbridge 2003 JAMA review, teaching summary (University of Florida); EBM Consult: Murphy’s sign

    3. Urine dipstick: nitrite for a UTI

      Burning when you wee. The GP dips a strip in your urine. Positive here means the nitrite pad changes colour.

      Sensitivity 53% (range 44%–65%); specificity 88% (range 81%–96%).

      Nitrite positive is a decent clue. Nitrite negative? Means little: about half of UTIs don’t show it.

      Twist: Imagine a clinic where half the people with burning wees have a UTI.

      Pooled from 6 general practice studies (2004 meta-analysis).

      Source: Devillé 2004 meta-analysis: the urine dipstick test (BMC Urology)

    4. Straight leg raise for sciatica from a slipped disc

      Back pain shooting down one leg. You lie flat and the doctor slowly lifts your straight leg. If that brings on the pain down your leg, it’s positive.

      Sensitivity 92% (range 87%–95%); specificity 28% (range 18%–40%).

      Brilliant at catching it, hopeless at clearing it: lots of people without a slipped disc get leg pain too. A negative test is the useful one. Lifting the other leg flips it: 28 caught, 90 cleared.

      Cochrane 2010, mostly in people having surgery, so general practice may differ. Specificity ranged from 10 to 100 across studies.

      Source: Cochrane review: physical examination for lumbar radiculopathy (2010)

    5. Urea breath test for H. pylori (the stomach ulcer bug)

      Burning tummy. You drink a special drink, then blow into a bag.

      Sensitivity 94% (range 89%–97%); specificity 90% (range 89%–91%).

      The best non-invasive test for H. pylori. Stop acid tablets and antibiotics beforehand or you’ll fool it.

      The Cochrane review compared tests at a fixed specificity of about 90%, so that figure is set, not measured.

      Source: Cochrane review: non-invasive tests for H. pylori (2018)

  11. #2 · Tuesday 3 November 2026 Easy

    1. Urine dipstick: nitrite or white cells for a UTI

      Same strip, but now either pad (nitrite or white cells) changing colour counts as positive.

      Sensitivity 90% (range 89%–92%); specificity 65% (range 61%–69%).

      Count either pad and you catch most UTIs, but you also get lots of false alarms. Symptoms still matter most.

      General practice estimate rests on only 2 studies; treat as rough.

      Source: Devillé 2004 meta-analysis: the urine dipstick test (BMC Urology)

    2. Thompson’s (Simmonds’) test for a torn Achilles tendon

      A loud snap at the back of your ankle during touch footy. You kneel on a chair and the doctor squeezes your calf. If your foot doesn’t point down, that’s positive.

      Sensitivity 96% (range 96%–96%); specificity 93% (range 93%–93%).

      A ten-second squeeze that almost never gets it wrong. Rare for a bedside test.

      Reiman 2014 meta-analysis (J Athl Train), as reported by Physiotutors; FPnotebook gives the same figures.

      Source: Physiotutors: Thompson test; FPnotebook: Thompson’s test

    3. COVID RAT with no symptoms for COVID

      You feel perfectly fine. You swab before visiting your nan, just in case.
