Late Diagnosis Starter Kit: ADHD, Autism and AuDHD

Diagnosed with ADHD, autism or both (AuDHD) as an adult? Welcome. You’re not broken, lazy or “too much”: you’ve been running a different operating system without the manual. Here’s the manual, or at least the quick-start guide.

  • First feelings are all valid: relief, grief, anger at the lost years, imposter syndrome, all in the same afternoon.
  • Nothing has to change overnight. The diagnosis is information, not a sentence.
  • ADHD has effective medicines; autism doesn’t need “treating”, but the things that come with it (anxiety, sleep, burnout) do.
  • Your best tools are boring and brilliant: sleep, routines, external reminders, and fewer things that drain you.

Congratulations, you’ve unlocked the explanation for your entire personality. Please don’t rewrite your whole life by Tuesday.

The first 30 days

  • Read, but don’t binge. Pick 2–3 trusted sources (below). Social media is great for “oh, that’s me!” and terrible for medical facts.
  • Write your “brain owner’s manual”. What drains you, what recharges you, your sensory no-gos, your best and worst times of day.
  • Book a follow-up with your GP to plan treatment and support, and to check sleep, mood, iron, thyroid and anything else that’s been ignored.
  • Decide who to tell, if anyone. You don’t owe anyone your diagnosis. Telling a few safe people first is a good test run.

ADHD: medication, honestly

  • Stimulants (methylphenidate, dexamfetamine, lisdexamfetamine) help about 7 in 10 adults. They’re started low and adjusted over weeks.
  • Non-stimulants (atomoxetine, guanfacine) are options when stimulants don’t suit.
  • Expect some trial and error with dose, timing and brand. Track sleep, appetite, mood, heart rate and blood pressure.
  • Good medication feels like “finally, a quiet desk”, not a buzz. If you feel wired, flat or like a robot, the dose or medicine needs changing.
  • Stimulants are controlled (Schedule 8) medicines with state rules on who can prescribe. Carry your script details when travelling.
  • Driving: stable, well-treated ADHD usually makes driving safer. Check with your doctor if you’ve had problems.

Autism: support, not a cure

  • There’s no medicine for autism itself, and it doesn’t need one.
  • What helps: understanding your sensory and social energy budget, fewer masking marathons, routines that protect recovery time, and treating anxiety, depression or sleep problems if they’re there.
  • An occupational therapist can help with sensory and daily-living strategies; a neurodiversity-affirming psychologist can help with burnout, anxiety and self-understanding.

Your starter toolkit

  • External brain: one calendar, alarms for everything, visible to-do lists, “launch pad” by the door for keys, wallet and meds.
  • Body doubling: doing boring tasks alongside someone else (in person or online). Weirdly effective.
  • Sensory kit: noise-reducing earplugs, sunglasses, soft layers, a fidget, a “leave early” plan.
  • Energy accounting: socialising, masking and noise cost energy. Budget it like money and schedule recovery.
  • Make it easy: automate bills, subscribe-and-save essentials, simplify wardrobes and meals. Willpower is not a strategy.
  • Sleep first: regular wake time, screens down earlier, light in the morning. Everything is harder tired.

Work and study

  • Employers and education providers must make reasonable adjustments under disability discrimination law, if you choose to disclose.
  • Common adjustments: written instructions, quiet space or headphones, flexible hours, fewer open-plan days, clear deadlines, regular check-ins.
  • University disability services can give extensions, exam adjustments and note-taking support.

Watch for

  • Burnout: exhaustion, loss of skills and shutdowns after long periods of masking or overload. Rest and reducing demands help; it isn’t laziness.
  • Co-occurring conditions: anxiety, depression, eating problems, substance use, sleep disorders, hypermobility and POTS are more common. Mention them.
  • Thoughts of self-harm are more common in neurodivergent adults. Please reach out: Lifeline 13 11 14, 13YARN 13 92 76, or 000.

Save your money

  • Supplement stacks and “ADHD diets”Omega-3 has a tiny effect at best; elimination diets and supplement bundles mostly sell hope.
  • Neurofeedback and brain-training appsWell-blinded trials show little or no benefit over sham.
  • Quick online “diagnoses” with no proper assessmentA good assessment includes your history, a collateral history and co-occurring conditions. Cheap and fast can mean wrong.
  • Anything promising to “cure” autismChelation, bleach (MMS), stem cells and hyperbaric oxygen: no benefit, real harms.

Common questions

What should I do after an adult ADHD or autism diagnosis?

Give yourself time, read a few trusted sources, book a GP follow-up to plan treatment and support, and set up simple tools: one calendar, reminders, a sensory kit and protected recovery time.

What is AuDHD?

An informal term for being both autistic and ADHD. It’s common: many autistic people also meet criteria for ADHD.

Do I have to tell my employer?

No. Disclosure is your choice. If you do disclose, employers must consider reasonable adjustments under disability discrimination law.
